# Windows, views and sky access in rotating orbital habitats

Research tranche 9, section A2. Started and written 2026-11-01.

## 1. Scope

Windows and views in rotating habitats in low Earth orbit (wheel or torus, cylinder, sphere): the measured human value of windows and views (spaceflight, Antarctic and submarine analogs, daylight research on Earth, the "overview effect"), how published rotating-habitat designs admit light and views, what an occupant would see at real spin rates and in a 90-minute orbit, and what large windows cost in radiation, impact, ultraviolet, heat and pressure-pane mass. Spans of 1, 5 and 20 m at one atmosphere are worked by material. Physics and engineering only.

## 3. Findings

Conventions. Each Confidence cell starts with an evidence level: **DEMONSTRATED** (measured or flown), **ENGINEERING-STUDIED** (a design study, standard or analysis), or **SPECULATIVE**. #### A5. Measured effects versus claims (summary)

| Statement | Status |
|---|---|
| Crews choose to spend free time at windows and photograph Earth | Measured behavior (Robinson 2006; Stuster 2010) |

## 4. Conflicts

**K1. Cupola pressure-pane thickness.** Wikipedia (S18): "two 25 mm pressure panes." Royer Carfagni and Suffredini 2025 (P64): pressure panes "25.4 or 36.8 mm-thick," and 36.83 mm for the circular top window. Press (S29): "four panes ... each between 1.2 and 3.2 centimeters." A search digest (S21): "two 1.25-inch-thick" panes. Likely reason: the six trapezoidal side windows (from the Shuttle program) use 25.4 mm panes and the 80 cm top window 36.83 mm; the press range mixes debris, pressure and scratch panes. I use 36.83 mm for the top window because it is the only value tied to a stated window and it reproduces a NASA-like factor of safety (5.4).

**K2. Size of the particle that chipped the Cupola.** ESA (May 2016): a 7 mm chip from a particle "no bigger than a few thousandths of a millimetre across." Hyde et al. 2001: a 10 mm crater on a Shuttle debris pane is "best explained by a 0.76 mm ... paint chip" (crater about 13 times the particle). A few micrometers making a 7 mm chip would be a ratio over 1,000. Possible reasons: a wording slip (thousandths versus tenths), or a different damage mode (spall of a coating). I treat the ESA projectile size as unverified.

**K3. Does glass have a fatigue limit?** Moore 1980, citing Shand: annealed (soda-lime) glass has an endurance limit near 40-45% of its 5-second strength. Royer Carfagni 2025, citing Wiederhorn and Bolz 1970: "Silica has no static fatigue limit. Reduced durability and delayed fracture." The two refer to different glasses and different data sets; for fused silica under a permanent one-atmosphere load the conservative reading (no limit; design by flaw-growth life, as NASA-STD-5018 §4.5 requires) applies.

**K4. Are plastics good window materials?** NASA JSC (TFAWS 2024; partnering notice 2021): acrylic panes are lighter, structurally better and more damage-tolerant than glass; Orion uses them. NASA HIDH (2014): plastics are "very poor" optically "due to extreme wavefront errors" and scratch easily. Royer Carfagni 2025: at about 80 C an acrylic pane needs 153 mm to match 36.8 mm of fused silica, and an exposed acrylic pane reached 195 C in a model. The disagreements are about different properties (strength and toughness versus optics, scratch and heat) and different dates (2014 handbook versus 2021-2025 NASA practice).

**K5. Earth's albedo.** HIDH (2014) uses a visual albedo of 0.367 for its earthshine estimate (citing the NSSDC fact sheet of that time). The NSSDC Earth Fact Sheet now lists geometric albedo 0.434 and Bond albedo 0.294 (P30). Different definitions and fact-sheet revisions; the HIDH "50,000 lx or less" is an estimate either way.

**K6. Acrylic allowable stress.** Stachiw 1967: factor of at least 4 on short-term critical pressure for long-term loading (about 18 MPa if applied to the 72.4 MPa tensile strength). Gramann: aquarium design stresses of 10.3 MPa (10 years) and 4.5 MPa (20 years), with overall factors of 11-14. The first is a preliminary sizing rule from short-term tests; the second reflects creep-rupture data and decades of service. For a permanent habitat window the 20-year figure is the relevant one, and section 5.10 shows both.

**K7. Vision and rotation sickness.** Kennedy et al. 1965: blindfolded subjects in a rotating room had fewer symptoms than those who could see. Lonner et al. 2023: an Earth-fixed (motion-congruent) visual scene cut moderate-nausea dropouts from 67% to 21%. The visual scene differed: the 1965 subjects saw the rotating room's interior, which disagrees with the true motion; the 2023 subjects saw a scene consistent with the true motion. Neither tested a view out of a steadily rotating room. The theory cuts both ways: NASA SP-483 (1985) defines visual-vestibular conflict as "motion ... seen in the absence of vestibular corroboration." If the inner ear does not report a steady spin (the semicircular canals respond to angular acceleration; I did not open a primary giving their behavior under hours of constant rotation, see section 8), a window that shows the sky turning would be motion seen without vestibular corroboration, a possible provocation; Hall (1994), citing the same pages, argues the true outside view should help orientation. Which effect dominates is unmeasured.

**5.2 Earth and Sun through a floor window in LEO.** Earth's angular radius from altitude h: rho = asin(Re/(Re+h)). Fraction of the whole sky: (1 - cos rho)/2.
- 400 km: rho = 70.2 deg; Earth fills 33.1% of the sky; period 92.56 min; 15.56 orbits/day; maximum eclipse (beta = 0, cylindrical shadow) 36.1 min (matches sibling 5.2).
- 600 km: rho = 66.1 deg; 29.7% of the sky; period 96.69 min; 14.89 orbits/day; eclipse 35.5 min.
- Case A, spin axis normal to the orbit plane (nadir always in the spin plane): a floor window's line of sight sweeps a great circle through nadir, so Earth fills it for 2 rho / 360 = 39% of each spin at 400 km (37% at 600 km): 23.4 s of Earth and 36.6 s of space every minute at 1 rpm; 11.7 s and 18.3 s at 2 rpm; 5.9 s and 9.1 s at 4 rpm. The orbital motion adds only 0.065 deg/s, about 1% of the 6 deg/s spin at 1 rpm. The Sun crosses a floor window only when the beta angle is within about half the window's field of view of zero.
- Case B, spin axis pointed at the Sun (O'Neill, SP-413, Kalpana One): floor windows never see the Sun; Earth's disk enters their sweep whenever the angle between nadir and the axis is between 90 - rho and 90 + rho, which at beta = 0 is 4 rho/360 = 78% of each orbit at 400 km (73% at 600 km). Half of every orbit the Earth below is the night side.
- Basis: spherical Earth, circular orbit, no atmosphere refraction, the window looks straight "down" (radially outward).

**5.3 Far side.** Angle subtended by a window of width w at distance d: 2 atan(w/2d). SP-413 windows at 65 m: 1 m 0.88 deg, 5 m 4.41 deg, 20 m 17.5 deg. Kalpana One far side at 500 m: 0.11, 0.57, 2.29 deg. O'Neill Model 4 far side at 6,400 m: 0.01, 0.04, 0.18 deg. For scale, the Sun's disk is 0.533 deg (NASA Sun Fact Sheet), so a 5 m window on Kalpana One's far side looks about the size of the Sun.

**5.4 Plate stress, checked against flown and published windows.** Kirchhoff plate theory, MIT 2.080 Lecture 7 (P53), verified symbolically (plate_check.py: both deflection forms satisfy D grad^4 w = p; simply supported Mr(R) = 0):
- Circular plate radius R, thickness t, pressure p, simply supported: center moment (3 + nu) p R^2/16, so sigma_max = 3(3 + nu) p R^2 / (8 t^2).
- Circular plate, clamped edge: edge moment p R^2/8, so sigma_max = 3 p R^2 / (4 t^2) (MIT eq. 7.34).
- Long strip of width b (one-way bending, beam theory): simply supported sigma = 3 p b^2/(4 t^2); clamped sigma = p b^2/(2 t^2).
- Cupola top window (R = 0.40 m, t = 36.83 mm, nu = 0.16, p = 101,325 Pa): 14.16 MPa simply supported, 8.96 MPa clamped. Abraded MOR 52.4 MPa / 14.16 = 3.70; / 8.96 = 5.85. Consistent with NASA's 3.0.
- SP-413 glass (b = 0.5 m, t = 0.028 m, p = 50 kPa): 11.96 MPa simply supported, 7.97 MPa clamped. Same working-stress level as the Cupola.
- Validity: Moore 1980 found linear theory accurate for load intensity factor LIF = p b^4/(D t) below about 50 (deflection) to 100 (stress), with D = E t^3/(12(1 - nu^2)). Cupola: LIF = 3.6. Every glass case in 5.10 has LIF 3-65 (linear theory valid); room-temperature acrylic at 18 MPa has LIF 111 (slightly nonlinear; linear theory then overstates stress, so its thickness is conservative).

**5.5 Areal density.** Cupola top-window stack: (0.00945 + 2 x 0.03683) m x 2,201 kg/m3 = 183 kg/m2 (18.3 g/cm2). Thickness to equal 4.5 t/m2: fused silica 4,500/2,201 = 2.04 m; acrylic 4,500/1,190 = 3.78 m; water 4.50 m; ALON 4,500/3,800 = 1.18 m. For 10 t/m2: 4.54, 8.40, 10.0, 2.63 m. The flown stack is 1/25 to 1/55 of a settlement shield.

**5.6 Interior air as a shield (aircolumn.py).** An occupant on the floor at radius R receives rays from elevation theta along a chord of length 2 R sin(theta) (plane normal to the axis). Air density follows O'Neill's profile rho0 exp(-a (R^2 - r^2)), a = 1.2 x 10^-4 / (2R) per m, rho0 = 1.217 kg/m3 (1 atm). Integrated air mass:

Basis: two-dimensional chords; rays along the axis direction are ignored; the ray must also pass the far-side hull (window or land), which is not counted. Conclusion: O'Neill's statement that the atmosphere protects the inhabitants holds only for steep rays in the largest (6.4 km) cylinder; below about 1 km radius the windows themselves must carry or bypass (chevrons, mirrors) the shielding.

**5.7 Large-particle hits on window areas.** N = F A per year with F = 1.0 x 10^-5 /m2/yr for particles of 0.9 cm or more at 400 km (sibling, Christiansen 2003). One hit per 100,000 yr on 1 m2; per 5,093 yr on a 5 m circle; per 318 yr on a 20 m circle (97% chance of none in 10 years); per 10 yr on 10,000 m2; about 1 per year on 100,000 m2; about 7 per year on an unshielded window area equal to one third of the SP-413 torus surface (7 x 10^5 m2), if such an area were placed at 400 km (SP-413 itself was sited at L5 and covered its windows with the chevron shield). Small pits are constant: the Shuttle averaged 32.2 window impacts and 1.77 window replacements per mission.

**5.8 Sunlight through a window.** P = 1,361 W/m2 x 0.823 x A: 1 m circle 0.9 kW; 5 m 22.0 kW; 20 m 352 kW. SP-413's residential 200 W/m2 is 15% of full sunlight and agricultural 1,000 W/m2 is 73%.

**5.9 Aquarium cross-check (acrylic).** Treat each panel as a one-way strip spanning its height, simply supported, under a triangular hydrostatic load with the water surface at the panel top (a lower bound; actual depths not found): p0 = 1,000 x 9.80665 x H; M_max = p0 H^2 / (9 sqrt 3); sigma = 6 M / t^2.
- Dubai (H = 8.3 m, t = 0.75 m): p0 = 81.4 kPa (0.80 atm); sigma = 3.84 MPa (556 psi), close to the 650 psi 20-year acrylic design stress. Under a uniform 1 atm the same panel would see 9.31 MPa (1,350 psi), near the 10-year figure.
- Churaumi (H = 8.2 m, t = 0.60 m): 5.78 MPa (838 psi); under 1 atm 14.2 MPa (2,059 psi).
- Reading: the largest acrylic panels on Earth carry up to about 0.8 atm over about 8 m at 0.6-0.75 m thickness (the Dubai panel weighs 245.6 t). A uniform 1 atm on the same 8.3 m one-way span at the 20-year stress (4.48 MPa) needs t = 8.3 x sqrt(0.75 x 101,325 / 4.48 x 10^6) = 1.08 m; an 8 m circular acrylic window at that stress needs 8 x 0.084 = 0.67 m (5.10).

**5.10 Thickness and mass of one pane at 1 atm by span and material.** t = L sqrt(k p / sigma_d), where k = 3(3 + nu)/32 for a simply supported circle of diameter L, 3/4 for a simply supported long strip of width L. The ratio t/L is constant for a given material, so thickness and mass per area grow in proportion to span.

Circular panes, simply supported. A long strip of the same width needs sqrt(0.75 / (3(3 + nu)/32)) = 1.55-1.59 times the thickness (for example fused silica at 17.5 MPa: 66, 330, 1,319 mm); a clamped circle needs sqrt((3/16) / (3(3 + nu)/32)) = 0.77-0.80 times.

- Size effect: breaking strength falls as area^(-1/5) to area^(-1/7) (Moore). Relative to the Cupola top window (0.503 m2), a 5 m circle keeps 0.48-0.59 of the strength and a 20 m circle 0.28-0.40, so the large-span glass thicknesses above are underestimates by a factor of 1.3-1.9 (thickness goes as the inverse square root of the allowable).
- Many small panes versus one large pane: for the same material and design stress, mass per area is proportional to span (rho x L x t/L). A 20 m opening glazed as 400 panes of 1 m needs 41 mm of fused silica per pane (91 kg/m2 per pane layer) instead of 829 mm (1,825 kg/m2): 20 times less glass, before adding the frame. The frame then carries the load. In a pressurized hull the efficient frame is the hull's own rib or cable net in membrane tension (O'Neill's 84%-transparent steel mesh; SP-413's ribs 0.5 m apart), not beams in bending, so the frame mass is the hull mass the opening replaced. Small panes also limit the damage of one failure, keep each pane within the sizes that can be made and inspected, and let a pane be swapped like the Cupola's.
- Curved transparent hull instead of flat panes: a transparent cylindrical shell carries pressure by hoop tension, t = p R / sigma. With 17.5 MPa: hull radius 10 m 0.06 m; 65 m 0.38 m; 250 m 1.45 m; 830 m 4.81 m; 3,200 m 18.6 m (tempered glass at 57.5 MPa: 0.02, 0.11, 0.44, 1.46, 5.64 m). A transparent hull is reasonable only at small radius; at settlement radii the load must go into ribs or cables with small panes between them.
- Floor windows: the floor load is small next to the pressure. SP-413's internal mass (530,000 t on 678,000 m2) is 7.66 kPa, 15% of its 50 kPa atmosphere; a floor window is governed by the pressure, and by the extra requirement that a person standing on it must not be able to break the outer pressure boundary (the scratch-pane and redundant-pane logic of NASA-STD-5018).

**5.11 Dose with part of the sky behind a window (illustrative).** Mean dose rate = f x D_thin + (1 - f) x D_thick, with D_thin = 0.2-0.5 mSv/day (ISS, behind thin walls, sibling A2) and D_thick = 5 mSv/yr (SP-413 target). Basis: dose split by solid angle, which ignores Earth's own shadowing (Earth covers 33% of the sky at 400 km, 5.2), angular anisotropy of the trapped-proton flux and secondaries from the frame. ## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

- **People seek out windows when they have them.** In orbit, crews spent free time at windows and took 84.5% of Earth photographs on their own initiative; Earth viewing was 40% of leisure entries in astronaut journals (A1). Plan viewing places near rest, exercise and private areas, as NASA's handbook advises (A1).
- **The benefit is real but modest, and light is a separate benefit.** On Earth, views measurably improve recovery, mood and working memory; windowless rooms reduce light exposure and sleep (A3). A sealed volume is tolerable for months if lighting is controlled (submarines, A2), so a design can separate the daylight job (lamps or mirrors) from the view job (windows).
- **The orbital day is not a usable day.** In LEO, light outside cycles every 92.6 min with up to 36 min of darkness and about 16 sunrises a day (C1). Any window that admits direct sunlight into living or sleeping space needs fast shutters (NASA: closable to 2 lux at 0.5 m; operable within 10 s), and the residents' 24-hour clock must come from interior light (A1, D2).
- **Through a floor window the view moves.** Stars sweep by at the spin rate: 6 deg/s at 1 rpm, 12 deg/s at 2 rpm (a 60 deg view replaced every 10 s or 5 s). With the spin axis normal to the orbit, Earth fills a downward view 39% of every turn at 400 km; with a Sun-pointed axis, floor windows never see the Sun, and Earth crosses their sweep during about 78% of each orbit (5.1, 5.2).
- **Sunlight in a spinning room flickers or orbits** unless mirrors or the axis keep it steady (Hall, C1). Direct sunlight in LEO is about 132,000 lux, and a 20 m sunlit window admits about 350 kW of solar power (C1, 5.8).
- **Nobody has tested a window in a rotating room.** The rotation-tolerance data were taken in a windowless room; theory and experiments point both ways on whether a true outside view helps or hurts (C3, K7). Introducing a rotating-sky view should be treated as a new exposure, with graduated exposure being the only measured mitigation for visual motion sickness (Hu 1991, C3).
- **Windows are thin spots in the shield.** A flown window stack is about 18 g/cm2, 25-55 times lighter per area than settlement shielding (4.5-10 t/m2); matching that with transparent material takes 2-4.5 m of glass or 3.8-8.4 m of acrylic (5.5). Air inside the habitat helps only in the largest (multi-km) cylinders (5.6). Dose rises with the fraction of the sky seen through windows (5.11).
- **Impacts are certain on large areas.** Small pits arrived at about 32 per Shuttle mission on windows; centimeter-class particles hit about once per decade per 10,000 m2 at 400 km (D2, 5.7). Every pane needs an outer sacrificial layer, a shutter, and replaceability from inside or outside.
- **Unfiltered sunlight above the atmosphere includes UV that fused silica passes** (D3). Windows need UV filtering matched to NASA's exposure limits, which may still allow some UVB for vitamin D (HIDH).
- **Pressure panes grow with span.** At one atmosphere a glass pane needs a thickness of about 4% of its span (2.3% for tempered glass, 1.5% for ALON, 8.4% for acrylic at its 20-year stress), and NASA requires two such panes. A 20 m single pane would be 0.3 m (ALON) to 1.7 m (acrylic at its 20-year stress) thick and weigh 1.0-2.4 t per square meter per pane; 1 m panes need 15-84 mm (5.10).

## 7. Candidate handwaves

- **Large single panes (5-20 m) at one atmosphere.** Physics allows them only at 0.2-1.7 m thickness per pane, doubled for redundancy, with glass strength falling a further 41-72% for the size effect (5.10). Treating a 20 m pane as thin is a handwave. The physical design is many small panes in a tension frame (O'Neill's 84%-transparent cable mesh; SP-413's 0.5 m rib spacing).
- **Windows "in the floor" with no shielding.** Over living space, an unshielded floor window raises dose roughly in proportion to its solid-angle share (5.11); designs that avoid this route light by mirrors around a shield (SP-413 chevrons) or put windows only where people stay briefly (observation decks, Hall). Claiming full-size shielded transparency (2-10 m of transparent material) without accounting for its mass, absorption and color is a handwave.
- **Interior air as the window shield.** Valid only for steep rays in a 6.4 km diameter cylinder (5.6); not for 0.5-2 km habitats.
- **Seeing the sky "above" on the far side as a bright sky.** Through far-side windows the occupant sees black space, stars, Earth and Sun glints, not a blue sky; O'Neill's blue-sky remark refers to air depth equivalent to 3,300 m on Earth (sibling D2), and whether 6.4 km of air at that density scatters enough light to look blue was not researched (section 8).
- **Turning sky with no adaptation cost.** No human data exist on viewing a rotating star field from a rotating habitat (C3).
- **Virtual windows as equivalent to real ones.** NASA says they have not been tested in long-duration analogs (A1).
