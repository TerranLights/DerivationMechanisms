# Gas-giant floating cities, bulk mass and deuterium: what a popular-science video adds, and where it meets earlier findings

**Source.** Isaac Arthur, *Science & Futurism*, "Floating Cities Above Gas Giants" (YouTube id n68esPT4Gco). The author holds the subtitle file in the Inner Tepenia GDD's `Reference/Materials/Videos/Isaac Arthur/space colonization/`. This note was written from that transcript, read in full on 2026-11-12. It is a **popular-science source**: the host gives numbers without citations. Quotes below are short and exact; everything else is my paraphrase.

**Labels.** *VIDEO* = what the host says. *DERIVED* = my arithmetic, with its inputs shown. *GENERAL* = my background knowledge, not checked in this session and possibly stale; treat as a lead. *SOURCED (earlier file)* = a figure an earlier research file already read from its source.

## 1. What the video says

1. **The reframing.** The host says we would go to the gas giants "because we're running out of stuff", not because we run out of energy. The cities are "mass cities": refineries, ports and logistics hubs, not fusion depots. *VIDEO.*
2. **Deuterium is not the reason.** *VIDEO:* a civilization run entirely on deuterium fusion would need about 2,000 to 3,000 tons of deuterium a year. Earth's oceans hold more than 46 trillion tons, enough for billions of years, "long enough for the Sun itself to swell into a red giant". The host also says deuterium is plentiful in the ices of the outer moons, dwarf planets and comets, where there is no deep gravity well to fight.
3. **Deuterium economics.** *VIDEO:* deuterium sells for a few million dollars a ton. Supplying all of Earth's fusion needs would be a market of a few billion dollars a year, enough to support an outpost of about ten thousand people, "not a particularly impressive empire". Fusion isotopes in a gas giant's air are parts per ten thousand and slow to filter out.
4. **What the gas giants hold.** *VIDEO:* about 99% of the hydrogen outside the Sun is in the gas giants, mainly Jupiter and Saturn. Hydrogen serves as:
   - reaction mass for thermal rockets and beamed-power propulsion;
   - radiation shielding ("Your propellant tanks become your radiation shield");
   - feedstock for water, ammonia, methane and hydrocarbons;
   - bulk filler for large structures.

   The inner solar system has oxygen in its rock but little hydrogen. Hydrogen is only 11% of water's mass, so shipping hydrogen in to combine with local oxygen may be the cheap way to make water for domes and habitats. Helium is a coolant and pressurizing gas. Nitrogen is scarce in the inner system, and "No nitrogen, no plants".
5. **Propellant, not fuel, is the big demand.** *VIDEO:* a fusion economy needs thousands of tons of fuel a year, but moving millions of people and their habitats around the solar system needs millions to billions of tons of propellant a year, because propellant is spent at every burn. "Energy is cheap compared to momentum."
6. **How the cities float.** *VIDEO:*
   - Hot hydrogen in large envelopes floats in the denser hydrogen-helium air, at altitudes near one atmosphere of pressure and survivable temperatures.
   - Industrial waste heat keeps the lifting gas warm.
   - Altitude is controlled slowly: heat or cool the gas, compress or vent it, shift ballast.
   - Sideways travel rides the jet streams.
   - Big is easier: lift grows with volume while weight, heat loss and drag grow with surface area.
   - Graphene might serve as a light hull that holds hydrogen in.
7. **Power without fusion.** *VIDEO:* energy beamed out from large solar collectors near the Sun or Mercury, fission for reliability (its waste heat helps lift), and wind turbines and temperature differences between air layers as backup. "Instead of hauling fuel inward, you export energy outward."
8. **The people.** *VIDEO:* first rotating work crews, like offshore rigs or Antarctic stations. Then families, schools and clinics, because permanent residents cut training costs and keep institutional memory. A generation born there will find it "just home". "These cities don't begin as dreams. They begin as supply chains."
9. **Shipping out.** *VIDEO:* orbital rings, mass drivers and skyhooks, each possibly the center of its own settlement above the cloud cities.

## 4. What the video leaves open (for later research)

1. **The cost of getting mass out of the gas giants.** The video says the wells are "deep enough to hold" the gas but "shallow enough" to skim once in orbit, and it names orbital rings, mass drivers and skyhooks. It gives no velocity figures. Leaving Jupiter's cloud tops takes tens of km/s (GENERAL), far more than the 9 km/s the repo already notes for leaving Venus's clouds. Whether bulk hydrogen is exportable at a price anyone would pay is not established.
2. **Delivered cost of inner-system hydrogen against local sources.** The repo's `oxygen_and_hydrogen_supply_for_mars_water.md` already compares hydrogen from the solar wind, giant planets, comets, Ceres, polar ices and Mars's own water. The video's "ship hydrogen in, use local oxygen" is one of those routes. This note does not re-run the comparison.
3. **Buoyant-city engineering.** Hot-hydrogen lift at one atmosphere, hull materials that hold hydrogen in, storm loads on a structure tens of km across, safety of hydrogen-filled envelopes with industrial heat. All *VIDEO* only.
4. **The host's cost and revenue numbers** (deuterium "a few million dollars" a ton; revenue "a few billion dollars" a year; a ten-thousand-person outpost) are uncited. The earlier F2 file's heavy-water book value of about $300 per kg of D₂O gives about $1.5 million per tonne of deuterium, the same order.

## 5. Proposal: the Tepenian maritime fleet as radio relays above the waterline and deuterium extraction below it, from the water and from the seabed Earth (author, 2026-11-12) — PROPOSED

**What canon already has (read from the GDD, not changed).** The Official Population Census lists a non-city **maritime fleet**: 49,991 in Census I, 17,938 in Census II. Its definition is "fishing vessels and radio-relay points on the waters around Antarctica (the Drake Passage excluded), crews on rotating tours; crews ashore stay with friends and family in their home cities as guests and remain counted here" (rows set 2026-10-28, `DR-68`). So floating radio-relay points **are already part of the fleet**. Deuterium extraction would be a third function of the same platforms.

**Why it fits.**
- The video's own logic applies at sea: "people always live near infrastructure". A fleet already on rotating tours at radio relays already has a crew, power and a supply line.
- The deuterium Tepenia needs is small (§2: about 55 t of D₂O a year for the whole national D-T figure). That is one ship's cargo, so very few plants would do.
- Heavy water is compact and stable, so it moves easily by sea. Tepenian ports are few (`Ports.md`; Janbogo's is the year-round one), and a product that needs little port-side handling suits a fleet that rarely sees one.
- The Southern Ocean is cold. A Girdler-sulfide plant (GENERAL) needs a cold tower and a hot tower; the ocean supplies the cold side for free. The hot side needs heat: reactor or process waste heat, geothermal or the relay platform's own power. Not researched here.

**Cautions (DERIVED or GENERAL; unresearched).**
- **Water or seabed Earth?** The author's correction (2026-11-12): the extraction is "not just seawater, but from the underwater Earth": the seafloor and the crust below it. What follows is my reading of the physics (GENERAL, unresearched); what each source holds:
  - **Seawater** has D/H of about 156 ppm (§2) and is the richest common deuterium reservoir per hydrogen atom. It needs a pump and a plant, no digging.
  - **Pore water in seafloor sediment, and hydrothermal vent fluid,** are mostly seawater that has soaked in, with about the same D/H. A vent fluid supplies heat at the same time.
  - **Hydrated minerals** (clays, serpentine in the oceanic crust) and **gas hydrates** hold more hydrogen per cubic meter than seawater does, but their D/H is generally **lower** than seawater's (GENERAL). Seabed hydrogen is not richer in deuterium per atom; mining it for deuterium would move far more rock for less.
  - **Mantle water,** deeper still, also has lower D/H than the ocean (GENERAL) and is out of reach.

  So the seabed is a poorer deuterium source than the water above it. It matters for three other reasons, each of which the earlier files make relevant:
  1. **Lithium.** The earlier F2 file found that the fusion bottleneck is Li-6 for breeding tritium, and lithium in seawater is only about 0.1 to 0.2 mg/L. Seafloor brines and deposits (GENERAL) may hold more. A platform that already handles seawater and seabed intake could handle lithium too.
  2. **Uranium and the heavy-water link.** The GDD's uranium discussion treats uranium as an input; seawater and seafloor phosphate deposits hold some (GENERAL). Heavy water is also the moderator of CANDU-type reactors.
  3. **Heat.** Girdler-sulfide needs a hot tower. Seafloor hydrothermal vents, and geothermal heat reaching the shore (Janbogo's loop is canon), could supply it (GENERAL). Vents exist in the Southern Ocean (GENERAL).

  A speculative lead (GENERAL, very uncertain): ocean-ridge vent fluids carry helium with extra helium-3 from the mantle. The concentrations are tiny, and no resource has been proposed.

  The word "mining" fits the seabed part only. For the water, it is pumping, filtering and chemistry; "below the water" means submerged intake lines and process modules.
- **The process is heavy.** Girdler-sulfide uses large volumes of toxic, flammable hydrogen sulfide and high-temperature water. The earlier F2 file found **no** sourced energy-per-kilogram figure for it. Electrolysis-based and distillation routes exist (GENERAL) and may suit a floating platform better. Choosing one needs its own research.
- **A floating platform in the Southern Ocean faces sea ice, icebergs and the Furious Fifties storms** (GENERAL). The fleet's relay points already face these (canon), so the platform design must already answer them; adding a plant adds weight, hazard and heat on top.
- **Headcount.** The fleet's census number is fixed (arithmetic from the Santa Luce redistribution, not a migration). A few plants need few people, so adopting this would not need a new category. It could change what some of the existing fleet do.
- **Fusion is not ruled in canon.** The GDD discussion file says "Nothing here is ruled or canon". The author's intent (2026-11-12) is that the deuterium is for fusion, so the plan depends on a fusion plant that no one has built. The earlier files' tritium and lithium-6 problems still apply to it. Heavy-water reactors stay open as a second use, and as a possible tritium source for that fusion (see the author's answers).

**What adopting it would touch (the author decides; nothing has been written there).** The census row would gain "extraction from the sea and the seabed" as a third function beside fishing and radio relays. The GDD census row for the maritime fleet (one more function), the GDD's fusion and nuclear files if a deuterium source is wanted, and a decision record. The Timeline repo and the GDD were not edited for this note.

   Should the seabed part also gather lithium, uranium or other minerals, since seabed hydrogen is not richer in deuterium than the water?

3. Is a spare product for export wanted (heavy water is the one thing the Federation could sell, small as the volume is), or only a national supply?

**The author's answers (2026-11-12).**
1. **The deuterium is for fusion, definitely.** Heavy-water reactors are **kept open** as a possible further use; the author said they "may be a viable technology" and should not be dismissed. (The author asked what they are: fission reactors such as Canada's CANDU design, which use heavy water to slow neutrons so that natural uranium can run without enrichment.) They are also tied to fusion's hardest problem: the earlier F2 file found that heavy-water reactors are the only commercial source of tritium, at about 130 to 260 g per reactor-GWe-year (SOURCED, earlier file, CRS and Canadian figures). A heavy-water fleet fed by a Tepenian deuterium supply could therefore also help with the tritium shortage (§3). Whether canon has such reactors, and how many tritium-start-up kilograms they could yield, is unresearched.
2. **The seabed side also gathers other materials "where available"**: lithium (for tritium breeding), uranium and the like.
3. **Platform or separate ship is open.** Whether the plants sit on the relay platforms or on separate ships of the same fleet needs further research, to find the most realistically practical option. See the research items below.
4. The export question (item 3 above) was not answered.

**Research needed (author's call: find the most practical option).** None of this has been researched. The full plan, with the author's words, is in `_research_plan_sea_deuterium_and_seabed_2026-11-12.md` (same folder).

## 6. What this changes in the notes

- **Starlifting.** The gas giants are the solar system's bulk store of hydrogen and helium and a source of nitrogen. The reason to reach them is propellant, shielding and water, with fusion fuel a late bonus. This supplements `starlifting_synthesis.md`; it does not change the finding that solar matter itself is not a competitive source.
- **LEO.** Hydrogen-rich material doubles as shielding and propellant; imported hydrogen becomes water for habitats; nitrogen is the scarce part of an atmosphere and of plant growth. All are consumables for the habitats in `leo_infrastructure_architecture.md`, delivered by the supply chain the First and Second Interwar delivery modes represent. The video's hydrogen and nitrogen supply chain starts beyond the inner system, so it matters mainly for a later era than the two interwar periods.
- **Tepenia.** One idea for a deuterium source, and for seabed extraction beside it, in §5. It is the author's to decide.
