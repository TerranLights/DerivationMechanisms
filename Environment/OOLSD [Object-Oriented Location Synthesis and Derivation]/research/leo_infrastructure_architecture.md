# LEO infrastructure architecture: structure types, rotating-habitat anatomy, interiors, light and growth paths

*Research tranche 9, section A1. Started 2026-11-01. Research file (physics and engineering only).*

## 1. Scope

Structures in low Earth orbit (about 300-1,000 km), as architecture: modular stations flown and planned, rotating habitats (wheel or torus, cylinder, sphere, tethered or dumbbell pairs), and non-habitat infrastructure (depots, construction bases, hangars, power, debris removal). Covers the anatomy of a rotating habitat, interior layout under spin, how light is brought in, how stations grow, and what the LEO site forces on the outside. Environment numbers already in `leo_and_orbital_habitats.md` and `life_support_lowg_power_structures.md` are cited, not re-derived.

## 4. Conflicts

**C1. Tiangong dimensions and volume.** The Chinese Academy of Sciences gives Tianhe as 16.6 m long and 22.5 t; some secondary pages give 18.1 m and 22 t. China Daily, citing the agency, gives Mengtian "about 17.9 meters" and "more than 23 metric tons", and "as much as 110 cubic meters" of usable crew space; secondary pages give 122 m3 habitable and 340 m3 pressurized. Likely causes: length with or without docking hardware; "usable" versus "habitable" versus "pressurized" volume; launch mass versus on-orbit mass. I keep the agency-sourced figures.

**C2. Continuous sunlight in an LEO settlement.** Kalpana One aligns its axis with the ecliptic pole "to provide continuous natural light through transparent end caps" and proposes an equatorial orbit near 600 km. In an equatorial orbit the beta angle never exceeds the 23.44-degree obliquity, while an eclipse-free orbit at 600 km needs a beta angle above 66.1 degrees, so the settlement passes through Earth's shadow on every orbit, about 34-35 min of every 97 (section 5.7). The paper's lighting argument holds outside Earth's shadow (its general case "is not orbit-specific"); in the pages I read it does not address the eclipse. Not a disagreement between two measurements, but between a design claim and the orbit it proposes.

**C3. Acceptable spin rate.** SP-413: 1 rpm for living quarters (conservative). Kalpana One: 2 rpm. Nixon (2022): "around 2.0 rpm for human comfort ... based on present criteria". Globus and Hall (2015): up to 4 rpm for visitors with some adaptation, up to 6 rpm for residents. CR-178345's cited ground data: of 175 people, 0% sick at 3 rpm, 2% at 4, 15% at 5, 80% at 6, falling to 0, 0 and about 3% after two days of conditioning. Lackner and DiZio: adaptation to 10 rpm "with 10-20 additional movements" for arm and leg tasks. Zipay (NASA, 2019): HRP experiments planned "up to 15 rpm". Why they differ: residence versus short exposure; visitors versus adapted residents; ground rooms (with Earth's 1 g present and small radius) versus none in orbit; different end points (symptoms, task errors, adaptation time). No long human residence on a spinning structure has occurred.

**C4. Shield attached to the hull or separate.** SP-413 keeps a 9.9 Mt shield separate and non-rotating 1.5 m from the torus, with a "positive positioning device" not designed in the report; Kalpana One rotates the shield with the hull and calls the separate design a catastrophic failure mode. Both are design choices: a separate shield saves hull strength, a co-rotating one removes the contact risk. In ELEO with little or no shielding (Globus and Strout's model), the question largely disappears; above the belts it does not.

**C5. Gravity-gradient precession formula.** Myers (1966), read through OCR damage, gives an averaged precession coefficient with (C-A)/C; a search summary (source not opened) gives "3n^2(I2/I1 - 1)/2 omega (1 - e^2)^(3/2)", that is (C-A)/A. For a thin ring the two differ by a factor of 2 (0.5 against 1.0), for a Kalpana-type cylinder by 1.2 (0.167 against 0.2). The likely cause is which spin rate is meant (body spin relative to the orbit frame versus inertial) or OCR damage; either way the precession period stays days to months (section 5.21), which is the architectural point.

**C6. Spoke design inside one report.** NASA CR-178345 Table 2.0-1 gives the reference spokes as a 3.2 m tube inside a 5.2 m truss; its Table 7.1-7 gives telescoping spokes 9.2 m in diameter and 90 m long at 66,491 kg each. The first is the reference configuration carried from an earlier study; the second is this study's proposed layout with elevators and a variable-gravity facility inside.

**C7. Precession load for a Sun-facing spinning station.** CR-178345 budgets 1,866 N m to precess its torus once a year to stay Sun-facing (109,200 kg of propellant a year, or negligible with counter-rotation). My orbit-averaged gravity-gradient estimate for the same torus held 23-45 degrees off the orbit normal is 7-15 kN m (section 5.22), four to eight times larger. In the pages I read the report does not include a gravity-gradient term in that budget (a figure title mentions a gravity-gradient-stabilized central tube during assembly). Either the term is elsewhere in the report or it was left out; the thin-ring inertia assumption makes my number an order of magnitude only.

**C8. Gravity Ring abstract.** It cites "low rotational speeds (less than 2 rpm)" as the reason for centrifugation, yet promises "up to Mars-level artificial gravity" on a 60 m ring, which needs about 3.4 rpm (at 2 rpm the rim gets 0.13 g). Probably the 2 rpm sentence describes the field in general, not this ring.

**C9. Required volume per person.** NASA's expedition minimum is 25 m3 per person (Mars-class mission); SP-413's settlement allowance is 823 m3 per person for community uses, 33 times more; Haven-1's design gives 11.25 m3 per person for two-week visits. These differ by mission type (expedition, settlement, short stay), not by error.

**C10. Solar constant.** SP-413 used 1,400 W/m2; NASA's current figure is 1,361.0 W/m2 (updated 15 Nov 2024). Epoch and measurement method; the sibling file notes the same for 1,390 W/m2 in SP-413 Ch. 2.

**5.5 Curvature of the floor.** For an observer on a floor of radius R, a point s further along the floor lies h = R(1 - cos(s/R)) above the observer's tangent plane. R 224 m: 5.6, 22.0, 83.5 m. R 56 m: 50 m along rises 20.9 m (51 degrees of arc). R 3,200 m: 0.4, 1.6, 6.2 m.

**5.6 Star field through a window.** The view turns at the spin rate: 1 rpm 6 degrees/s (a full turn in 60 s); 1.9 rpm 11 degrees/s (32 s); 2 rpm 12 degrees/s (30 s); 4 rpm 24 degrees/s (15 s); 6 rpm 36 degrees/s (10 s). Section A2 of this tranche treats what that does to viewers.

**5.7 Orbit, eclipse and beta angle.** Circular orbit, cylindrical shadow: eclipse fraction f = (1/pi) acos( sqrt(h^2 + 2 Re h) / (r cos beta) ) for |beta| < asin(Re/r), else 0.
```

h 500 km: period 94.6 min; 35.8 / 34.7 / 21.8 min; eclipse-free above 68.0 deg

check: h 350 km, beta 58.5 deg -> 26.7 min of 91.5 (TRMM: about 26 min, [LEO §A1])
sunrises per day: 15.6 (400 km), 15.2 (500 km), 14.9 (600 km)
```

**5.16 Settlements on one shell.** At 500 km the orbit circumference is 2 pi (6,878.137 km) = 43,217 km; at 1,000 km spacing, 43 slots (Globus and Strout: "about 40").

**5.17 Adding mass to a spinning ring.** With no external torque, I1 omega1 = I2 omega2. Adding a fraction f of the rim mass at the rim: omega falls by f/(1+f) and g by 1 - 1/(1+f)^2: f = 1% gives 0.99% and 1.97%; 5% gives 4.76% and 9.30%; 10% gives 9.09% and 17.36%.

Annual Sun-tracking precession rate 2 pi / 3.156 x 10^7 s = 1.99 x 10^-7 rad/s; then L = I omega Omega = 3.198 x 10^10 x 0.2932 x 1.99 x 10^-7 = 1,866 N m, which reproduces the report.

**5.21 Gravity-gradient precession of a spinning habitat.** Orbit-averaged rate for an axisymmetric body spinning at omega_s, axis at angle theta from the orbit normal: Omega = (3/2)(n^2/omega_s)((C - A)/C) cos theta (Myers 1966 coefficient for a circular orbit; see Conflicts C5), n = sqrt(GM/r^3). Inertia ratios: thin ring (C - A)/C = 0.5 (standard thin-ring moments, MR^2 about the axis and MR^2/2 about a diameter); Kalpana-type cylinder at the 1.2 stability limit (C - A)/C = 1 - 1/1.2 = 0.167. Periods 2 pi / Omega at cos theta = 1:
```
            n (rad/s)    1 rpm ring/cyl   2 rpm ring/cyl   4 rpm ring/cyl   (days)
h 400 km   1.1314e-3     7.9 / 23.8       15.9 / 47.6      31.7 / 95.2
h 500 km   1.1068e-3     8.3 / 24.9       16.6 / 49.7      33.2 / 99.5
h 600 km   1.0831e-3     8.7 / 26.0       17.3 / 51.9      34.6 / 103.9
```
The rate scales as n^2/omega_s and does not depend on the habitat's size or mass.

**5.22 Gravity-gradient torque on the CR-178345 torus.** Averaged torque T = (3/2) n^2 (C - A) sin theta cos theta, with C - A taken as C/2 (thin ring) and n at 480 km. Torus only (C = 2.16 x 10^10 kg m2): 10.0 kN m at theta 45 degrees, 7.3 kN m at 23.44 degrees. Torus assembly (C = 3.198 x 10^10): 14.8 and 10.8 kN m. The report's Sun-tracking torque is 1,866 N m.

## 6. Constraints for any inhabitant

Each is a condition a structure in LEO imposes on whoever lives or works in it, tied to the findings above.

- **The flown stations checked here are clusters of cylinders about 4.2 m across, sized to their launchers,** joined at nodes and grown one module at a time; none has spun for gravity (A). Habitable volume per metric ton ran from 0.9 (ISS, with its trusses and arrays) to about 4 (Mir) m3/t (5.1).
- **The only flown spin-gravity system gave about 0.00015 g for a few hours** (Gemini XI, 1966). Everything else about living under spin comes from ground rooms with Earth's gravity present (B, E).
- **Spin rate is a trade between size and comfort.** On the ground, 3 rpm made none of 175 people sick, 6 rpm made 80% sick before two days of conditioning and about 3% after; residents may tolerate 4-6 rpm, which allows 1 g at radii of 25-56 m (E, C3, 5.3). Changes of spin rate and moving between rim and axis are separate adaptations (D, `[LEO §C]`).
- **Up, down, east and west differ.** Apparent slope changes with walking speed and direction; no stair can keep a constant apparent slope both ways at small radius; ladders, poles and handholds suit the low-g levels near the hub; a 5 m/s elevator in a spoke pushes sideways at 10% of g at 1 rpm and 43% at 4 rpm (E, 5.4).
- **Loads handled near one's limit become risky** because Coriolis forces may reach a quarter of the weight being moved (E).
- **The floor curves up into the view.** At a 250 m radius the floor 100 m away stands 20 m above the observer's own floor level; at 895 m a kilometer of street turns through 64 degrees (E, 5.5). The sky, if seen at all, may lie beneath the floor (Hall).
- **The star field through any window turns at the spin rate**, 6-36 degrees per second for 1-6 rpm (5.6).
- **In low-inclination LEO the Sun sets every orbit**, for 34-36 minutes of every 92-97, about 15-16 times a day; sunlight-based lighting must store, shutter or supplement light for that third of every orbit (H, 5.7).
- **Electric light for people is cheap in power; for crops it is not.** Light for nominal activities (300 lux, the top of NASA's 100-300 lux range) needs about 2 W per m2 of floor at 158 lm/W before room losses; a settlement design with artificially lit crops budgeted 60 kW per person, 50 kW of it for agricultural lights (F, 5.8, 5.13).
- **The rotating-to-static joint is a single point of failure that wears.** The largest flown rotary joint found in this research (ISS SARJ, 3.2 m, unpressurized) failed in under five months from lubrication and needs regreasing about every three years; no large pressurized rotary seal has flown, and a LEO station study sized its 16.5 m hub seal with replaceable inflatable elements (D).
- **A spinning habitat's axis will not stay where it is put in LEO.** Gravity gradient turns it in days to months at 1-4 rpm, whatever the habitat's size; holding it Sun-facing costs torque continuously (kN m-class for a 229 m torus) or the axis must sit along the orbit normal (H, 5.21, 5.22).
- **Mass added to a spinning rim slows the spin** (1% more rim mass, about 2% less gravity) unless the addition arrives already spinning or thrust restores the rate (G, 5.17).
- **Outer surfaces facing the direction of travel lose polymer films at tens of micrometers a year** to atomic oxygen; polymer mirrors and films designed for L5 do not last in LEO without protection (H, 5.18).
- **Large exposed areas collect debris strikes and drag.** A 50 x 35 x 25 m hangar needed 23-30 t of 1.4 mm aluminum shielding for a 50% chance of no 7 mm or larger hit in 10 years; shrinking exposed area cuts shield mass, drag and reboost (H).
- **Building very large, light structures in LEO is dominated by attitude control**, not drag: about 10% of the structure's mass in propellant for a power-satellite build in LEO against under 0.1% at GEO (H).
- **No settlement in orbit has ever existed;** every settlement-scale number here is a design study, and the unshielded-ELEO idea rests on one model the authors call "preliminary" (B).

## 7. Candidate handwaves

Flagged only; none accepted. The developer decides.

1. **A large pressurized rotary seal that does not leak or wear out.** None has flown at meter scale; NASA's LEO station study lists "Large-diameter gas seals for joints that can have motions ranging from steady state to cyclic, with reversals of direction and extended periods of no motion" as a technology need and planned seal change-out; the flown 3.2 m SARJ (unpressurized) failed in months. A design that treats the hub joint as maintenance-free relies on an unproven component (D).
2. **Continuous or Earth-like natural daylight in LEO.** At low inclination every orbit has 34-36 min of shadow; eclipse-free orbits need beta angles above 66-70 degrees, which only high-inclination (for example dawn-dusk) orbits reach for long. A design showing steady sunlight in an equatorial LEO habitat is bending the geometry (H, C2, 5.7).
3. **A spin axis that points at the Sun by itself in LEO.** Gravity gradient precesses a spinning habitat in days to months; Sun-pointing requires continuous control torque (or an orbit-normal axis, which is within 23.44 degrees of ecliptic-normal only for equatorial orbits). Flag any design that assumes passive Sun-pointing (5.21, 5.22, C7).
4. **Permanent residence at 4-6 rpm or more.** Supported by ground studies and a preprint review; no one has lived on a spinning structure. Choosing a small radius (25-56 m for 1 g) is a stated assumption (C3).
5. **No radiation shielding in equatorial LEO.** One modeling study (OLTARIS, a model its authors say runs low for trapped particles) supports it, and only within about 5 degrees of the equator. Section 4 of this tranche owns the question (B).
6. **Light piped from outside into a spinning interior.** Silica fiber carries concentrated sunlight with about 6% loss per 10 m, but only within a narrow acceptance angle (over 50% transmission below about 15 degrees off-axis); no habitat design was found that does this, and a spinning hull keeps changing the angle to the Sun (F).
7. **Polymer mirrors and films that survive in LEO.** Unprotected PET and Kapton facing the ram direction lose 36-64 micrometers a year (5.18); a mirror design copied from L5 studies needs protective coatings not researched here.
8. **Extending a spinning ring without disturbing it.** Only a patented concept (a non-rotating core with circling modules) claims expansion "without perturbing the ongoing artificial gravity"; for a rigid ring, added rim mass slows the spin unless matched (G, 5.17).
9. **A "positive positioning device" between a spinning hull and a non-rotating shield 1.5 m away.** SP-413 names it without a design; Kalpana One calls the arrangement a catastrophic failure mode (D, C4).
10. **Assembling an entire very large structure in LEO cheaply.** The 1977 power-satellite study found attitude-control propellant of about 10% of the structure's mass and up to 350 MW for control in LEO; it judged complete LEO assembly "not ... technically desirable" (H).
