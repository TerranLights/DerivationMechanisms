# Avenues of investigation: 11 Aquarius (community and ideals)

**What this file is.** For each Aquarius concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Aquarius concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings. For every concept, the Words avenue also covers frequency in corpora and search data (Google Books Ngram, the Corpus of Contemporary American English, the Real Academia's CORPES XXI, the *Diccionario del español de México*, Google Trends) and the names that carry the concept (places, streets, children, businesses, mottos), as in the Investigation Sheet (B1); only cases worth naming are listed below.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; settled with the author just before this sign's pass): community, group, equality, reform, idealism, friendship, nonconformity, detachment, hope. Hope is the author's named example (settled under Aquarius, 2026-11-02). Freedom (proposed home Sagittarius) and belonging (proposed home Cancer) get cross-references only, at the end of the file. Questions to settle:
- **Community and group** overlap. This file splits them: **community** is the bond among people who share a life (a place, a faith, a trade, an origin, a cause) and owe one another something; **group** is the collective as a unit and its pull on the person (us and them, loyalty, conformity, acting together). Two entries, or one?
- **Idealism, reform and hope** all face toward a better state. Idealism holds a standard of how things ought to be; reform is deliberate change toward it; hope wants a good that may or may not come. Three entries, or fewer?
- **Equality** sits beside Libra's justice and fairness and against Capricorn's hierarchy. Is Aquarius its home?
- **Detachment** could sit in Pisces (dissolution, surrender) or beside Libra's impartiality. This file takes it as emotional and social distance, both valued (calm, objectivity) and condemned (coldness, indifference).
- **Nonconformity** borders Aries (identity, self-assertion) and Capricorn (order).
- **Possibly missing** from the list (see section 0): solidarity and cooperation; civic participation; humanitarianism toward strangers; tolerance and diversity; progress and the future; loneliness.

## 0. The domain: community and ideals

**Traditional areas:** friends, groups, hopes, humanitarian ideals. **The domain question:** what binds people into groups, and what do they hope for?

- **The person and the group.** Individualism and collectivism (Hofstede; Triandis's horizontal and vertical forms; GLOBE's in-group and institutional collectivism); Vandello and Cohen's collectivism index by U.S. state (1999); Woodard, *American Character* (2016), on individual liberty against the common good, region by region; Elazar's political cultures, in which the moralistic culture treats politics as the pursuit of the common good (Elazar classed Oregon and Washington as moralistic *check*); Lipset, *Continental Divide* (1990), on the United States and Canada. Where the culture draws the line between "mine" and "ours".
- **Associational life.** Tocqueville on the American "art of association" (*Democracy in America*, 1835-1840); Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000); Skocpol, *Diminished Democracy* (2003), on the decline of mass-membership federations. What kinds of associations the culture builds:
  - churches and congregations; lodges and fraternal orders (Masons, Odd Fellows, Elks, Knights of Columbus); the Grange (strong in rural Oregon and Washington *check*); service clubs (Rotary, Lions, Kiwanis);
  - mutual-aid societies (*sociedades mutualistas* in the Southwest, such as the Alianza Hispano-Americana, Tucson, 1894); migrants' hometown associations (*clubes de oriundos*); rotating savings groups (*tandas*); Chinese district and family associations in San Francisco and Vancouver; Japanese prefectural associations (*kenjinkai*);
  - cooperatives (REI; PCC Community Markets in Seattle; credit unions; Group Health Cooperative of Puget Sound, 1947); unions; outdoor clubs (the Sierra Club, 1892; the Mazamas, 1894; The Mountaineers, 1906); volunteer fire departments;
  - *ejido* and *comunidad agraria* assemblies; the nations' own governments, societies and clan systems, as they describe them.
- **Humanitarian ideals** (a traditional area the concepts don't cover). Who counts as "humanity", and what is owed to strangers far away:
  - the moral circle: how far concern reaches (the Moral Expansiveness Scale, Crimston et al., 2016 *check*);
  - world citizenship: the World Values Survey items "I see myself as a world citizen", "part of my local community", "part of the [country] nation" and "an autonomous individual" (*check* the waves);
  - human rights as an ideal; the "brotherhood of man";
  - aid abroad: Peace Corps volunteers per capita by state and metro (the Peace Corps' yearly rankings *check*); Canada's peacekeeping self-image (Pearson's Nobel Prize, 1957); Mexico's asylum tradition (Spanish Republican exiles from 1939; South American exiles in the 1970s) and the Estrada Doctrine of non-intervention;
  - strangers at the door: private sponsorship of refugees in Canada (since 1979; the Syrian arrivals of 2015-2016, by community); *casas del migrante* (the Scalabrinians' house in Tijuana, 1987 *check*); the churches of the 1980s sanctuary movement, which sheltered refugees from Central America (strong in the Bay Area *check*);
  - philanthropy: the Gates Foundation and the large Seattle and Bay Area foundations; giving to causes abroad (Giving USA by cause; IRS Statistics of Income on charitable deductions by ZIP code);
  - religious teaching on the stranger (the Good Samaritan; Matthew 25; Catholic social teaching; Quaker service, such as the American Friends Service Committee);
  - effective altruism, a present-day movement strong in the Bay Area, and its critics.
  (Compassion and charity as such belong to Pisces; here the subject is ideals toward humanity at large.)
- **Networks and weak ties.** Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973); networking as a career practice (LinkedIn; alumni networks); Saxenian, *Regional Advantage* (1994), on Silicon Valley's open networks against Boston's Route 128; *palancas* (string-pulling connections) and *compadrazgo* in Mexico; *guanxi* in Chinese communities (an example from elsewhere, also present in Vancouver and San Francisco); Facebook's Social Connectedness Index (Bailey et al., 2018), which maps friendship ties between counties.
- **Entering society.** How a person is presented to the wider group and received by it: debutante and cotillion balls; the *quinceañera* as social presentation (its identity side is under Aries); the Portland Rose Festival's court of high-school princesses (*check* since when); rushing fraternities and sororities; being "jumped in" to a gang; joining a congregation; naturalization ceremonies; "coming out" into a gay community; taking a name or first standing up at a potlatch, as the nations describe it.
- **Progress, science and the future** (the domain's forward face). Belief in progress (Nisbet, *History of the Idea of Progress*, 1980); trust in science (Pew's science surveys; the Wellcome Global Monitor, 2018 and 2020, country); GLOBE's future orientation; Silicon Valley's techno-optimism and its critics; transhumanism, a present-day Bay Area current; present-day attitudes to AI and robots as bringers of a better or worse future (Pew's AI surveys *check*).
- **Revolution and upheaval.** The Mexican Revolution (1910-1920) as a founding myth and its official memory (November 20); the American Revolution; Canada's founding without a revolution (Lipset's "counter-revolutionary" reading, contested); the 1911 Magonista rising in Baja California; the World Values Survey item asking whether society must be changed by revolutionary action, gradually improved by reforms, or defended against subversion.
- **Causes and activism.** Protest, boycotts and petitions (the World Values Survey's and the ISSP's political-action items; the Crowd Counting Consortium's U.S. protest counts, from 2017); ballot-measure campaigns; online activism and "slacktivism"; the *luchador social* (Mexican Spanish for a lifelong activist).
- **Tolerance and diversity.** Welcome for people of other classes, races, faiths and ways of life: the World Values Survey item on groups one would not want as neighbors; the GSS civil-liberties items (from Stouffer's 1954 survey); Canada's multiculturalism policy (1971); Mexico's national discrimination survey (ENADIS, 2017 and 2022); residential segregation by metro (dissimilarity indexes from the census).
- **Across cultures.** For any society in the world: the Human Relations Area Files' Outline of Cultural Materials, under interpersonal relations (social relationships and groups, friendships, cliques, visiting and hospitality, sodalities, ethics, in-group antagonisms; the 570s *check*) and community (the 620s *check*); the *Ethnographic Atlas* in D-PLACE (settlement pattern, community size, levels of political organization above the local community, class stratification *check* the variable codes); the Standard Cross-Cultural Sample's codes on formal friendship and age-sets (*check*); newcomer and immigrant guides (WelcomeBC's newcomers' guide; relocation forums), for what outsiders are warned to expect about making friends and joining in.
- **Social ways** (Fischer's folkway for Aquarius; in Fischer, the customs of settlement, migration, association and affiliation *check*):
  - settlement pattern: compact towns, dispersed farms and ranches, company towns, logging and cannery camps, mining camps, reserves and reservations, *ranchos*, *ejidos*, *colonias* on the urban edge, suburbs and gated communities; how each shapes who meets whom;
  - migration pattern: who came with whom (chain migration from one Mexican town to one California town; the Oregon Trail's family parties against the gold rush's lone young men; whole congregations moving, such as the Doukhobors to British Columbia);
  - hospitality: dropping in against calling first; *mi casa es su casa*; potlucks (the English word is older than "potlatch" and unrelated to it *check*); feeding guests; the *sobremesa* (lingering at the table after a meal);
  - neighboring: borrowing; watching each other's houses; Nextdoor (launched in San Francisco, 2011); block parties; *posadas* that go from house to house;
  - gathering places: plazas and the evening stroll; churches and church halls; taverns, coffeehouses and "third places" (Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place*, 1989); county fairs; community halls and Royal Canadian Legion branches; longhouses and community centers, as the nations share them;
  - greeting strangers: whether people nod or greet in passing; *buenos días* to a whole room; *provecho* to people eating;
  - mixing and separation by class, race, faith and generation: segregated neighborhoods and their legacy (the federal "redlining" maps of the 1930s; sundown towns); exclusive clubs (the Bohemian Club in San Francisco; the Rainier Club in Seattle; the Arlington Club in Portland); intermarriage rates (ACS; Pew).
- **Freedom ways** (runner-up; home sign Sagittarius): see `09 - Sagittarius.md`. The freedom to join a group and to leave one is noted here under group.

## 1. Community

**Working definition:** the bond among people who share a life (a place, a faith, a trade, an origin, an interest, a cause), recognize one another as members and owe one another something; and the felt quality of that bond.
**Facets:** the place community (block, neighborhood, *barrio*, *colonia*, town, *rancho*, reserve or reservation); the faith community (congregation, parish); the ethnic or national community ("the Latino community"); the work community (logging camps, fishing fleets, mining towns, tech campuses); communities of interest (clubs, scenes, fandoms, online forums); intentional communities (communes, cohousing, religious colonies); civic community (associations, volunteering, public meetings); community in disaster; the moral community (whom one owes); the loss of community.
**Neighbors and opposites:** neighborhood, fellowship, solidarity, social capital, mutual aid, the commons; *Gemeinschaft* against *Gesellschaft* (Tönnies, 1887); isolation, anonymity, atomization, loneliness, anomie (Durkheim). **Excess:** insularity, parochialism, neighbors watching neighbors, exclusion of outsiders, gated communities. **Deficiency:** isolation; "bowling alone".

- **Words:** "community" and how widely it is stretched ("the tech community", "the LGBTQ community", "community theater", "community college"); "neighborly", "small-town feel", "everybody knows everybody", "pitch in", "barn raising", "potluck", "bee" (quilting bee), "block party", "town hall", "NIMBY" and "YIMBY" (the YIMBY movement began in the San Francisco Bay Area in the 2010s); "the Seattle Freeze"; Spanish *comunidad*, *vecinos*, *vecindad* (a shared courtyard tenement, and the life in it), *barrio*, *colonia*, *pueblo* (both a town and a people), *convivir* and *convivencia* (spending time together, a value word in Mexican Spanish; the office party called a *convivio*), *echar la mano* (lending a hand), *tequio*, *faena* and *mano vuelta* (communal and exchanged labor in rural Mexico, kept by Oaxacan migrants in Baja California and Los Angeles), *cooperación* (the household's share for a fiesta), *mayordomía* (sponsoring a saint's fiesta); the Nuu-chah-nulth phrase *hishuk ish tsawalk* ("everything is one"), as the Nuu-chah-nulth publish it *check*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is community given (born into a place or people) or chosen (joined among like minds)? Is it a duty, or a good one may take or leave? Community against individual liberty (Woodard, *American Character*); communitarian thinkers (Etzioni; Bellah et al., *Habits of the Heart*, 1985, with interviews in California *check*); Christian fellowship and the parish; the Puritan covenant ("a city upon a hill", Winthrop, 1630); Catholic teaching on the common good and subsidiarity; the Latter-day Saints' ward; Indigenous teachings that community includes land, animals and ancestors, as the nations state them; the ideal against the real (praise of "community" alongside neighbors unknown by name).
- **Feeling and the body:** the felt sense of community (McMillan and Chavis, 1986; the Sense of Community Index *check*); loneliness and its toll on health (Holt-Lunstad's meta-analyses, 2010 and 2015); warmth or reserve in public greeting; collective effervescence (Durkheim) at festivals, games and services; shared meals; touch in greeting (the *abrazo*, the handshake, the nod).
- **Everyday practice:** knowing neighbors' names; borrowing; watching each other's children; casseroles and meal trains for the sick and bereaved; church potlucks and coffee hours; community gardens (Seattle's P-Patch program, from 1973 *check*); Little Free Libraries; Buy Nothing groups (begun on Bainbridge Island, Washington, 2013); mutual-aid networks (especially in 2020); *tandas*; fiesta *cooperaciones*; *tequio* among Oaxacan migrants; neighborhood and homeowners' associations; Nextdoor; school volunteering; volunteer firefighting; neighbors rescuing neighbors in disasters.
- **Life course:** being raised by "the village"; whether children may roam the block; youth groups, Scouts, 4-H; community-service hours that schools require for graduation (*check* where); Mexico's *servicio social*, the community service university students give before they graduate (*check* since when); joining and leaving congregations; retirement communities against "aging in place"; who comes to the funeral, and the memberships an obituary lists.
- **Ritual:** town festivals and parades (the Portland Rose Festival; Seattle's Seafair; the Fremont Solstice Parade; *fiestas patrias*; *fiestas patronales*); *posadas*; county and state fairs; Fourth of July and Canada Day picnics; Día de Muertos as a neighborhood event; gatherings the nations open to the public (Tribal Canoe Journeys on the Salish Sea, since 1989; powwows; first salmon ceremonies, as shared).
- **Institutions:** how close local government sits to residents (towns against counties; Mexico's *municipio* and its smaller units *check*); neighborhood councils (Los Angeles's, from 1999; Portland's neighborhood associations, from 1974); community land trusts; cohousing; congregations; community foundations (the Vancouver Foundation; the Seattle Foundation); community colleges; public libraries, parks and community centers; food banks; neighborhood houses and settlement houses (*check* which in the pilot); *ejido* assemblies; tribal and First Nations governments, with elected councils alongside hereditary governance, as the nations describe them.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** citizen-of-the-year awards; being known and greeted by name; gossip, shunning and the cold shoulder; NIMBY fights; expulsion from an *ejido* or a congregation; banishment as a sanction in some nations' traditions, as they describe it; neighborhood shaming online (Nextdoor's reputation).
- **Expression:** small-town and neighborhood fiction (Steinbeck, *Cannery Row*, 1945, and *Tortilla Flat*, 1935, both in Monterey); television (*The Beachcombers*, set in Gibsons, BC; *Northern Exposure*, filmed in Roslyn, Washington; the *vecindad* of *El Chavo del Ocho*); murals (Chicano Park in San Diego, 1970; Balmy Alley in San Francisco); community radio (KPFA, Berkeley, 1949); Carlos Monsiváis, *Entrada libre: crónicas de la sociedad que se organiza* (1987), and Elena Poniatowska, *Nada, nadie* (1988), on citizens organizing after the 1985 earthquake.
- **Exemplars:** organizers (Fred Ross and the Community Service Organization in Los Angeles, 1947, where César Chávez learned organizing; Saul Alinsky's method behind it); Father Greg Boyle and Homeboy Industries (Los Angeles, from 1988); Glide Memorial Church in San Francisco; the Black Panther Party's free breakfast program (Oakland, 1969); the volunteers of the 1985 and 2017 Mexico City earthquakes; searching mothers' collectives (*Madres Buscadoras de Sonora*); the nations' leaders and elders; counter-exemplars: the busybody, the slumlord, the cult leader.
- **History:** the founders' forms (Yankee towns and congregations carried to Oregon and Washington; the Spanish mission, presidio and pueblo; Hudson's Bay Company forts; Mormon settlements in the Great Basin; Chinatowns and Japantowns; company towns and logging camps); the nations' own communities before and after contact, told in their voices first (villages, house groups, seasonal camps; the great gathering and fishery at Celilo Falls, drowned by The Dalles Dam in 1957; removal to reserves and reservations; residential and boarding schools; resurgence); the 1906 San Francisco earthquake; the incarceration of Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians (1942) and the loss of whole communities; freeways and urban renewal through neighborhoods (Chavez Ravine in Los Angeles; Hogan's Alley in Vancouver; Albina in Portland); the 1985 Mexico City earthquake as the birth of organized civil society (Monsiváis's reading).
- **Measures:** the Current Population Survey's Civic Engagement and Volunteering Supplement (state and some metros: talking to neighbors, doing favors, group membership); the GSS items on spending a social evening with neighbors; the Joint Economic Committee's Social Capital Project index (2018, state and county); civic and social organizations per capita (County Business Patterns, NAICS 8134); nonprofits per capita (IRS Business Master File; the National Center for Charitable Statistics); Putnam's Social Capital Community Benchmark Survey (2000; *check* which pilot communities it sampled); Statistics Canada's "sense of belonging to local community" (Canadian Community Health Survey, by health region) and its survey on giving, volunteering and participating; the Vancouver Foundation's Connections and Engagement surveys (2012, 2017); INEGI's time-use survey (ENUT: unpaid community and volunteer work), its civic-culture survey (ENCUCI, 2020) and its satellite account of nonprofit institutions (volunteer work valued in money); World Values Survey memberships; loneliness (the UCLA Loneliness Scale; Statistics Canada's Canadian Social Survey; Cigna's surveys *check*).
- **Variation:** urban, suburban and rural; renters and owners; newcomers and old families; class (which neighborhoods have associations, and which have only their churches); ethnic enclaves against mixed areas; faith; generation (online communities); Indigenous nations' communities on and off reserve, each its own voice.
- **Relations:** group (its sibling entry); belonging and family (Cancer); friendship; equality (who is a full member); trust (Scorpio); service (Virgo); compassion (Pisces); autonomy and independence (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** newcomers (when does a newcomer become a member?); migrants and the undocumented; homeless people (neighbors or nuisances?); animals and land as members of the community, as the nations state it; companies that call their customers a "community"; online communities; whether people feel community with AI companions or social robots (present-day attitudes only).
- **Silences:** loneliness, especially men's and elders'; who is left out of "the community"; the coercive side of close communities; encampments and the homeless.
- **Change:** the decline of membership groups and church attendance; the rise of online communities; remote work; the 2020 pandemic's mutual aid and its isolation; housing costs scattering old neighborhoods; YIMBY against NIMBY.

**Key questions:** Is community something one is born into or something one joins? What does a member owe the others, and what happens to one who does not pay it?

## 2. Group

**Working definition:** a set of people who see themselves, or are seen by others, as a unit; here, how a culture weighs the group against the person, draws the line between "us" and "them", and expects members to act together.
**Facets:** the person's interest against the group's; in-groups and out-groups; loyalty and desertion; group pressure and conformity; acting together (strikes, teams, crews, campaigns); collective responsibility (shared credit, shared blame, collective punishment); the forms groups take (kin groups; house groups, clans and moieties, as the nations describe them; age-mates; teams, crews and gangs; cliques; parties, unions, congregations, clubs); joining and leaving; how groups decide (vote, consensus, elders, a leader); crowds and mobs.
**Neighbors and opposites:** collective, team, crew, faction, party, crowd, "us"; the individual, the loner, the lone wolf, the outsider. **Excess:** groupthink (Janis, 1972), tribalism, the mob, the cult, factional hatred. **Deficiency:** atomization; free riding; inability to act together.

- **Words:** "team player", "lone wolf", "us and them", "groupthink", "herd mentality", "sheeple", "clique", "crew", "squad", "posse", "gang", "joiner", "tribe" and "tribalism" (a disputed use where Indigenous nations call themselves tribes), "rat", "snitch" and "scab" (words for betraying the group); Spanish *grupo*, *colectivo* (also the name taken by collectives of families searching for the disappeared), *la banda* and *la palomilla* (a group of friends, in Mexican Spanish), *pandilla* (gang), *clica* (a gang's local set, from "clique"), *la bola* (the crowd; the masses of the Revolution), *nosotros* and *ellos*, *chaquetero* (turncoat), *malinchista* (one who sides with outsiders), *acarreados* (people bused to a political rally, a word from Mexico's corporatist politics); Canada's "two solitudes" (MacLennan's novel, 1945).
- **Beliefs and standing:** which comes first, the person or the group? Is loyalty to the group a virtue (the loyalty foundation in Moral Foundations theory) or a danger (tribalism)? Collective guilt and inherited responsibility (apologies by governments and churches for past wrongs); "united we stand"; Christian teaching on the body of believers; union solidarity ("an injury to one is an injury to all", the IWW's slogan); whether a people as a whole can be wronged and owed amends, or only its members can; whether a person is free to join a group and to leave it, or is held in it for life; the nations' teachings on clan and house obligations, as they share them.
- **Feeling and the body:** the pull of a crowd; chanting, singing and moving in unison (McNeill, *Keeping Together in Time*, 1995); uniforms, team colors and gang colors (red for Norteños, blue for Sureños in California); crests and regalia, as the nations describe them; the fear of being left out; the discomfort of standing out.
- **Everyday practice:** group work at school and work; team sports; office cliques; carpools; group chats; supporters' groups (the Seattle Sounders' March to the Match; the Portland Timbers Army); churches' small groups; family chat groups; who sits with whom at lunch; group travel; voting as one's group votes.
- **Life course:** schoolyard cliques and the popular crowd; graduating classes and age-mates; fraternity and sorority rush; gang initiation; military basic training; joining a union; taking one's place in a clan or house, as the nations describe it; retirement and the loss of the work group.
- **Ritual:** pep rallies and homecoming; initiation and hazing; oaths and pledges; union meetings; party conventions; gatherings of nations (Tribal Canoe Journeys; potlatches, as the nations describe them); regimental and veterans' reunions; the *grito* on the night of September 15 as a mass ritual of the nation.
- **Institutions:** parties; unions (the ILWU on the West Coast docks; the IWW in Northwest timber, from 1905; Mexico's corporatist labor federation, the CTM, in the PRI's labor sector); fraternities and sororities; gangs; churches and denominations; clubs and fraternal orders; homeowners' associations; sports clubs and supporters' groups; the military and its units; work crews (logging, fishing, firefighting); youth groups (Scouts; 4-H; church youth groups); online groups and forums; land held in common (*ejidos* and *comunidades agrarias*; the nations' shared territories, as they describe them).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** being one of the gang; nicknames and in-jokes; promotion as a "team player"; for the deserter: "rat", "scab", "traitor", shunning, expulsion, and violence in gangs; for the newcomer: hazing and exclusion; collective punishment (a whole class kept in); mobbing.
- **Expression:** labor and team songs ("Solidarity Forever", 1915); *corridos* about bands of men; films of crews and gangs (*Boyz n the Hood*, 1991; *Zoot Suit*, 1981; *Blood In Blood Out*, 1993); Ken Kesey, *Sometimes a Great Notion* (1964), an Oregon logging family against its striking town; Steinbeck, *In Dubious Battle* (1936); poles and crests as group emblems, as the nations describe them; team mascots and the fights over Native mascots.
- **Exemplars:** team captains; union leaders (Harry Bridges and the 1934 West Coast waterfront strike); founders of clubs and lodges; the loyal comrade; counter-exemplars: the traitor, the scab, the cult leader (Jim Jones and Peoples Temple, San Francisco, 1970s); the lone wolf who leaves the pack, hero or villain depending on the teller.
- **History:** the founders' groups (Yankee congregations; Appalachian kin groups on the Oregon Trail; the Spanish colonial *castas* and the separate *república de indios*; HBC brigades; Chinese and Japanese mutual-aid associations); the nations' own house groups, clans and moieties, as they describe them, and how the Canadian and U.S. governments replaced them with elected councils; the Everett Massacre (1916); the Seattle General Strike (1919); the 1934 waterfront strike; the Zoot Suit Riots (Los Angeles, 1943); the wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians as punishment of a whole group; the movements of the 1960s; gangs and the drug war.
- **Measures:** Hofstede's individualism; GLOBE's in-group and institutional collectivism; Vandello and Cohen's collectivism index (U.S. state, 1999); Gelfand's tightness-looseness (country, 2011) and Harrington and Gelfand (U.S. state, 2014); Bond and Smith's meta-analysis of Asch-type conformity studies across countries (1996); Moral Foundations loyalty scores; union membership (BLS by state; Statistics Canada by province; Mexico's employment survey, ENOE *check* that it asks); party identification by state (Gallup); affective polarization (the ANES feeling thermometers); county vote margins as a measure of political sorting (Bishop, *The Big Sort*, 2008); congregation membership by county (the U.S. Religion Census, 2020); gang membership as students report it in school surveys (the California Healthy Kids Survey, by district *check*); in-group favoritism and fairness in economic games across societies (Henrich et al., 2005 and 2010).
- **Variation:** generation (joiners and non-joiners); class (unions, clubs); ethnicity (enclaves; pan-ethnic labels such as "Latino" and "Asian American"); gender (men's and women's groups); urban and rural; political camp; each Indigenous nation's collective identity, in its own voice.
- **Relations:** community (its sibling entry); nonconformity (the person against the group); friendship (the small group of chosen equals); identity (Aries); belonging (Cancer); betrayal and power (Scorpio); hierarchy and duty (Capricorn); fairness and partiality (Libra).
- **Who it applies to:** whether a group (a nation, a people, a union, a company) can act, earn credit or bear blame as one, or only its members can; companies that people speak of as persons ("the company betrayed us"); animals as packs and herds (in metaphor and in fact); robots and AI seen as a group ("them") in present-day attitudes, and fears that AI deepens divisions between groups.
- **Silences:** what the group did (lynchings, riots, incarceration, expulsions); the pressure to stay in a group one wants to leave (gangs, cults, high-control churches); prejudice inside the in-group.
- **Change:** the decline of unions and lodges; partisan polarization; online tribes and echo chambers; identity-based groups; changes in gangs (*check* by place).

**Key questions:** When the person and the group want different things, which wins? Can a group as a whole be praised, blamed or punished?

## 3. Equality

**Working definition:** the idea that people are of equal worth or standing and should be treated alike in some respects. Which respects, and among whom, is where cultures differ.
**Facets:** equal worth (before God, by nature, as citizens); freedom from discrimination; political equality (one person, one vote); equality of opportunity; equality of outcome (income, wealth); equality of manners and status (forms of address, deference, dress); equality between the sexes, ethnic groups, classes, generations, regions and peoples; leveling norms that keep anyone from rising too far.
**Neighbors and opposites:** egalitarianism, parity, equity, democracy, solidarity; fairness and justice (Libra); hierarchy and rank (Capricorn), privilege, aristocracy, discrimination, segregation. **Excess:** forced sameness; leveling down; envy as a sanction; identical treatment that ignores different needs. **Deficiency:** privilege, deference, discrimination.

- **Words:** "all men are created equal", "equal opportunity", "level playing field", "nobody's better than anybody", "who do you think you are?", "elitist", "uppity", "putting on airs", "privilege" and "check your privilege", "equity" against "equality" (a live dispute of the 2020s), "the one percent" and "the 99 percent" (Occupy, 2011), "tall poppy"; Canada's "equalization" (federal payments to poorer provinces); Spanish *igualdad*, *equidad*, *igualdad sustantiva* (substantive equality, a term of Mexican official and feminist usage *check*), *paridad* (gender parity), *todos somos iguales*, *nadie es más que nadie* (*check* its use in Mexico), *igualado* (one who acts as the equal of their betters: equality as an insult); class words that mark inequality: *naco* (a classist slur), *fifí* (the elite, in López Obrador's usage from 2018), *whitexican*, *mirrey*, *pirrurris* (*check*); forms of address (*tú* against *usted*; first names at work; "sir" and "ma'am"); the Quakers' plain "thee" in Fischer's account.

## 4. Reform

**Working definition:** deliberate change meant to improve a society's institutions, practices or people, short of overturning the whole; and the figure of the reformer.

## 8. Detachment

**Working definition:** keeping distance (emotional, personal or social) from people, from one's own feelings, or from outcomes. Cultures value some forms of it (calm, objectivity, impartiality, equanimity) and condemn others (coldness, indifference, withdrawal).
**Facets:** emotional reserve and display rules; composure under stress (cool, stoicism); objectivity (in science, journalism and the professions); impartiality (the referee's; see Libra); equanimity and non-attachment in religious and contemplative practice; reserve with strangers; solitude and withdrawal; indifference and apathy (political apathy; the bystander); ironic detachment ("whatever"); detachment from place and possessions.
**Neighbors and opposites:** reserve, composure, equanimity, coolness, objectivity, aloofness, solitude, indifference, apathy; engagement, attachment, warmth, passion, involvement, *simpatía*. **Excess:** coldness, indifference to suffering, alienation, isolation. **Deficiency:** enmeshment; being swept along by feeling or by the crowd; partiality.

- **Beliefs and standing:** is distance wisdom or coldness?
