# Avenues of investigation: 07 Libra (partnership and justice)

**What this file is.** For each Libra concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Libra concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; each sign's list is settled with the author just before its pass): justice, fairness, partnership, balance, impartiality, diplomacy, reciprocity, indecision, peace at any price, projection. Justice was settled under Libra by the author (2026-11-02). Questions to settle:
- **Near-synonyms inside the sign.** Justice and fairness overlap. This file takes **justice** as the large order of wrongs, rights and their answers (courts, punishment, redress, the just society) and **fairness** as everyday even-handedness in shares, turns and rules. **Impartiality** overlaps with both; it is kept for favoritism, kin and connections against equal treatment. **Balance** is the widest and most abstract entry.
- **Near-synonyms in other signs.** Fairness against equality (Aquarius's ten); diplomacy against mediation (Gemini's ten); justice against law (Capricorn's ten) and crime (Pisces, settled); negative reciprocity against revenge (Scorpio's ten); peace at any price against conflict avoidance (in four signs' tables, a Tier 2 candidate). Indecision also appears in Gemini's and Pisces's tables.
- **Fit.** Projection is a psychological mechanism more than a value; this file researches it through blame, scapegoating and the images a culture makes of its enemies and outsiders. Peace at any price is the excess of peacekeeping; the virtue it distorts (peace, peacemaking) has no entry of its own.
- **Gaps.** Two of the domain's traditional areas, contracts and promise-keeping and open enemies, have no concept; they are in section 0. Mercy and forgiveness, cooperation and compromise, and corruption are candidates for the author.

## 0. The domain: partnership and justice

**Traditional areas:** partnership, marriage, contracts, open enemies, balance, justice. **The domain question:** what is a fair bargain and a good partnership?

- **Self and other.** How far other people's claims count as much as one's own. Morality as cooperation: Curry, Mullins and Whitehouse (2019) looked for seven cooperative behaviors (helping kin, helping one's group, reciprocating, bravery, deferring to superiors, dividing disputed goods, respecting prior possession) in the ethnographies of 60 societies (*check* the list). Economic games across societies: ultimatum, dictator and public-goods games (Henrich et al., 2001, 2005 and 2010, in small-scale societies; Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter, 2008, on punishing cooperators in 16 cities). Free riders and how they are handled.
- **Bonds between equals.** The forms a culture uses to bind unrelated people: marriage (below); business partnership; ritual co-parenthood, *compadrazgo* (Mintz and Wolf, 1950), with *compadres* chosen at baptism, first communion, *quinceañera* and wedding, and in Mexico sometimes for a new house or truck; sworn friendship; adoption into a house or clan, as the nations describe it; *paisanos* and hometown associations (*clubes de oriundos*); fraternal orders; political coalitions; treaties.
- **Contracts and promises.** Handshake against paper: "a man's word is his bond", "shake on it", *palabra de honor*, *trato hecho*. Macaulay's "Non-Contractual Relations in Business" (1963), on how seldom businessmen used contract law; Ellickson's *Order Without Law* (1991), on how Shasta County, California, ranchers settle cattle-trespass disputes by neighborly norms, not law; Bernstein (1992) on diamond dealers' private rules. Oral deals in ranching, fishing and farm labor; labor contractors (*contratistas*) and the old *enganche* (labor recruited against an advance). The notary: in Mexico the *notario público* is a lawyer appointed by the state, unlike a U.S. notary, and "notario" fraud preys on immigrants who confuse the two. Prenuptial agreements and Mexico's *capitulaciones matrimoniales*. The common law's "consideration" (a promise binds only if something is given for it) against the civil law's *causa* (a seam between the U.S. and Canadian common law and Mexican and Quebec civil law). Measures: the World Bank's discontinued Doing Business "enforcing contracts" indicator, with its subnational reports on Mexico's states (*check*); civil contract cases per capita (the National Center for State Courts' Court Statistics Project).
- **Treaties as promises between peoples.** The Douglas Treaties on Vancouver Island (1850-1854); the Stevens treaties in Washington Territory (1854-1855), negotiated through Chinook Jargon; Treaty 8 (1899), which reaches northeastern British Columbia; the eighteen California treaties of 1851-1852 that the Senate never ratified and kept secret until 1905; the modern BC treaties (Nisga'a, 2000; Tsawwassen, 2009; Maa-nulth, 2011); the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) and its promises to Mexican citizens. How each side has understood the promise since, told in the nations' own voices first.
- **Open enemies and rivals.** The declared adversary, whom the traditional areas place beside the partner. Words: "enemy", "rival", "nemesis", "frenemy", "beef"; *enemigo*, *rival*, *contrincante*, *traer bronca* (to have a beef with someone *check*). Feuds and their rules; dueling (the Broderick-Terry duel, California, 1859, in which a former state chief justice killed a U.S. senator); range wars; prison and street-gang rivalries (Norteños and Sureños in California); city and school rivalries (Aries, competition); the loyal opposition of Westminster government in British Columbia; enemies in war and how they were treated (enemy aliens and internment; prisoners of war; the Geneva Conventions); rules for fighting fair (the Marquess of Queensberry rules; "no hitting below the belt"; *pelea limpia* *check*); how enemies are reconciled; warfare and peacemaking as the nations describe them. Measures: affective polarization (the American National Election Studies' feeling thermometers; Pew on how partisans see each other).
- **Settling disputes.** Who settles a quarrel: the parties, family elders, clergy, *compadres*, a mediator, a court, an arbitrator, or a feud. The dispute pyramid (Felstiner, Abel and Sarat, 1980-1981: naming, blaming, claiming); small-claims courts; British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal (2016, Canada's first online tribunal); Mexico's *juzgados cívicos* for neighbor quarrels and minor offenses, and its laws on alternative dispute mechanisms (*check* years); community mediation centers; restorative justice (the first victim-offender reconciliation case, Elmira, Ontario, 1974); circle sentencing (*R. v. Moses*, Yukon, 1992); tribal courts and peacemaking (the Northwest Intertribal Court System *check*; the Navajo Nation's Peacemaking Program, elsewhere). Measures: civil filings per capita by state (Court Statistics Project); INEGI's censuses of state justice systems.
- **Civility and manners.** The forms that keep strangers' dealings smooth: queues, *usted* and *tú*, *mande*, "please" and "thank you", door-holding, road manners, apology. Etiquette books (Emily Post; Miss Manners; Carreño's *Manual de urbanidad y buenas maneras*, 1853, long taught in Mexico *check*). Surveys of incivility (Weber Shandwick's *Civility in America*, practitioner). The overlap with speech ways (Gemini).
- **Marriage ways** (Fischer's folkway for Libra):
  - choosing a spouse: dating, *noviazgo*, family approval, the formal request for the bride's hand (*pedida de mano*), apps; how much say parents and kin have;
  - the wedding: civil and religious ceremonies (in Mexico only the civil one has legal effect, since the Reform Laws of 1859); *padrinos* who sponsor the rings, the *lazo*, the *arras* (thirteen coins) and the band; the money dance; who pays; registries and gifts; elopement; the wedding chapels of Las Vegas and Reno; *bodas colectivas* (mass civil weddings held by municipalities);
  - forms of union: civil marriage; common-law partnership (Canadian federal law after a year of living together; British Columbia's Family Law Act after two years in a marriage-like relationship *check*); Mexico's *unión libre* and *concubinato*; domestic partnership (California, 1999); same-sex marriage (*Barbeau*, British Columbia, 2003; Canada's Civil Marriage Act, 2005; Washington's Referendum 74, 2012; California's Proposition 8, 2008, and *Obergefell*, 2015; Mexico City, 2009; Baja California Sur, 2019 *check*); polygamy (Bountiful, British Columbia, and the 2011 reference case that upheld the ban);
  - age at marriage and the end of child marriage (Mexico's federal ban below 18, 2019; Washington's ban, 2024 *check*; states and provinces that still allow minors to marry with consent);
  - property in marriage: community property in California, Washington, Nevada and Idaho, a Spanish civil-law inheritance, against Oregon's equitable division (a seam to research); Mexico's choice at marriage between *sociedad conyugal* and *separación de bienes*; British Columbia's equal division of family property;
  - marriage across lines: *Perez v. Sharp* (California, 1948), the first state high court ruling against a ban on interracial marriage; Oregon's repeal of its ban (1951); fur-trade marriages *à la façon du pays* (Van Kirk, *Many Tender Ties*, 1980); picture brides; war brides; the "bachelor societies" left by Chinese exclusion; intermarriage rates (Pew; ACS);
  - divorce: California's Family Law Act (1969), the first no-fault divorce law; Canada's Divorce Act (1968, 1985); Mexico's no-fault *divorcio incausado* (Mexico City, 2008, then the states *check*); custody, support and alimony; remarriage and blended families; widowhood;
  - marriage as the nations themselves describe it: alliances between houses; rules of marrying outside one's clan in the matrilineal nations of the northern coast, as they publish them (*check* each);
  - measures: marital status and age at first marriage (ACS, county; Statistics Canada census; INEGI census); INEGI's annual marriage and divorce statistics (state); the CDC's National Vital Statistics marriage and divorce rates (state; some states, California among them, don't report divorces *check*); Pew on marriage and cohabitation; the GSS's marital-happiness item; the World Values Survey item on marriage as an out-of-date institution; the ISSP's Family and Changing Gender Roles modules.
- **Gender ways** (Fischer's folkway for Libra; runner-up Cancer):
  - the ideal man and woman: "a real man", "a lady"; *hombría*, *machismo* and *caballerosidad* (its protective form, as scaled by Arciniega et al., 2008 *check*); *marianismo* (Stevens, 1973) and *abnegación* (Díaz-Guerrero's studies of Mexican family premises); each is a concept to test, never a trait of a people; the "Man Box" survey of young men in the U.S., the U.K. and Mexico (Promundo, 2017);
  - who does what: housework, care and paid work by sex (the American Time Use Survey; Statistics Canada's time-use survey; INEGI's ENUT); women's labor-force participation (BLS; Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey; INEGI's ENOE); the pay gap; "the second shift" (Arlie Hochschild, 1989);
  - attitudes: the GSS item on whether it is better if the man achieves outside the home and the woman keeps the home; World Values Survey items on men as political leaders and on men's right to scarce jobs; GLOBE's gender egalitarianism; Hofstede's masculinity;
  - power: women's suffrage (Washington Territory, 1883, then lost; Washington, 1910; California, 1911; Oregon, 1912; British Columbia, 1917; Mexico, 1953); Mexico's 2019 constitutional reform for gender parity in every office, and its first woman president (2024); the Oregon Donation Land Claim Act (1850), which let married women hold land in their own names and limited claims by race;
  - violence: intimate-partner violence surveys (the CDC's NISVS; INEGI's ENDIREH; Statistics Canada's Survey of Safety in Public and Private Spaces, 2018); *feminicidio* as a federal crime (2012); the murders of women in Ciudad Juárez; Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (Canada's National Inquiry, 2019) and the Highway of Tears in northern British Columbia;
  - gender as the nations describe it: matrilineal descent on the northern coast; women's authority in governance; Two-Spirit people (the term adopted at a 1990 gathering in Winnipeg); each nation in its own words;
  - men's lives: men's suicide and "deaths of despair" (CDC); dangerous work (BLS); fathers' time with children;
  - live debates: transgender rights (British Columbia's SOGI 123 school resources, 2016); gender-neutral language (*Latinx*, *Latine*, *lenguaje incluyente*; Pew found few Hispanic adults used "Latinx", 2020).
- **Order ways** (runner-up; home sign Capricorn): the courts, punishment and dispute settlement are researched here under justice; policing and what the culture fears as disorder are Capricorn's (see `10 - Capricorn.md`).

## 1. Justice

**Working definition** (settled under Libra by the author, 2026-11-02): giving each person and group their due: how wrongs are judged and answered, and how rights, goods and burdens are rightly ordered in a society.
**Facets:** retributive (punishment that fits the wrong); restorative (repairing harm and relationships); procedural (due process, the fair trial); corrective (compensation for harm); distributive and social justice; historical justice (redress, reparations, apologies, truth commissions); divine justice; self-help justice (vigilantes, lynching, feud); justice for groups (land, treaty and language rights).
**Neighbors and opposites:** righteousness, law, rights, desert, equity, accountability; mercy (its rival); injustice, impunity, corruption, lawlessness. **Excess:** vengeance, rigid legalism, zeal that wears opponents down. **Deficiency:** impunity, indifference, cynicism ("there's no justice").


**Law:** the letter of the law is researched in this sign's section 0; justice draws on it, and on the spirit of the law in Capricorn's law (`10 - Capricorn.md`, section 8).
- **Words:** "justice", "injustice", "due process", "rights", "payback", "get what's coming to you", "do the time", "paid his debt to society", "law and order", "social justice", "justice for [a name]", "no justice, no peace"; Spanish *justicia*, *hacer justicia*, *justicia por propia mano* (taking justice into one's own hands), *impunidad* (a central word of Mexican public life), *el que la hace la paga* (whoever does it pays), *derecho* (one word for "law" and for "a right"), *ajusticiar* (to execute: literally, to bring to justice), *fuero* (officials' immunity from prosecution), *amparo* (the Mexican writ that protects rights), *castigo*, *la ley del más fuerte*; Navajo *hózhó* and *k'é*, as the Navajo Nation's courts publish them (an example from elsewhere of justice framed as restored relations *check*); the iconography: Lady Justice's scales, sword and blindfold.
- **Beliefs and standing:** What is justice for: punishment, deterrence, repair, restored relations or the community's peace? Retributive against restorative leanings; belief in a just world (Lerner, 1980) against fatalism; divine justice (*Dios tarda pero no olvida*: God is slow but does not forget; the Last Judgment); justice against mercy (the prodigal son; the Book of Mormon's teaching on justice and mercy in Alma 42); Catholic social teaching (*Rerum Novarum*, 1891); Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971) against Nozick (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974); Juárez's maxim that respect for the rights of others is peace; Zapata's *Tierra y libertad*; Indigenous teachings of justice as restored relations, as the nations state them; whether the law is just (Thoreau; King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail", 1963); who has the right to punish: the state, the community, the victim's family, God.
- **Feeling and the body:** indignation and moral outrage; the satisfaction of seeing a wrong punished (de Quervain et al., 2004, on the brain's reward from punishing cheats); victims' wish for "closure"; the body in court (standing for the judge, the oath, the robe, handcuffs, the "perp walk"); bodily punishments past and present (flogging at the missions; execution methods and their debates); injustice felt as stress and illness (David Williams's Everyday Discrimination Scale).
- **Everyday practice:** calling the police or not; reporting crimes (the share never reported: the National Crime Victimization Survey; INEGI's ENVIPE and its *cifra negra*, the dark figure of crimes never reported or investigated); jury duty and who shows up; small claims; neighborhood watch and Nextdoor; online call-outs; street justice; lynching in Mexico (*linchamientos*, such as Acatlán, Puebla, 2018), and *Fuenteovejuna* as a byword for a whole village acting together (from Lope de Vega's play); armed community police and self-defense groups (Guerrero and Michoacán, 2013, elsewhere); restorative circles in schools; children's games and the referee.
- **Life course:** children's sense of justice (Piaget, *The Moral Judgment of the Child*, 1932: "immanent justice"); school discipline (zero tolerance; suspensions by race in the U.S. Civil Rights Data Collection; Oakland Unified's restorative-justice program); mock trial and youth courts; the age of criminal responsibility (12 in Canada; 12 in Mexico's 2016 adolescent justice law *check*; 12 in California since 2019 *check*); juvenile courts; old age (elder-abuse law); how a life's justice is judged at its end (obituaries; posthumous pardons; exonerations).
- **Ritual:** the trial as ritual ("All rise"; the oath on a Bible or an affirmation; sentencing and victim-impact statements); executions and their witnesses; pardons and clemency (governors; Mexico's Amnesty Law, 2020); Law Day (May 1, U.S.); Mexico's *Día del Abogado* (July 12); Juneteenth; Canada's National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (September 30, since 2021) and Orange Shirt Day; vigils and marches ("Justice for ..."); memorials to victims of injustice (the National Memorial for Peace and Justice, Montgomery, 2018, elsewhere); sentencing and healing circles, as the nations share them.
- **Institutions:** courts and who staffs them: elected judges in Washington, Oregon and California, and the recall of a judge (Aaron Persky, Santa Clara County, 2018); appointed judges in British Columbia; Mexico's 2024 reform and its first judicial elections (2025); juries (in the U.S. and Canada; none in Mexico); elected prosecutors and their recalls (Chesa Boudin, San Francisco, 2022); public defenders (*Gideon*, 1963) and legal aid (Legal Aid BC; Mexico's public defenders); police oversight (Seattle's consent decree, 2012; Portland's settlement with the Justice Department, 2014); prisons (California's CDCR; Correctional Service Canada; Mexico's CERESOs); the *Gladue* principles in Canadian sentencing (1999); the First Nations Court in British Columbia (New Westminster, 2006) and the BC First Nations Justice Strategy (2020); tribal courts and police; Mexico's human-rights commissions (the CNDH and the states'); truth commissions (Canada's TRC, 2008-2015; Mexico's commission on Ayotzinapa; its commission on the state violence of 1965-1990 *check*); redress (the Civil Liberties Act of 1988 for Japanese-American internment; the Japanese Canadian Redress Agreement, 1988; Canada's head-tax apology, 2006; the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement, 2006; California's Reparations Task Force, 2020-2023).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** honor for those who "do the right thing" (whistleblower awards; judges' reputations); punishment itself: fines, prison, the death penalty (abolished in Canada, 1976, and Mexico, 2005; struck down in Washington, 2018, and repealed there, 2023; moratoriums in Oregon, 2011, and California, 2019); shaming sentences; banishment (two Tlingit youths sent to islands by a tribal panel, 1994 *check*); the sanction on the vigilante; the sanction on seeking too much justice ("vindictive", "litigious"); redemption: parole, expungement, pardon, "paid his debt".
- **Expression:** proverbs (*Dios tarda pero no olvida*; "the wheels of justice grind slow"; "justice delayed is justice denied"); courtroom drama (*Perry Mason*, set in Los Angeles; *12 Angry Men*); Westerns: the sheriff, the posse, the hanging tree; avengers of injustice (Joaquín Murrieta in Gold Rush California; Zorro, 1919); *corridos* of injustice; *Fuenteovejuna*; murals (José Clemente Orozco's murals on justice in Mexico's Supreme Court, 1941; Chicano murals); "Equal Justice Under Law" over the U.S. Supreme Court; protest music ("Fuck tha Police", N.W.A., Compton, 1988); true-crime podcasts and wrongful-conviction stories; justice in the nations' own stories, as they share them.
- **Exemplars:** Thurgood Marshall; Fred Korematsu, Gordon Hirabayashi (Seattle) and Minoru Yasui (Oregon), whose wartime convictions were overturned in the 1980s; Billy Frank Jr. (Nisqually) and the fish wars; Frank Calder (Nisga'a) and *Calder* (1973), the first Canadian ruling that Aboriginal title existed; Chief Joseph (Nez Perce) asking in Washington for one law for all (1879); Benito Juárez; Murray Sinclair (the TRC); contested figures: Earl Warren (who backed internment as California's attorney general, then led the Supreme Court); Matthew Begbie, British Columbia's "hanging judge", who tried the Tsilhqot'in chiefs; the San Francisco Vigilance Committees (1851, 1856); Joaquín Murrieta (bandit or avenger).
- **History:** Native law before contact, as the nations describe it (compensation for wrongs among the peoples of northwestern California appears in older ethnographies, which are contested); Spanish colonial justice (the *audiencias*; the *Juzgado General de Indios*, 1592: Borah, *Justice by Insurance*, 1983); mission discipline; Gold Rush miners' courts and lynch law; *People v. Hall* (1854), which barred Chinese testimony against whites; the Tsilhqot'in War (1864), the hanging of the chiefs, and their exoneration (2018); the Modoc War (1873); internment (1942) and its redress; the Sleepy Lagoon trial (1942); the Watts uprising (1965) and Los Angeles (1992); *Boldt* (1974), *Sparrow* (1990), *Delgamuukw* (1997) and *Tsilhqot'in* (2014); the occupation of the Malheur refuge over federal land and two ranchers' sentences (Oregon, 2016); in Mexico, the Revolution's promises, Tlatelolco (1968), the Dirty War, the move to oral trials (2008-2016), Ayotzinapa (2014) and the judicial reform (2024).
- **Measures:** the World Justice Project's Rule of Law Index (country) and its Mexico States Rule of Law Index (state); Gallup's confidence in the criminal justice system and the Supreme Court; the GSS items on whether local courts are too harsh and on the death penalty (Census division); Pew on criminal justice; Statistics Canada's victimization survey and confidence in courts (province); INEGI's ENVIPE and ENCIG (state) and its censuses of state justice and prisons; the AmericasBarometer items on approving of people taking justice into their own hands and on whether authorities should always respect the law to catch criminals (country *check*); the Global Preferences Survey's negative reciprocity (willingness to punish unfairness, country); the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-2 splits fairness into equality and proportionality; Atari et al., 2023); BJS and the Vera Institute's incarceration data (state, county); the World Prison Brief (country); the Death Penalty Information Center; the National Registry of Exonerations; the author's correctional-control share.
- **Variation:** race and ethnicity in trust of police and courts (as measured, a question for each unit); class; victims' and offenders' families; urban and rural; generation; party (California's Propositions 47, 2014, and 36, 2024, which cut and then raised penalties); the Left Coast's reform politics against the interior's; each Indigenous nation's own justice tradition and its courts.
- **Relations:** fairness and impartiality (this sign); law and authority (Capricorn); crime and compassion (Pisces); revenge and trust (Scorpio); anger at injustice (Aries); equality (Aquarius); truth (Sagittarius).
- **Who it applies to:** strangers, migrants and enemies (immigration courts; fair trials for enemies); animals (cruelty law); rivers and land (the Yurok Tribe's declaration of the Klamath River's rights, 2019); future generations (*Juliana v. United States*, filed in Oregon, 2015); corporations; the dead (exonerations, posthumous pardons); machines: present-day attitudes to risk-assessment algorithms in bail and sentencing, and to who is liable when a self-driving car kills (Tempe, Arizona, 2018).
- **Silences:** wrongful convictions; the lynchings of Mexicans and Chinese in the West (Carrigan and Webb, *Forgotten Dead*, 2013); injustices done by "our side"; sexual violence never reported; deaths in custody; wrongs inside families.
- **Change:** restorative justice spreading; bail reform; "defund the police" (2020) and the backlash (recalls; Proposition 36); Mexico's elected judiciary; reconciliation and its critics in Canada; AI in courts.

**Key questions:** Is justice mainly punishing the wrongdoer, repairing the harm, or restoring the relationship? Who may deliver it: the state, the community, the family, the victim or God?

## 2. Fairness

**Working definition:** even-handed treatment in everyday dealings: getting one's due share, taking turns, and being held to the same rules as everyone else.
**Facets:** equal shares; shares by merit or contribution (equity); shares by need (Deutsch, 1975, on the three); fair process (Tyler's procedural justice); fair play in games and sport; fair prices, wages and deals; fair turns (queues); fairness among siblings and heirs; fair rules (a level playing field against a rigged system); fairness to future generations.
**Neighbors and opposites:** equity, equality, even-handedness, square dealing, sportsmanship; unfairness, cheating, favoritism, rigging, taking advantage. **Excess:** scorekeeping over every crumb; litigiousness; inability to decide anything that might come out unequal. **Deficiency:** tolerance of cheating; "life isn't fair" as resignation.

- **Words:** "fair", "unfair", "no fair!", "fair and square", "fair share", "fair play", "fair game", "fair shake", "fair enough", "square deal" (Theodore Roosevelt), "level playing field", "rigged", "gaming the system", "cutting in line", "cheater"; English "fair" has no exact equivalent in many languages (Wierzbicka, *English: Meaning and Culture*, 2006); Spanish splits it: *justo*, *equitativo*, *parejo* (even, the same for all *check*), *juego limpio*, *no se vale* (Mexican: that's not fair, that's not allowed), *ventaja* and *ventajoso* (taking unfair advantage), *gandalla* and *agandallar* (Mexican: a grabber; to grab unfairly), *trampa*, *tramposo*, *transa* (Mexican: a swindle), *palancas* (connections: "levers").
- **Beliefs and standing:** Is fair equal shares, shares by merit, or shares by need? - **Change:** pay transparency; ban-the-box and fair-chance hiring; algorithmic fairness; inequality and "fair share" taxation debates; Mexico's 2021 outsourcing and profit-sharing reform.

**Key questions:** Is fair equal shares, shares by merit, or shares by need? Is the culture more upset by unequal outcomes or by unequal rules?

## 3. Partnership

**Working definition:** a committed bond between two or more parties who share aims, risks and returns as (more or less) equals: in love and marriage, in work and business, and between peoples.
**Facets:** the couple (romantic and married partnership; marriage ways are in section 0); co-parenting; business partnership and co-founding; work partners (patrol partners, fishing crews, climbing and dance partners); ritual kin (*compadrazgo*); alliances between families, nations and governments (treaties, compacts, "nation-to-nation" relations); public-private partnerships; equality inside the bond (who leads, who decides).
**Neighbors and opposites:** union, alliance, companionship, cooperation, teamwork, collaboration; solitude, going it alone, rivalry, the open enemy (the traditional areas pair partner and enemy), domination. **Excess:** fusion and codependence; losing oneself in the other; treating a partner as a possession. **Deficiency:** inability to commit or to share.

- **Words:** "partner" (spreading for married, unmarried and same-sex couples), "spouse", "better half", "significant other", "teammate", "wingman", "pardner" (Western), "partners in crime", "silent partner", "power couple", "the old ball and chain" (a joke that sanctions marriage); Spanish *pareja*, *socio* (business partner; also a club member), *compañero* and *compañera* (companion, comrade, life partner), *media naranja* (one's other half: literally half an orange), *mi viejo*, *mi vieja* (Mexican: my spouse), *arrejuntarse* (Mexican: to move in together unmarried), *compadre*, *comadre*, *carnal* (Mexican: a close buddy), *mancuerna* (a pair who work well together), *yunta* (a yoke of oxen; a partner *check*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** Is marriage a contract, a covenant or a sacrament? Companionate against institutional marriage (Coontz, *Marriage, a History*, 2005; Cherlin); "the two shall become one flesh"; the husband as head of the household (complementarian teaching) against equal partnership (egalitarian teaching); eternal marriage in Latter-day Saint temples; the soulmate ideal; partnership as life's purpose against solo achievement; "never go into business with friends or family" against the family firm; *compadrazgo* as a sacred tie; alliances as kinship, as the nations describe them; "nation-to-nation" (Canada) and "government-to-government" (U.S.) as official ideals of partnership with Indigenous nations.
- **Feeling and the body:** love, attachment, companionship; public displays of affection and their limits; touch (holding hands; the *abrazo*); dancing in pairs (*cumbia*, *quebradita*, the two-step, square dancing); the grief of breakup, divorce and widowhood; loneliness; working in step (the two-man crosscut saw; rowing; roped climbing). Sexual partnership is Scorpio's (sex ways).
- **Everyday practice:** dating and apps; who pays on dates; moving in together; dividing housework; joint or separate bank accounts; business partnerships, co-founders and LLCs; family businesses; fishing and logging crews; police partners; carpools; co-parenting after divorce; *compadres* at baptisms, first communions, *quinceañeras* and weddings; the fishing cooperatives of Baja California Sur's Pacific coast (lobster and abalone *cooperativas*).

- **Ritual:** weddings (section 0); engagement parties; anniversaries and vow renewals; *bodas colectivas*; Valentine's Day and Mexico's *Día del Amor y la Amistad*; baptisms that make *compadres*; signing ceremonies for deals, compacts and treaties; treaty days (the Tulalip Tribes' Treaty Days *check*).
- **Institutions:** marriage law; domestic partnership (California, 1999; Washington and Oregon, 2007); common-law status (Canada); Mexico City's *sociedad de convivencia* (2006); partnership and company law (partnerships and LLCs; Mexico's *sociedad anónima* and *sociedad cooperativa*); cooperatives (REI; credit unions; fishing cooperatives); tribal-state compacts (gaming compacts; Washington's Centennial Accord, 1989); co-management (salmon co-managed with the treaty tribes after *Boldt*, through the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission, 1974; the Gwaii Haanas Agreement between the Haida Nation and Canada, 1993); cross-border partnerships (the Pacific Coast Collaborative of California, Oregon, Washington and British Columbia *check*; the Cascadia Innovation Corridor, 2016; California's cap-and-trade link with Quebec, 2014); sister cities.

- **Change:** later and fewer marriages; cohabitation rising; same-sex marriage; dating apps; living apart together; "partner" replacing "husband" and "wife"; AI companions.

**Key questions:** Is a couple two equals, a head and a helper, or the joining of two families? Is a partnership a contract one may leave or a bond one may not?

## 4. Balance

**Working definition:** the even weighting of two or more forces, parties or demands so that none overwhelms the others; and the steadiness that results, in a person, a household, a society or the land.
**Facets:** balance in a life (work and rest, work and family, moderation); balance in the body (health as balance: hot and cold in Mexican folk medicine, the humoral legacy); balance between partners (give and take); balance of powers (checks and balances, federalism, divided government); balance in judgment and reporting (weighing evidence; "both sides"); balance between cultures (biculturalism); balance in nature (ecological balance; the nations' teachings, as they share them); balance in accounts (balanced budgets, the ledger); balance as form (symmetry in art and building).
**Neighbors and opposites:** equilibrium, harmony, moderation, proportion, the golden mean, temperance, steadiness; imbalance, excess, extremism, burnout, lopsidedness, polarization. **Excess:** fence-sitting; false balance (treating unequal claims as equal); stasis. **Deficiency:** extremism; burnout; one side swallowing the other.

- **Words:** "balance", "work-life balance", "keep your balance", "find your center", "grounded", "everything in moderation", "checks and balances", "balanced budget", "balance the books", "fair and balanced" (Fox News's slogan, 1996-2017), "both sides", "off-kilter", "out of whack", "burned out"; Spanish *equilibrio*, *balance* (in accounting), *armonía*, *término medio*, *ni tanto que queme al santo ni tanto que no lo alumbre* (not so close the candle burns the saint, not so far it leaves him dark), *ni muy muy ni tan tan* (neither too much nor too little), *pesos y contrapesos* (checks and balances), *estar en sus cabales*; the folk-medicine pair *frío* and *caliente*; Nuu-chah-nulth *hishuk ish tsawalk* ("everything is one"), as the Nuu-chah-nulth publish it (*check*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** Is balance a virtue in itself (Aristotle's mean; temperance among the cardinal virtues) or a dodge (the lukewarm church of Revelation 3:16; zeal praised over moderation)? Health as balance: the humoral medicine of Spain and New Spain and its legacy in Mexican folk medicine (Foster, *Hippocrates' Latin American Legacy*, 1994); yin and yang in Chinese medicine in the pilot's Chinatowns; balance in Indigenous teachings, as each nation states them (the medicine wheel is a Plains teaching that many, not all, nations use); Madison's *Federalist* 51 (ambition set against ambition); the "balance of nature" against newer ecology's stress on disturbance; balance as the goal of wellness culture.
- **Feeling and the body:** hot and cold foods and illnesses (*empacho*, *susto*, *mal aire*), and *la cuarentena*, the forty days of rest after giving birth; dietary guides that picture balance (MyPlate, 2011; Canada's Food Guide, 2019; Mexico's *Plato del Bien Comer*); sleep and rest; stress and burnout (burnout entered the WHO's ICD-11 in 2019); feeling "centered"; physical balance in work and sport (logrolling at loggers' sports; surfing).
- **Everyday practice:** juggling work and family; how much vacation people get and take (no legal minimum in the U.S.; two weeks in most of Canada; Mexico doubled its minimum to 12 days in 2023); four-day weeks; balancing the checkbook; dividing holidays between in-laws; outdoor recreation as restoring balance; living between two cultures and two languages; ticket-splitting in voting; "both sides" journalism.
- **Life course:** teaching moderation (screen-time limits; "everything in moderation"); school and sports against rest; postpartum rest (*la cuarentena*); the midlife crisis as imbalance; retirement; elders as keepers of balance, as the nations describe them; the end of life judged as a balanced or a driven life.
- **Ritual:** the equinoxes (crowds at Teotihuacan on the spring equinox; Chichén Itzá's equinox shadow, elsewhere); solstice gatherings; *limpias* (cleansings) in *curanderismo*; ceremonies to restore balance, as the nations share them; sabbaths and days of rest; New Year's resolutions.
- **Institutions:** the separation of powers; two-chamber legislatures; the Senate filibuster; divided government; federalism (state and federal; province and Ottawa); Mexico's autonomous constitutional bodies (the INE; the INAI, dissolved in 2025 *check*) as checks; balanced-budget rules (most U.S. states); the U.S. Fairness Doctrine in broadcasting (1949-1987) and the Canadian Broadcasting Act's call for balance; hours-of-work law (California's daily overtime after eight hours; Mexico's 2021 telework law and its right to disconnect); wildlife management (predator control; wolves returning to Washington and Oregon); ecological restoration (the Elwha dam removals, 2011-2014; the Klamath dam removals, 2023-2024); the Northwest Forest Plan (1994), meant to balance timber and owls.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the "well-rounded"; the sanction on the workaholic and on the slacker; "extremist" and "radical" as slurs; and the reverse: "fence-sitter", "wishy-washy", *tibio* (lukewarm) as slurs on too much balance; burnout's cost in health and careers.
- **Expression:** proverbs (above); the scales of Lady Justice; formline design on the Northwest Coast, with its rules of symmetry and form (Bill Holm, *Northwest Coast Indian Art: An Analysis of Form*, 1965, and the artists' own accounts); mobiles (Calder); self-help ("sharpen the saw" in Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, 1989); "work hard, play hard"; Steinbeck and Ricketts's *Sea of Cortez* (1941), with its whole-system view of the Gulf of California; nature writing (Muir; Leopold's "land ethic", 1949; Carson's *Silent Spring*, 1962).
- **Exemplars:** political moderates and centrists; elders; John Muir (the Sierra Club, 1892); Ed Ricketts; counter-exemplars: the workaholic founder, the extremist.
- **History:** humoral medicine in New Spain; mission agriculture and grazing, and the upset of land and water; the salmon decline; the Columbia dams (Grand Coulee, 1942; Celilo Falls drowned by The Dalles Dam, 1957, a loss to the Plateau peoples); Progressive-era checks on railroad power; Canadian federalism's shifting balance; Mexico's long single-party rule, the alternation of 2000, and the debate over checks and balances since 2018; the timber wars and the spotted owl (1990s); the sustainability movement.
- **Measures:** work-life balance (the OECD Better Life Index, country; Mexico's long hours in OECD data *check*); hours worked and leisure time (ATUS by state; Statistics Canada's time use; INEGI's ENUT); vacation days taken (U.S. Travel Association, practitioner *check*); stress and burnout (Gallup; the Gallup-Sharecare Well-Being Index by state *check*); split-ticket voting and divided government (election returns by county); constraints on the executive (V-Dem, country; the World Justice Project's "constraints on government powers", country and Mexican state); self-placement at the center of the left-right scale (World Values Survey; GSS); folk-medicine use among Mexican-Americans (surveys *check*).

- **Relations:** justice (the scales), reciprocity (the balance of exchange), indecision (balance as paralysis), peace at any price (balance as avoidance), diplomacy (this sign); stability (Taurus); health (Virgo); order (Capricorn); escapism (Pisces); recklessness (Aries).

- **Silences:** whose lives are thrown out of balance so others can have balance (care workers, farmworkers, night shifts); the cost of "balanced" coverage when one side is wrong; burnout among the poor.
- **Change:** the wellness boom; remote work blurring home and work; the four-day week; polarization emptying the political center; dam removals and restoration; climate change described as a world out of balance.

**Key questions:** Is balance a virtue in itself or a dodge? What must be kept in balance here: the body, the household, the powers of government, or people's relations with the land?

## 5. Impartiality

**Working definition:** judging or treating people without favoring anyone for who they are or how they are tied to the judge: no favorites, no prejudice, no special treatment for rank, wealth, kin or friendship.
**Facets:** the impartial judge, referee, examiner and official; nonpartisanship (civil service, election officials, public broadcasters); neutrality (taking no side); universal rules against personal ties (Trompenaars); blind procedures (blind auditions, anonymous grading, selection by lot); conflict of interest and recusal; nepotism and cronyism; equality before the law for the powerful; journalistic objectivity.
**Neighbors and opposites:** neutrality, objectivity, even-handedness, disinterestedness, nonpartisanship, the rule of law; favoritism, partiality, nepotism, cronyism, bias, prejudice, conflict of interest; loyalty (a rival virtue). **Excess:** coldness to one's own; "nobody's ally"; bureaucratic rigidity. **Deficiency:** favoritism, tribalism, corruption.

- **Words:** "impartial", "unbiased", "neutral", "nonpartisan", "objective", "no favorites", "conflict of interest", "recuse", "cronyism", "nepotism", "old boys' network", "it's who you know", "the fix is in", "teacher's pet", "Do you know who I am?"; Spanish *imparcial*, *parcial*, *palancas* (connections), *influyentismo* (Mexican: influence-peddling), *tráfico de influencias* (the legal term), *amiguismo* and *cuatismo* (cronyism), *nepotismo*, *compadrazgo* in its sense of cronyism, *charolazo* (Mexican: flashing an official badge for special treatment), *¿No sabe usted con quién está hablando?* (Don't you know who you're talking to?; DaMatta's study of the Brazilian version, elsewhere), the "Lady" and "Lord" nicknames for entitled people on Mexican social media (#LadyProfeco, 2013), *mirrey* (a flashy rich kid), *para el refresco* (a small bribe: "for a soda"); the saying attributed to Juárez and others, "for my friends, justice and grace; for my enemies, the law alone" (*check* attribution); Chinese *guanxi* (connections) in the pilot's Chinese communities.
- **Beliefs and standing:** Must a good person favor family and friends, or treat everyone alike? Trompenaars' dilemma of the friend who hit a pedestrian (would you testify falsely for him?) and its older form (Stouffer and Toby, 1951); "God is no respecter of persons" (Acts 10:34) and "do not show partiality" (Leviticus 19:15); Adam Smith's impartial spectator (1759); Rawls's veil of ignorance; Weber's bureaucracy; whether journalists can be objective; Indigenous governance where kin and rank structure office (hereditary chiefs), and how each nation describes fairness within it; loyalty as the higher virtue in some settings (family, clan, gang, party).
- **Feeling and the body:** the blindfold of Lady Justice; the judge's and the referee's poker face; a calm, detached bearing as a sign of fairness; rage at a biased referee; embarrassment at being seen to favor one's own child; the warmth of favoring one's own.
- **Everyday practice:** jobs through family and friends ("networking"; *palancas*); coaches and teachers with their own children on the team or in the class; parents favoring a child; landlords choosing tenants; traffic stops and who is let off; "Do you know who I am?" incidents; recusal in small towns where everyone knows everyone; jury selection; anonymous grading; blind orchestra auditions (Goldin and Rouse, 2000); paying a fixer to jump the line at government offices (*coyote*, *gestor*); small bribes to traffic police (*mordida*).
- **Life course:** "I love you all the same"; sibling favoritism; teacher's pets; admissions advantages for legacies and donors' children; the coach's kid; the first job through family; wills that favor one child; professional ethics training.
- **Ritual:** the judge's oath; the referee's coin toss; drawing lots: jury summonses; citizens' assemblies chosen by lot (British Columbia's Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform, 2004); Mexico's *insaculación*, the lottery that picks the citizens who run polling stations, and the 2025 *tómbola* that picked judicial candidates (*check*); blind draws in tournaments; the swearing-in of election officials; party observers at the count.
- **Institutions:** judicial conduct codes and recusal; how judges are chosen (appointment through judicial councils in British Columbia; elections in Washington, Oregon and California; Mexico's 2024 change to elected judges); civil-service merit systems (the Pendleton Act, 1883; Canada's Public Service Employment Act; Mexico's professional career service, 2003); nepotism and conflict-of-interest rules and their watchdogs (Washington's Public Disclosure Commission, 1972; California's Fair Political Practices Commission, 1974; British Columbia's Conflict of Interest Commissioner); independent election bodies (Elections BC; Elections Canada; Mexico's IFE, 1990, autonomous from 1996, now the INE); nonpartisan local offices (most California city and county offices) and top-two primaries (Washington, 2004; California, 2010); public broadcasters' impartiality rules (the CBC; NPR); press ethics codes (the Society of Professional Journalists); peer review; ombudsmen (the BC Ombudsperson, 1979); auditors (the Washington State Auditor; Mexico's Auditoría Superior de la Federación); Mexico's National Anticorruption System (2016).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** trust and respect for a fair judge or umpire; the sanction on favoritism: scandal, resignation, impeachment, recall; "rigged" as an accusation; and the reverse sanction where loyalty is expected (a traitor to one's own; *malagradecido*, ungrateful; "nobody's ally").
- **Expression:** Lady Justice's blindfold; *12 Angry Men*; "kill the umpire"; the Mexican film *La ley de Herodes* (1999), a satire of local corruption under one-party rule; *mordida* jokes; the "Lady" nicknames; Judge Bao in Chinese opera, elsewhere; King Solomon's judgment.
- **Exemplars:** officials who refused favors; Elliot Richardson and William Ruckelshaus, who resigned rather than fire the Watergate prosecutor (1973); José Woldenberg, first president of the autonomous IFE (1996-2003); Jody Wilson-Raybould (We Wai Kai), whose clash with the prime minister's office over a prosecution (the SNC-Lavalin affair, 2019) became a test of prosecutorial independence, and is contested; umpires and referees known for fairness; counter-exemplars: political bosses and fixers.
- **History:** the Southern Pacific's political machine in California and Hiram Johnson's campaign against it (1910); Progressive reforms (civil service, nonpartisan local government, direct democracy); the Pacific Scandal (1873), campaign money traded for the charter of the railway promised to British Columbia; in Mexico, the *dedazo* (the outgoing president choosing his successor), the 1988 election and its "system crash", the IFE's independence (1996), and the *Casa Blanca* scandal (2014); Watergate; the Wet'suwet'en hereditary chiefs and elected councils in the Coastal GasLink dispute (2020), and which authority outsiders recognized.
- **Measures:** Trompenaars' universalism and particularism (country); the Quality of Government Institute's expert survey on impartial public administration (country; Rothstein and Teorell, 2008, define good government as impartiality); V-Dem's "rigorous and impartial public administration" (country); Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index and Global Corruption Barometer (country); INEGI's ENCIG (corruption experienced in dealing with officials, state); the AmericasBarometer's items on being asked for a bribe (country *check*); confidence in courts (Gallup; Pew; the GSS; Statistics Canada); the World Justice Project's factors on impartial justice (country; Mexican state); hiring audit studies (Pager, 2003, and Kline, Rose and Walters, 2022, in the U.S.; Arceo-Gómez and Campos-Vázquez, 2014, in Mexico).
- **Variation:** small towns against cities; immigrant and ethnic networks; class (who has connections to use); generation; party (trust in judges); profession; the hereditary and elected systems inside many First Nations.
- **Relations:** justice and fairness (this sign); diplomacy (the neutral broker); loyalty and trust (Scorpio); law and hierarchy (Capricorn); equality and community (Aquarius); corruption (a candidate concept).
- **Who it applies to:** the powerful (nobody above the law: Mexico's *fuero*; presidential immunity, *Trump v. - **Silences:** the favoritism people practice themselves; "networking" as accepted favoritism; legacy admissions; favoring one's own group; small bribes treated as normal.
- **Change:** blind hiring; diversity programs and their backlash; anti-nepotism rules; falling trust in courts; Mexico's elected judiciary; automated decisions.

**Key questions:** Must a good person favor family and friends, or treat everyone alike? Does the culture expect its officials to be impartial, and does it believe they are?

## 6. Diplomacy

**Working definition:** managing relations and disagreements between parties by talk, tact and negotiation rather than force: between nations and peoples, and in everyday social life.
**Facets:** diplomacy between states; between Indigenous nations, and between them and settler governments (treaty-making, protocol); go-betweens and mediators; negotiation in business and labor; tact and politeness in daily life; persuasion and coalition-building; protocol, ceremony and gifts; family diplomacy (in-laws, holidays); speaking for others (interpreters; children who translate for their parents).
**Neighbors and opposites:** tact, negotiation, mediation, persuasion, compromise, protocol, courtesy, discretion; bluntness, confrontation, coercion, force, war, the ultimatum. **Excess:** two-facedness, evasion, "diplomatic" answers that say nothing, flattery. **Deficiency:** tactlessness, bluntness passed off as honesty, aggression.

- **Words:** "diplomatic", "tactful", "smooth things over", "read the room", "agree to disagree", "pick your battles", "white lie", "walk on eggshells", "sugarcoat", "win-win", "getting to yes" (Fisher and Ury, 1981), "statesmanlike", "the art of the deal"; Spanish *diplomacia*, *tacto*, *mano izquierda* (skill in handling people: literally "left hand"), *quedar bien* (to come off well, to please), *dorar la píldora* (to gild the pill), *dar el avión* (Mexican: to humor someone, to pretend to agree), *dar largas* (to put off), *ahorita* (Mexican: "right now", which can politely mean later or never), *con todo respeto* (said before disagreeing), *hablando se entiende la gente* (people understand each other by talking), *cantinflear* (to talk at length and say nothing, after the comedian Cantinflas), *lambiscón* and *barbero* (Mexican: a bootlicker, a flatterer); Chinook Jargon, the trade language of the Northwest, as a tool of diplomacy and treaty-making, and its limits; Filipino *pakikisama* (getting along with the group), in the pilot's Filipino communities.
- **Beliefs and standing:** Is tact a courtesy or a lie? - **Feeling and the body:** composure and the poker face; the smile; handshakes, the *abrazo*, the greeting kiss; eye contact; personal space; holding one's tongue; the strain of keeping the peace ("emotional labor": Arlie Hochschild, *The Managed Heart*, 1983); awkwardness after a gaffe.
- **Everyday practice:** declining invitations without saying no; white lies; managing in-laws; neighbor and HOA disputes; office politics and "managing up"; customer-service scripts; salary negotiation; holiday-table rules ("no politics or religion"); bringing a gift to a host; land acknowledgments at meetings (common in British Columbia and Washington); children translating for immigrant parents ("language brokering"); online moderation.
- **Life course:** "use your words"; "if you can't say something nice, don't say anything at all" (Thumper, *Bambi*, 1942); teaching apologies and *respeto*; peer-mediation programs in schools; Model UN and debate; student government; learning to network; elders as the family's mediators.
- **Ritual:** state visits, summits and treaty signings; toasts; the Peace Arch on the Washington-British Columbia border (1921), with its "children of a common mother"; Friendship Park on the San Diego-Tijuana border (1971); protocol at Tribal Canoe Journeys (since the Paddle to Seattle, 1989), where visiting canoes ask permission to come ashore; welcoming and gift-giving protocols, feasts and the potlatch as diplomacy, as the nations share them; land acknowledgments; the signing of the UN Charter in San Francisco (1945).
- **Institutions:** foreign ministries (the State Department; Global Affairs Canada; Mexico's SRE); consulates (Mexico's large consular network in California; the *matrícula consular*); state and provincial trade offices abroad; sister cities; government-to-government agreements with tribes (Washington's Centennial Accord, 1989, and Millennium Agreement, 1999; British Columbia's Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, 2019); boundary bodies (the International Joint Commission, 1909; the International Boundary and Water Commission, and the 1944 water treaty with Mexico); the Columbia River Treaty (1964) and its renegotiation (agreement in principle, 2024; *check* its status); labor mediation (the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service; British Columbia's Labour Relations Board; Mexico's conciliation centers under the 2019 labor reform); community mediation centers; ombudsmen.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for "statesmanship"; Nobel Peace Prizes (Lester Pearson, 1957; Alfonso García Robles, 1982, for the Treaty of Tlatelolco, which made Latin America free of nuclear weapons); careers in the foreign service; the sanction on tactlessness (gaffes, "foot in mouth", exclusion); and on too much diplomacy ("two-faced", "weasel words", "a politician's answer", *hipócrita*, *lambiscón*); "appeasement" as an accusation (Munich, 1938).
- **Expression:** proverbs ("you catch more flies with honey than with vinegar"; *hablando se entiende la gente*); Dale Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (1936); *Getting to Yes*; Cantinflas's films and the verb they gave the language; political memoirs; satire of diplomats; jokes about Canadian politeness; land acknowledgments as a new genre.
- **Exemplars:** Sarah Winnemucca (Northern Paiute), interpreter, negotiator and author (*Life Among the Piutes*, 1883); Chief Seattle (Si'ahl) and the famous speech attributed to him, a later reconstruction whose wording is contested; Chief Joseph; Juan Francisco de la Bodega y Quadra and George Vancouver at Nootka (1792), who disagreed and parted friends; Matías Romero, Mexico's envoy in Washington during the French intervention; Lester Pearson; Alfonso García Robles; Benito Juárez; contested: Sacagawea's popular image.

- **Measures:** directness and conflict style (Hall, scholarly; Erin Meyer's *The Culture Map*, 2014, practitioner); GLOBE's assertiveness and humane orientation (country); politeness and hedging in regional language data (*check* for studies); cross-cultural negotiation experiments (Jeanne Brett and colleagues); surveys on avoiding political talk with family and friends (Pew *check*); counts of consulates, sister cities and trade offices (administrative); government-to-government agreements with tribes (counts by state and province); the Lowy Institute's Global Diplomacy Index (country); INEGI's ENCUCI on civic culture, including how people settle disagreements (state *check*); Weber Shandwick's *Civility in America* (practitioner).
- **Variation:** gender (women expected to smooth things over; "tone policing"); class; region (direct and indirect styles, as a question); immigrant generation and language brokering; profession (sales against engineering); polarization (refusing to talk across lines); each Indigenous nation's own protocols.
- **Relations:** peace at any price (its excess); impartiality (the neutral broker); partnership and reciprocity (this sign); mediation and communication (Gemini); honesty and truth (Sagittarius: tact against candor); power (Scorpio); authority (Capricorn); courage and self-assertion (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** foreign states; Indigenous nations as treaty partners; in-laws and neighbors; enemies (whether one talks to them at all); corporations (corporate diplomacy, lobbying); other-than-human beings, as some nations describe relations with them (as shared); machines: present-day attitudes to polite and rude chatbots, to saying "please" to voice assistants, and to AI negotiators (surveys *check*).
- **Silences:** what diplomats can't say; the lies diplomacy requires; broken treaty promises; bribery dressed as diplomacy; smiling to one's face and talking behind one's back.
- **Change:** polarization and declining civility; "radical candor" and "brutal honesty" as ideals; online incivility; land acknowledgments spreading and drawing backlash; trade wars; Mexico's and Canada's shifting relations with the U.S.

**Key questions:** Is tact a courtesy or a lie? Does the culture prefer to settle differences by talk, through a third party, or by a show of strength?

## 7. Reciprocity

**Working definition:** returning what one receives, in kind: gifts for gifts, help for help, harm for harm; the give-and-take that binds people and keeps accounts between them.
**Facets:** positive reciprocity (returning gifts and favors); negative reciprocity (returning harm, "tit for tat"; revenge itself is Scorpio's); generalized, balanced and negative reciprocity by social distance (Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics*, 1972); the gift (Mauss, *The Gift*, 1925); hospitality; favors and favor banks; mutual aid and rotating savings; reciprocity between generations (care for parents); reciprocity with the land and other beings, as the nations describe it; reciprocity with God and the saints (vows, offerings); reciprocity between states (trade, visas, treaties); quid pro quo as corruption.
**Neighbors and opposites:** give-and-take, exchange, mutuality, "returning the favor", gratitude, obligation, debt; ingratitude, freeloading, one-sided giving, exploitation; unconditional giving (the gift that asks nothing). **Excess:** scorekeeping, "nothing is free", transactional relationships, revenge spirals. **Deficiency:** freeloading, ingratitude, being used.

- **Words:** "you scratch my back, I'll scratch yours", "return the favor", "I owe you one", "what goes around comes around", "tit for tat", "pay it forward", "there's no such thing as a free lunch", "quid pro quo", "IOU", "strings attached", "the Golden Rule"; Spanish *hoy por ti, mañana por mí* (today for you, tomorrow for me), *favor con favor se paga* (a favor is paid with a favor), *una mano lava la otra* (one hand washes the other), *agradecido*, *malagradecido*, *deber un favor*, *cobrar el favor*, *echar la mano* (to give a hand), *hacer la valona* (Mexican: to do someone a favor, to cover for them *check*), *tanda* (a rotating savings club), *cooperación* and *coperacha* (Mexican: a pooled contribution), *itacate* (Mexican: food the host sends home with guests), *mano vuelta* (rural exchange labor: "a returned hand" *check*), *gorrón* (freeloader), *colado* (gatecrasher), *el que da y quita, con el diablo se desquita* (a children's rhyme against taking back a gift *check* wording); Chinook Jargon *potlatch* ("to give"), the word that spread from the Northwest Coast; Filipino *utang na loob* (a debt of gratitude), in the pilot's Filipino communities; Chinese *renqing* (the favor owed); Māori *utu* (return, of gifts and of harm; elsewhere).
- **Beliefs and standing:** the Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12); "turn the other cheek" (refusing to return harm); "it is more blessed to give than to receive"; "lend, expecting nothing in return" (Luke 6:35); the norm of reciprocity as a base of social order (Gouldner, 1960); whether any gift is free (Mauss); welfare and reciprocity (work requirements; "something for nothing"); the rights and duties of citizens; teachings of reciprocity with the land and with animals and plants, as the nations state them (Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*, 2013, a Potawatomi writer, on the "honorable harvest"); obligations to parents (*familismo*; filial piety in Chinese families); thanks owed to soldiers ("thank you for your service").
- **Feeling and the body:** gratitude; the weight of being in debt; guilt at not returning; resentment of one-sided relations; the warmth of an exchange; the discomfort of a gift too large to return; feeding guests; embraces and handshakes of thanks.
- **Everyday practice:** gifts for birthdays, holidays and weddings; thank-you notes; returning dinner invitations; potlucks and "bring a dish"; the *itacate*; tipping; *tandas* among workers and immigrants; remittances home (Banco de México's data by state); borrowing and lending between neighbors; babysitting swaps and carpools; harvest help and barn raisings; favors in business; Buy Nothing groups (founded on Bainbridge Island, Washington, 2013); the *cooperación* collected for school and street repairs; mutual aid during COVID-19; online "follow for follow" and swapped reviews.
- **Life course:** teaching children to share and say thank you; birthday gifts and goodie bags; godparents throwing coins to children after a baptism (the *bolo*); *padrinos* who take on duties for life; adult children supporting their parents; care for elders, and inheritance as its return; funerals: bringing food, pooling money (*coperacha*), attending the funerals of those who attended one's own family's.
- **Ritual:** the potlatch, banned under the Indian Act from 1884 to 1951, as the nations describe it; giveaways at powwows; Christmas gift exchange (*intercambio de regalos*, *amigo secreto*); *posadas* (the request for shelter and its welcome); offerings on *Día de Muertos* (reciprocity with the dead, as Mexican families describe it); vows to saints (*mandas*) and the ex-voto paintings (*retablos*) that thank them; the *rosca de reyes* and the tamales it obliges; Halloween's "trick or treat"; first-salmon ceremonies, as the nations share them; Thanksgiving.
- **Institutions:** contract law's "consideration" (section 0); reciprocal trade agreements and tariffs; reciprocal visa rules (Canada's visa on Mexican visitors, 2009, lifted 2016, partly reimposed 2024); mutual recognition of licenses; tax treaties; mutual-aid societies (*mutualistas*, such as the Alianza Hispano-Americana, 1894; Chinese benevolent associations, such as San Francisco's Six Companies; Japanese rotating-credit *tanomoshi*); fraternal orders; credit unions and cooperatives; time banks; social insurance framed as "earned" (Social Security; the Canada Pension Plan); gift limits for officials (where reciprocity becomes bribery); lobbying rules.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** a name as generous and grateful; being invited back; the sanction on freeloaders (*gorrón*, "moocher", dropped from the invitation list); *malagradecido* as a deep insult; favors called in; the sanction on too much scorekeeping ("transactional", "keeps score"); a cheap gift as an insult; refusing a gift as an offense.
- **Expression:** proverbs (above); O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi" (1905); Silverstein's *The Giving Tree* (1964), read as love or as exploitation; *Pay It Forward* (Hyde, 2000); the favors owed in *The Godfather*; gift objects in Northwest Coast art (coppers), as the nations describe them; *corridos* of favors betrayed.
- **Exemplars:** good neighbors and organizers of mutual aid; godparents who kept every obligation; leaders known for giving, as the nations describe them; counter-exemplars: the ingrate, the freeloader, the fair-weather friend, the politician who trades favors.
- **History:** exchange networks before contact; the fur trade's gift protocols beside its markets (the Hudson's Bay Company); the potlatch ban and resistance (Dan Cranmer's potlatch, 1921, its regalia seized and later returned, now at the U'mista Cultural Centre, Alert Bay); *mutualistas* and benevolent associations; the Reciprocity Treaty (1854) and Canada's "reciprocity election" (1911); the New Deal's social insurance; COVID-19 mutual aid; tariffs and retaliation (2018, 2025).

- **Variation:** class (survival networks among the poor: Carol Stack, *All Our Kin*, 1974, elsewhere); immigrant and ethnic networks; rural neighborliness against urban anonymity; gender (who does the "kin work" of gifts and cards: Micaela di Leonardo, 1987); generation; religion (tithing as reciprocity with God); each Indigenous nation's own system of obligation.
- **Relations:** fairness, balance, partnership, and justice (retribution as negative reciprocity) in this sign; revenge and trust (Scorpio); generosity (Leo); gratitude; community (Aquarius); possession (Taurus); compassion and self-sacrifice (Pisces: giving without return).
- **Who it applies to:** kin, neighbors and strangers (Sahlins's circles of social distance); the land, the salmon and other beings, as the nations describe it; the dead; God and the saints; the state (taxes for services; "no taxation without representation"); other nations (trade and visas); machines: people return favors to computers (Fogg and Nass, 1997), and present-day attitudes to whether one owes politeness or thanks to a chatbot or a robot.
- **Silences:** debts no one mentions; the strings on gifts; family obligations that trap; unreturned labor (of women, of immigrants); land taken with nothing returned.
- **Change:** the gig economy; online gift economies; the decline of neighboring and its revival in mutual aid; "transactional" politics; tariffs and retaliation.

**Key questions:** Must every gift be returned, and how soon? Does the culture prize keeping accounts even, or giving without counting?

## 8. Indecision

**Working definition:** difficulty or failure in reaching a decision: weighing too long, wavering, putting a choice off, or leaving it to others.
**Facets:** personal indecisiveness; putting off choices (procrastination); "analysis paralysis" and choice overload; wavering in commitments (relationships, careers); fence-sitting in politics; collective indecision (committees, consensus that never comes, gridlock); handing the decision to someone else (family, experts, God, fate, chance); its virtuous form, deliberation; the culture's rules for who decides and how fast.
**Neighbors and opposites:** hesitation, wavering, ambivalence, dithering, procrastination, deliberation, prudence; decisiveness, resolve, conviction, impulsiveness, rashness. **Excess:** paralysis; leaving others to decide; decisions made by default. **Deficiency** (of deliberation): rashness and recklessness (Aries).

- **Words:** "indecisive", "wishy-washy", "on the fence", "fence-sitter", "dithering", "waffling", "flip-flopper", "analysis paralysis", "sleep on it", "FOMO", "decision fatigue", "make up your mind", "kick the can down the road", "punt", "the buck stops here" (Truman's desk sign); Spanish *indeciso*, *titubear*, *dudar*, *no sabe lo que quiere*, *ni pichas ni cachas ni dejas batear* (Mexican: you neither pitch nor catch nor let anyone bat), *nadar entre dos aguas* (to swim between two waters: to sit on the fence), *quedar bien con Dios y con el diablo* (to stay on good terms with God and the devil), *estar entre la espada y la pared*, *dar largas*, *ahorita*, *Dios dirá* (God will decide), *si Dios quiere*, *lo que sea, sonará* (whatever happens, happens *check*); "Seattle process" (Seattle's own term for endless public consultation).
- **Beliefs and standing:** Is long deliberation wisdom or weakness? "He who hesitates is lost" against "look before you leap" and "measure twice, cut once"; decisiveness as a leader's virtue (military, business, the frontier); consensus as the right way to decide (Quaker meetings; cooperatives; Indigenous councils, as the nations describe them), where slowness can be a sign of respect; leaving the outcome to God (*si Dios quiere*; Calvinist providence) and religious discernment (the Jesuits' Ignatian discernment); choice as freedom or burden (Schwartz, *The Paradox of Choice*, 2004, and the meta-analysis that found the "too many choices" effect inconsistent, Scheibehenne et al., 2010); the charge that women are indecisive (a stereotype to examine, not a finding).
- **Feeling and the body:** anxiety, rumination, regret; "sleeping on it"; gut feeling against reasoning; decision fatigue; the body language of hesitation; indecisiveness as a clinical sign (a symptom of major depression in the DSM; the doubting of obsessive-compulsive disorder); maximizers and satisficers (Schwartz et al., 2002).
- **Everyday practice:** "Where do you want to eat?" - **Institutions:** how groups decide: majority vote, supermajorities, vetoes, the filibuster, consensus (Quaker business meetings; co-op boards; Occupy's general assemblies in Oakland, Seattle and Portland, 2011), Robert's Rules of Order (1876); gridlock and government shutdowns (2013, 2018-2019, 2025); ballot measures that decide what legislatures won't; consultations and referendums (British Columbia's votes on electoral reform, 2005, 2009 and 2018, none of which changed the system; Mexico's *consultas populares*, 2021 and 2022, and the 2018 airport consultation); cooling-off periods (the FTC's three-day rule); default rules that decide for the undecided (automatic enrollment; organ-donor defaults; "nudges": Thaler and Sunstein, 2008); hung juries; Washington's advisory votes on taxes (ended 2023 *check*).

- **Expression:** Hamlet; Buridan's ass; Frost's "The Road Not Taken" (1916), often misread; "Should I Stay or Should I Go" (The Clash, 1982); films of the road not taken (*Sliding Doors*, 1998); self-help on decisiveness; jokes about "Seattle process"; memes about choosing where to eat.
- **Exemplars:** decisive leaders and how they are remembered (Truman, contested); leaders mocked as indecisive (Paul Martin); the patient deliberator held up as wise; the consensus builder; counter-exemplars of rashness.
- **History:** Seattle's long civic debates (the monorail, approved and then undone across five votes, 1997-2005; the Alaskan Way Viaduct and its advisory vote, 2007); Vancouver's freeway fight (1960s-1970s), which left the city without a downtown freeway; California's high-speed rail (approved 2008, still unfinished) and decades of Delta water plans; Mexico's canceled new airport (2018); the Trans Mountain pipeline, bought by the federal government (2018); consensus decision-making in Indigenous governance, as the nations describe it; the decades of BC treaty negotiations.

- **Who it applies to:** leaders, committees, legislatures and juries; families and couples; companies; machines: present-day attitudes to letting algorithms decide ("algorithm aversion": Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey, 2015; Pew, 2018), to recommendation systems choosing for people, and to self-driving cars making split-second choices.
- **Silences:** decisions people avoid talking about (wills, end of life, leaving a marriage); the cost of letting things drift; regret.
- **Change:** choice overload online; "decision fatigue" talk; algorithms deciding for people; polarization making shared decisions harder; delayed adulthood.

**Key questions:** Is long deliberation wise or weak? Who is expected to decide (the person, the family, the group, God or chance), and how long may they take?

## 9. Peace at any price

**Working definition:** keeping peace or harmony by giving way, staying silent or paying whatever it costs, even when the cost is one's own (or someone else's) rights, truth or justice.
**Facets:** conflict avoidance in families, friendships and workplaces; people-pleasing; "going along to get along"; silence about abuse to keep a family or a group together; settling to avoid a fight; paying off the powerful (protection money, bribes); appeasement between states; harmony norms that discourage open disagreement; the price borne by third parties (harmony bought with someone else's suffering); and its principled neighbor, pacifism and nonviolence.
**Neighbors and opposites:** conflict avoidance, appeasement, accommodation, placating, people-pleasing, "don't rock the boat", quietism, harmony; pacifism and nonviolence (principled, and not the same thing); confrontation, resistance, standing up, "no justice, no peace". **Excess:** complicity, enabling, being a doormat. **Deficiency** (of peacekeeping): belligerence, quarrelsomeness, feuding.

- **Words:** "peace at any price", "don't rock the boat", "go along to get along", "keep the peace", "pick your battles", "walking on eggshells", "people-pleaser", "doormat", "pushover", "appeasement", "enabler", "complicit", "not worth the fight"; Spanish *llevar la fiesta en paz* (to keep things peaceful), *más vale un mal arreglo que un buen pleito* (a bad settlement beats a good lawsuit), *no buscarle tres pies al gato* (don't go looking for trouble), *hacerse de la vista gorda* (to turn a blind eye), *la ropa sucia se lava en casa* (dirty laundry is washed at home), *el que calla otorga* (silence gives consent), *calladita te ves más bonita* (you look prettier quiet: a saying that silences women), *aguantar* (to endure; in Aries), *agachado* (one who keeps his head down), *dejado* (a pushover), *abnegación* (self-denial); Filipino *pakikisama* (going along with the group); Japanese *wa* (harmony, elsewhere); Hawaiian *hoʻoponopono*, a family practice of setting things right by airing the wrong, as Hawaiians describe it (a contrast, elsewhere).
- **Beliefs and standing:** Is giving way a virtue (meekness, humility, "turn the other cheek", "blessed are the peacemakers") or a vice (cowardice, complicity)? Pacifist faiths (Quakers; Mennonites; the Doukhobors in British Columbia's Kootenays since 1908) and principled nonviolence (King; Chávez) as the counter-case: peace by refusing violence, not by giving way; King's contrast between a "negative peace which is the absence of tension" and a positive peace "which is the presence of justice" (1963); Galtung's negative and positive peace (1969); *simpatía* and *abnegación* (among Díaz-Guerrero's premises of the Mexican family) as ideals to test; Munich as the lesson Anglo politics draws ("appeasement"); Canada's peacekeeping and Mexico's non-intervention, read as principle or as avoidance (a question); therapy culture's "boundaries" against "people-pleasing".
- **Feeling and the body:** fear of conflict; the "fawn" response of popular trauma psychology (Pete Walker); swallowed anger and its bodily costs (in Mexican folk medicine, *bilis* and *muina*); smiling while upset; silence; relief after avoiding a fight, and resentment later.
- **Everyday practice:** no politics at family dinners; agreeing to unwanted plans; passive-aggressive notes instead of a talk with a neighbor; settling out of court; silence about harassment at work (the EEOC's 2016 task force found most people never report it); bystanders who don't step in; paying extortion (*cobro de piso*) and bribes to avoid trouble; letting a bully win; staying in an unhappy marriage "for the kids"; keeping quiet online (Pew's 2014 study of the "spiral of silence").
- **Life course:** "be nice", "share", "say sorry", and children made to hug or apologize; girls taught to be agreeable (*calladita*); bullying policies ("work it out" against reporting); teenage conformity; marriages kept together at a cost; the elder who keeps the family peace; deathbed reconciliations.
- **Ritual:** handshakes after disputes and games (the hockey playoff handshake line); peace offerings; the sign of peace at Mass; holiday-dinner truces; Remembrance Day and Veterans Day; Seattle's lantern-floating ceremony for Hiroshima and Nagasaki (since 1984); the International Day of Peace.
- **Institutions:** conscientious objection (U.S. Selective Service; the Civilian Public Service camps of World War II, such as Camp Angel at Waldport, Oregon; Canada's exemptions for Doukhobors and Mennonites *check*); plea bargains and settlements (most criminal cases end in a plea; most civil cases settle); nondisclosure agreements and the laws limiting them (California's SB 820, 2018, and Silenced No More Act, 2021; Washington's 2022 law); HR departments and their mediation; mediation in family courts, and its exceptions for domestic violence; restorative justice, and the critique that it presses victims to forgive; the PRI's long peace by co-opting opponents (the "*pax priísta*" *check*); Mexico's security policy of *abrazos, no balazos* (hugs, not bullets, 2018-2024), debated as peace at any price or as a different idea of security.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise as "easygoing", "nice", "low-maintenance", *buena onda*; the sanction on troublemakers ("drama queen", "making a scene", *escandalosa*, *revoltoso*, *argüendero*: Mexican for a stirrer of quarrels); the reverse sanction on doormats ("pushover", "spineless", "enabler", *agachado*, *dejado*); whistleblowers punished for breaking the peace; "snitches get stitches".
- **Expression:** proverbs (above); Chamberlain's "peace for our time" (1938) as a cautionary quotation; "Give Peace a Chance" (1969); *telenovelas* and *rancheras* of long-suffering women and resigned men; self-help on people-pleasing (Beattie, *Codependent No More*, 1986; Braiker, *The Disease to Please*, 2001); films of silenced abuse coming to light.
- **Exemplars:** pacifists (the Doukhobor leader Peter Verigin; Quaker activists of the American Friends Service Committee); principled nonviolent leaders (King; Chávez); contested: the Japanese American Citizens League's cooperation with internment (1942), defended by some as protecting the community and condemned by others; Chamberlain as the cautionary figure; the self-denying mother (*madre abnegada*) as ideal and as critique; whistleblowers as the opposite.
- **History:** Munich (1938); internment and the community's divided responses; the McCarthy era's silences (the Hollywood blacklist); the peace churches in the West; the Vietnam-era peace movement (Berkeley; the Oakland Induction Center protests, 1967) and the debate between nonviolence and armed self-defense (the Black Panthers, Oakland, 1966); the PRI's co-optation of opponents; the Doukhobors' clashes with British Columbia, and the New Denver dormitory where the province held Sons of Freedom children (1953-1959); *abrazos, no balazos*; #MeToo (2017).
- **Measures:** willingness to fight for one's country (World Values Survey, country); joining petitions, boycotts and demonstrations (World Values Survey, country; the GSS; Statistics Canada); reporting rates for crime and harassment (NCVS; ENVIPE; the SSPPS); EEOC charges by state; plea and settlement rates (Bureau of Justice Statistics; the Court Statistics Project); GLOBE's assertiveness (country); Hofstede's masculinity and femininity; the Global Peace Index (country) and the Institute for Economics and Peace's Mexico Peace Index (state); *simpatía* scales (*check*).
- **Variation:** gender (whether girls are raised to keep the peace, as a question); class (who can afford to fight: lawyers cost money); immigrant status (fear of deportation keeps people quiet); minorities and "respectability"; religion (the peace churches); generation (call-out culture against "keep it in the family"); each Indigenous nation's own peacemaking traditions.
- **Relations:** diplomacy (its healthy form), balance, indecision, projection (suppressed conflict returns as projection) and justice ("no justice, no peace"), in this sign; anger, self-assertion and moral courage (Aries); fear and surrender (Pisces); secrecy (Scorpio: family secrets); duty (Capricorn); self-sacrifice and compassion (Pisces).
- **Who it applies to:** abusive partners and parents; bullies; criminals (extortion); powerful states (appeasement); employers; family elders; dominant groups (accommodation by minorities); machines: present-day criticism of chatbots that flatter and agree with their users ("sycophancy"), and of people accepting terms of service unread to avoid friction (Pew, 2019, on reading privacy policies).
- **Silences:** violence hidden to keep a family together; abuse in churches and schools kept quiet (the Archdiocese of Los Angeles settlement, 2007; residential schools); harassment buried by NDAs; what minorities don't say to keep the peace with the majority.
- **Change:** #MeToo breaking silences; NDA bans; therapy language ("boundaries", "people-pleasing"); polarization making peace harder to keep; the *abrazos, no balazos* debate; the return of "appeasement" talk since 2022.

**Key questions:** When peace and justice conflict, which does the culture choose, and who pays the price? Is keeping quiet to keep the peace a virtue or complicity?

## 10. Projection

**Working definition:** seeing in others (partners, rivals, enemies, outsiders) what one disowns in oneself: one's own faults, impulses, force or fears; and how a culture places its own unwanted traits and troubles on an outside "other".
**Facets:** personal projection (the psychological defense: Freud; Jung's "shadow"); blame in couples and families; scapegoating (Girard; the goat of Leviticus 16); enemy images and dehumanization in war and politics; moral panics and "folk devils" (Cohen, 1972); misfortune blamed on others' envy or ill will (the evil eye, witchcraft); conspiracy theories; how a culture explains wrongdoing, by the person's character or by the situation (attribution); the images a region holds of its neighbors; and the opposite practices of owning one's faults (confession, inventory, apology).
**Neighbors and opposites:** blame, scapegoating, othering, demonization, hypocrisy ("the pot calling the kettle black"), denial, suppression; self-awareness, accountability, owning one's faults, confession. **Excess:** paranoia, witch hunts, demonizing whole groups. **Deficiency** (the other side): taking all blame on oneself.

- **Words:** "projecting", "that's projection", "the pot calling the kettle black", "people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones", "every accusation is a confession" (an online saying), "scapegoat", "witch hunt", "blame game", "the enemy within", "outside agitators", "boogeyman", "folk devil"; Spanish *proyectar*, *el burro hablando de orejas* (the donkey talking about ears: the pot calling the kettle black), *ver la paja en el ojo ajeno y no la viga en el propio* (the speck and the beam, Matthew 7:3), *chivo expiatorio*, *echarle la culpa a otro*, *mal de ojo* (the evil eye: illness from an envious or admiring look), *envidia*, *brujería*, *le hicieron un trabajo* (someone put a hex on him), *me echaron la sal* (someone cursed my luck); labels for outsiders as possible mirrors (*gringo*, *gabacho*, *chilango*; "Californians" in Oregon and Washington; "coastal elites"; "rednecks"), each a question to research, not a finding.
- **Beliefs and standing:** psychoanalytic and everyday ideas of projection; Jung and Gestalt therapy in California (Fritz Perls at the Esalen Institute, Big Sur, in the 1960s, built much of his method around projection); Christian teaching on judging others (Matthew 7:1-5) and confession as its remedy; therapy culture's "owning your stuff"; the scapegoat rite (Leviticus 16) and Girard's theory of scapegoating (Girard taught at Stanford); Hofstadter's "The Paranoid Style in American Politics" (1964); folk theories of misfortune: others' envy (the evil eye), one's own sin, or chance; conspiracy belief.
- **Feeling and the body:** anger at another that is anger at oneself; fear; disgust (used in dehumanizing propaganda); shame turned outward into rage (Scheff's studies of shame and anger *check*); the evil eye's symptoms in infants (fever, crying) and its cure by a *limpia* with an egg; amulets (*ojo de venado*; red string; eye charms).
- **Everyday practice:** couples' fights ("you always ..."); "he started it"; blaming coworkers and bosses; road rage at "bad drivers"; blaming newcomers (Californians for housing costs in Oregon, Washington and Idaho; see "Don't Californicate Oregon" in Aries); blaming immigrants or elites; fans blaming referees; online call-outs; conspiracy content; protection against the evil eye; *limpias*.
- **Life course:** children blaming siblings; teaching responsibility ("own it"); scapegoated kids at school; couples therapy (John Gottman's "Four Horsemen" of criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling, from his lab at the University of Washington); midlife; elders blaming youth ("kids these days") and youth blaming elders ("OK, boomer"); accountability at the end of life.
- **Ritual:** the burning of Judas effigies on Holy Saturday in Mexico (*la quema de Judas*), often made to look like politicians; effigy burnings at protests; rally chants against enemies; exorcisms; *limpias*; confession and the examination of conscience; Alcoholics Anonymous's "searching and fearless moral inventory" (the fourth step) as the opposite rite; days of atonement (Yom Kippur in the pilot's Jewish communities).
- **Institutions:** wartime propaganda (World War II posters; the Office of War Information); state un-American activities committees (California's Tenney Committee, from 1941 *check* its end; Washington's Canwell Committee, 1947-1948); exclusion laws (the Chinese Exclusion Act, 1882; Canada's head tax, 1885); hate-crime and hate-speech law; defamation law; couples and family therapy; truth commissions and official apologies (institutions for looking inward); school anti-bullying programs; platform rules on hate speech.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** the political reward for naming an enemy; the sanction on hypocrites exposed for the vices they condemned; "projection" itself used as an accusation online; violence against the accused (witch hunts; lynchings); the reward for self-examination (recovery, therapy, a public apology accepted).
- **Expression:** proverbs (above); *The Crucible* (Miller, 1953); *Invasion of the Body Snatchers* (1956, set in a fictional California town), read as Cold War projection; *The Twilight Zone*'s "The Monsters Are Due on Maple Street" (1960); *Lord of the Flies*; the Mexican film *Canoa* (1976), about a town that lynched university workers it took for communists (Puebla, 1968); political cartoons; wartime posters; the "every accusation is a confession" meme.
- **Exemplars:** counter-exemplars: demagogues and witch-hunters (Joseph McCarthy; Jack Tenney in California; Albert Canwell in Washington); exemplars of looking inward: leaders who apologized for their own side's wrongs (Stephen Harper on residential schools, 2008; Gavin Newsom to California's Native peoples, 2019, and the Truth and Healing Council that followed); Gottman and Perls as teachers of the idea; contested figures whom each side calls the other's projection.
- **History:** the anti-Chinese movement ("The Chinese must go!", Denis Kearney's Workingmen's Party, San Francisco, 1877); the expulsion of the Chinese from Tacoma (1885) and the Seattle riot (1886); the Vancouver riot (1907); Japanese-American and Japanese-Canadian internment as "enemy aliens" (the 1983 federal commission blamed "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure of political leadership"); the Mexican Repatriation of the 1930s, for which California apologized in 2005; the Zoot Suit Riots (Los Angeles, 1943); the Red Scare; the McMartin preschool trial (Manhattan Beach, 1983-1990) and the Satanic Panic; Proposition 187 (1994), scapegoating to its critics and fairness to its backers (a question); anti-Asian hate during COVID-19 and Stop AAPI Hate (San Francisco, 2020); the Inquisition's trials for witchcraft and heresy in New Spain (1571-1820); Mexico's constitutional power to expel foreigners (Article 33).
- **Measures:** how explanations differ by culture (Morris and Peng, 1994, on American and Chinese newspapers' accounts of the same murders: character against situation); conspiracy belief (Uscinski's surveys; Pew); affective polarization (ANES feeling thermometers); dehumanization scales (Kteily et al., 2015); hate crimes (the FBI's Hate Crime Statistics by state and agency, with uneven reporting; Statistics Canada's police-reported hate crimes by metropolitan area; CONAPRED complaints and ENADIS in Mexico); attitudes to immigrants and crime (World Values Survey, GSS and Pew items *check*); belief in the evil eye and witchcraft (Pew's 2014 survey of religion in Latin America *check*; Gershman's cross-country work on witchcraft beliefs); media content on "folk devils" (content analysis).
- **Variation:** party (each side accuses the other of projecting); class (blaming the poor or blaming elites); urban and rural; immigrants and the native-born; religion; generation; gender (blaming women or men for social ills, as a question); each Indigenous nation's own account of how harm and blame are explained.
- **Relations:** peace at any price (suppression that returns as projection), partnership (projection onto partners), justice (blame against responsibility) and the open enemy (section 0), in this sign; anger and identity (Aries: "we" and "they"); revenge and betrayal (Scorpio); fear and crime (Pisces); humiliation (Leo); honesty and truth (Sagittarius).
- **Who it applies to:** partners; enemies; minorities and immigrants; neighboring regions and peoples; animals (wolves blamed for livestock losses; sea lions blamed for salmon declines, from Herschel at Seattle's Ballard Locks in the 1980s to the Columbia River today); nature (blaming nature or ourselves for wildfires); spirits and witches; "the system"; machines: present-day fears projected onto AI and robots (Asimov's "Frankenstein complex"), and the reverse, minds projected onto machines (the ELIZA effect, after Weizenbaum's program, 1966; the 2022 claim that Google's LaMDA was sentient); blaming technology for social ills (the contested debate over phones and teenagers).
- **Silences:** what a culture won't say about its own history; the images settlers made of Native peoples (Edward Curtis, a Seattle photographer, and *The Vanishing Race*, 1904); the gap between the Left Coast's self-image and its own exclusions (a question to research); blame turned inward and never spoken.
- **Change:** "projection" in everyday online talk; therapy language; polarization; scapegoating in pandemics; public apologies and truth commissions; people attributing minds to AI.

**Key questions:** When something goes wrong, does the culture look first inward (fault, sin, responsibility) or outward (enemies, envy, witches, outsiders)? Which groups serve as its mirror?
