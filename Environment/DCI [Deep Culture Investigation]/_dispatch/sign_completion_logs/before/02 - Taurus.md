# Avenues of investigation: 02 Taurus (possessions and value)

**What this file is.** For each Taurus concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Taurus concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. Indigenous-language terms appear only where a nation or its language program publishes them, and every spelling is marked *(check)*.

**The concept list** (proposed, **not yet settled**; each sign's list is settled with the author just before its pass): possession, ownership, self-worth, values, security, stability, wealth, possessiveness, stubbornness, sensuality.

**Questions to settle with the list:**
- **Possession and ownership** are two entries here, split the way autonomy and independence were split in Aries: **possession** is having and holding things, and the bond between people and their things; **ownership** is a claim to a thing that others recognize (property and tenure).
- **Security and stability** overlap. Proposed split: **security** is protection against loss and want (of livelihood, holdings and safety); **stability** is continuity, things staying as they are.
- **Security** is also in Cancer's ten. Its proposed home is Taurus, and `04 - Cancer.md` keeps a cross-reference. Cancer's **protection** and **shelter** would then cover the safety of persons and of the home; Taurus covers the security of livelihood and holdings.
- **Values** is close to the subject of DCI as a whole. This entry is kept to values as a category: the word, what people name as their values, how values are ranked, taught and fought over, what may not be priced, and valuation (what things are worth). **Self-worth** is the worth of a person.
- **Near-synonym groups** from the concept index that touch this list (not merged): envy / jealousy (under possessiveness); persistence / perseverance / endurance and grudge / grievance / resentment (under stubbornness); status / rank / reputation (under wealth and self-worth); appreciation, in the recognition group (under self-worth).
- **Money** is not on the list. It is covered in section 0, beside wealth.

## 0. The domain: possessions and value

**Traditional areas:** possessions, money, resources, values, self-worth. **The domain question:** what is worth having, and how is it held?

- **What is worth having.** Ask people to name their most cherished things and say why (Csikszentmihalyi and Rochberg-Halton, *The Meaning of Things*, 1981: a method that can be repeated anywhere); what people save from a fire (accounts from the Paradise, Lytton and Los Angeles fires of 2018, 2021 and 2025); what wills name and what heirs fight over; what is stolen most; what is set on Día de Muertos *ofrendas* or placed in graves; Pew's open-ended question on what makes life meaningful, where material well-being was one of the answers (17 advanced economies including the U.S. and Canada, 2021); the WVS question on how important work, family, friends, leisure, politics and religion are in life.
- **Money.** Words for it (*lana*, *varo*, *feria*, *billete*; "bucks", "dough"; Canada's "loonie" and "toonie"; Chinuk Wawa's word for money, as the Grand Ronde dictionary gives it *check*); how money is earmarked and given special meanings (Zelizer, *The Social Meaning of Money*, 1994; Thaler's mental accounting); who manages a household's money (Pahl, *Money and Marriage*, 1989); cash against cards and phones (INEGI and the CNBV's Encuesta Nacional de Inclusión Financiera; the Federal Reserve's Diary of Consumer Payment Choice); the unbanked (the FDIC's National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households, by state); remittances (Banxico's figures by receiving state); religion and money (tithing, including Latter-day Saint tithing; the prosperity gospel; Catholic teaching on usury and the just price).
- **Money talk and its silences.** Whether people say what they earn, what they paid, what they inherited; salary secrecy against openness about pay (pay ranges posted in job ads; salary-sharing sites such as Glassdoor); asking friends the price of things; money talk between spouses and with children; advice columns on money etiquette.
- **Saving, credit and debt.** Saving rates (BEA's personal saving rate; Statistics Canada's household saving rate); rotating savings groups (*tandas*); pension accounts (Mexico's Afores; Canada's RRSPs and TFSAs; 401(k)s); buying on trust at the corner store (*fiado*, and the shop sign *hoy no fío, mañana sí*); pawnshops (Mexico's Nacional Monte de Piedad, founded 1775); payday lenders and check-cashing stores, and who uses them *(check)*; household debt (the New York Fed's Household Debt and Credit Report, by state; the Urban Institute's county map of debt in collections *check*; Statistics Canada's debt-to-income ratio); whether debt is shameful or normal; *cuentas claras, amistades largas* (clear accounts, long friendships).
- **Thrift and display.** Thrift sayings ("use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without"; "waste not, want not"; *el que guarda siempre tiene*); mending and reuse; thrift stores (Goodwill, Value Village), swap meets (Pasadena's Rose Bowl Flea Market) and *tianguis*; secondhand U.S. clothing sold by the bale (*pacas*) and in *segundas* along the border; *venta de garage*; coupons and bulk buying (Costco, based in Issaquah, Washington); against these, display: conspicuous consumption (Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899), lowriders, luxury brands, *quinceañera* and wedding spending, "stealth wealth"; the WVS child quality "thrift, saving money and things".
- **Consumption and brands.** Shopping as leisure (the American Time Use Survey's time spent buying goods); malls and their decline; brands born in the pilot (Amazon, Costco, Apple, Nike, REI, Starbucks, Lululemon, In-N-Out, Levi's) and what they say about the region; sales rituals (Black Friday; Mexico's *El Buen Fin*, since 2011); campaigns against consumption (Adbusters and Buy Nothing Day, both from Vancouver; *The Story of Stuff*, 2007, from Berkeley); spending patterns (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey by region and some metros; INEGI's ENIGH by state; Statistics Canada's Survey of Household Spending); advertising as a record of what is held worth having.
- **Resources.** Who holds natural resources, and how people use and share them: land, water, timber, fish, minerals.
  - water: who gets water and who goes without; the Owens Valley water wars with Los Angeles (from 1905); what the dams drowned (Celilo Falls, the great Columbia River fishing place, flooded in 1957); drought habits (brown lawns, short showers); CONAGUA's yearly water statistics (*Estadísticas del Agua en México* *check*); Worster, *Rivers of Empire* (1985); Reisner, *Cadillac Desert* (1986);
  - forests: logging towns and what timber meant to them; the timber wars (the spotted owl, 1990; Clayoquot Sound, 1993; Fairy Creek, 2020-2022); wise use against preservation (Pinchot against Muir; the Hetch Hetchy fight, 1913);
  - fish: Indigenous fishing as the nations describe it (the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission's salmon plan, *Wy-Kan-Ush-Mi Wa-Kish-Wit* *check*); the fish-ins of the 1960s and 1970s in Washington; Baja's fishing cooperatives for abalone and lobster *(check)*; the Cucapá's struggle to fish in the Colorado River delta *(check)*;
  - minerals: prospectors and mining towns, and their booms and busts (the Cariboo gold rush, 1860s; the copper town of Santa Rosalía, Baja California Sur, built by a French company from 1885 *check*); local and Native opposition to mines; the Mount Polley tailings spill (British Columbia, 2014);
  - personal resources: time, skills, kin and contacts (Lomnitz, *Networks and Marginality*, 1977, on reciprocity among the poor of Mexico City); resourcefulness ("make do"; *rascuachismo*, the Chicano aesthetic of making do *check*).
- **Land and attachment to place.** Place-attachment studies (Lewicka's review, 2011 *check*); Spanish *terruño*, *patria chica* and *querencia* (the place one loves and returns to; in bullfighting, the spot in the ring where the bull feels safe); land as kin and teacher in Indigenous teachings, as the nations state them (the Stó:lō Nation's *S'ólh Téméxw*, "our world" *check*); century farms (Oregon's Century Farm and Ranch Program); the family *rancho* in the Baja California Sur sierra.
- **Comfort and the body's needs.** Food, sleep, warmth and rest as goods in themselves; see sensuality (section 10).
- **Prices and fair exchange.** Haggling against fixed prices; anger at price gouging after disasters and in shortages; tipping; "you get what you pay for" and *lo barato sale caro* (the cheap turns out dear). Fairness and reciprocity themselves belong to Libra.
- **Food ways** (Fischer's folkway for Taurus):
  - staples and regional cooking: salmon, shellfish, roots and berries on the coast and the Plateau; acorns in California, as nations such as the Miwok and Pomo describe gathering and preparing them; pine nuts in the Great Basin; camas and other roots on the Plateau; flour tortillas, *carne asada*, *machaca* and beef in the Mexican north against corn in the center; Baja fish tacos and chocolate clams; California cuisine (Alice Waters's Chez Panisse, Berkeley, 1971); Vancouver's Chinese and Japanese food heritage; coffee, wine and craft beer;
  - First Foods and food sovereignty as the nations publish them: the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation's First Foods mission (water, salmon, deer, roots and berries, in the order served *check*); eulachon grease and the grease trails; herring roe on kelp, gathered and traded by the Heiltsuk as they describe it; the First Nations Food, Nutrition and Environment Study (British Columbia, 2008-2009);
  - meals: when people eat (the Mexican *comida* in mid-afternoon against the evening dinner), who cooks and who serves, eating together or alone (ATUS), *sobremesa* (lingering at the table), *buen provecho*;
  - feasting and fasting: Thanksgiving and its counter-observance, the sunrise gathering on Alcatraz each November; *posadas* and Christmas *tamales*; Lent; Ramadan among Muslim residents; feasts at potlatches, as the nations describe them;
  - rules and taboos: the Latter-day Saints' Word of Wisdom (no alcohol, coffee or tea); the Seventh-day Adventists of Loma Linda; kosher and halal eating; vegetarian and vegan diets; foods avoided in pregnancy, illness or mourning;
  - food and status: street food against fine dining; "foodies"; Michelin stars in California; who eats what at a wedding; the snobbery of *fifí* and *naco* food;
  - food programs: SNAP and WIC; school meals; Mexico's DIF school breakfasts; Canada's national school food program (2024 *check*); food banks;
  - measures: BRFSS fruit and vegetable intake by state; USDA's Food Environment Atlas (county); ENSANUT (Mexico, by state); the Canadian Community Health Survey; FAO food balance sheets (country); food spending in the CE, ENIGH and the Survey of Household Spending; ATUS eating time;
  - expression: cookbooks; food writing (M.F.K. Fisher; James Beard, born in Portland); *Como agua para chocolate* (Esquivel, 1989); food television;
  - change: delivery apps; plant-based meat (Beyond Meat, Los Angeles; Impossible Foods, Redwood City); weight-loss drugs.
- **Dress ways** (Fischer's folkway for Taurus; runner-up Leo, which takes dress as display):
  - everyday regional dress: outdoor gear as daily wear (Patagonia, Columbia, Arc'teryx, Filson, Eddie Bauer, REI); athleisure (Lululemon, Vancouver, 1998); denim (Levi Strauss, San Francisco, 1853); grunge flannel; the tech hoodie and fleece vest; Western wear and the Pendleton Round-Up (Oregon, since 1910); *vaquero* and *norteño* dress (*texana* hat, boots, *cinto piteado*); the *charro* suit; *buchón* style; Pendleton blankets, given at Native ceremonies as the nations describe;
  - Native regalia as the nations show and describe it (button blankets; woven cedar-bark hats; Chilkat and Ravenstail weaving; Plateau beadwork), and the nations' guidance to call it regalia, not costume;
  - plain against display; natural against synthetic fibers; comfort against formality; who may wear what (rank, age, gender, office);
  - dress codes at school, work, worship, restaurants and clubs; Mexican school uniforms; debates over gendered school dress codes;
  - sayings: "clothes make the man"; "dress for success" (Molloy, 1975); *como te ven te tratan* (as they see you, they treat you); *aunque la mona se vista de seda, mona se queda*; *el hábito no hace al monje*;
  - appropriation debates (headdresses at music festivals);
  - measures: apparel spending (CE; ENIGH's *vestido y calzado*; Statistics Canada's SHS); Google Trends;
  - history: dress as a mark of caste in New Spain *(check)*; gold-rush workwear; the counterculture;
  - change: fast fashion against resale (ThredUp's resale reports *check*); repair and mending; the used-clothing trade across the border.
- **Wealth ways** (Fischer's folkway for Taurus; the concept of wealth is section 7):
  - how wealth is gained: work, land, inheritance, extraction (gold, timber, fish, oil), speculation, tech fortunes, patronage, crime;
  - how it is shared out: inequality by county (the ACS Gini index); wealth surveys (the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances; Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security; INEGI's ENIGH); Fischer's regional patterns of inequality and their heirs in the pilot (Woodard's Left Coast, Far West and El Norte);
  - how it is shown or hidden: display, stealth wealth, the gated estate, the modest house of the rich;
  - how it is given away: the potlatch and giveaways as the nations describe them (the U'mista Cultural Centre in Alert Bay holds regalia seized after the 1921 Cranmer potlatch and later returned); *padrinos* who share the costs of a wedding or *quinceañera*; philanthropy (the Gates Foundation; the Hewlett and Packard foundations); church tithes; remittances;
  - how it passes on: wills, inheritance customs, family firms, family land;
  - how people feel about taxes: whether a county's frontier past predicts its views on taxes and redistribution (Bazzi, Fiszbein and Gebresilasse, 2020); the California tax revolt of 1978, led by Howard Jarvis; whether cheating on taxes is ever justified (the WVS item); whether the rich should pay more (the ISSP Role of Government module *check*).
- **Building ways** (runner-up; home sign Cancer): the house as possession, investment and display (property values, remodeling, Vancouver's "monster house" debates *check*); see `04 - Cancer.md`.

## 1. Possession

**Working definition:** having and holding things: the bond between people and the things they keep, use, carry, store, lend, lose and give away.
**Facets:** everyday belongings; cherished objects and keepsakes; heirlooms; collections; clutter and hoarding; lending, borrowing and sharing; losing and finding; theft; gifts; possessions as part of the self; the possessions of the dead.
**Neighbors and opposites:** belongings, having, keeping, property in its everyday sense; dispossession, loss, destitution, nonattachment. **Excess:** hoarding, acquisitiveness, materialism. **Deficiency:** destitution; or, held up as an ideal, renunciation.

- **Words:** "mine", "stuff", "things", "belongings", "keepsake", "heirloom", "junk", "clutter", "pack rat", "hoarder", "collector", "treasure", "retail therapy", "finders keepers", "dibs", "shotgun" (claiming the front seat), "possession is nine-tenths of the law", "one man's trash is another man's treasure", "you can't take it with you"; the English word's second sense, spirit possession; Spanish *pertenencias*, *bienes*, *cosas*, and in Mexico *chivas* (*agarra tus chivas*), *tiliches*, *triques*, *cachivaches*; *apartar* (to set aside, to claim); *el que se fue a la Villa perdió su silla* (whoever leaves loses their seat); the courtesy *es suyo* or *está a sus órdenes*, said of an object a guest admires; the grammar of having (*tener*; whether a language marks some things, such as body parts and kin, as possessed differently from others: alienable against inalienable possession, found in many Indigenous languages of the region *check*); an old English slur for taking back a gift, usually traced to settlers' misreading of Native gift exchange (described, not quoted; *check*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is having things good, neutral or a spiritual danger? Christian teaching on treasure on earth and the rich young man; Franciscan poverty (the California missions were Franciscan); nonattachment as taught in the region's Zen centers (the San Francisco Zen Center); minimalism as a secular ideal; teachings on sharing as the nations state them (the Dene Laws published by the Dene Nation *check*); possessions as part of the self (Belk, "Possessions and the Extended Self", 1988); objects kept even while given (Weiner, *Inalienable Possessions*, 1992); the gift and its obligations (Mauss, *The Gift*, 1925); materialism as a cost to well-being (Kasser, *The High Price of Materialism*, 2002).
- **Feeling and the body:** attachment to objects; the grief of losing them (survivors of the Paradise, Lytton and Los Angeles fires); touching a thing makes people value it more (Peck and Shu, 2009); the stress of clutter (Saxbe and Repetti, 2010, from the UCLA study of Los Angeles families); comfort objects in childhood and old age; hoarding disorder (in the DSM since 2013); the thrill of acquiring.
- **Everyday practice:** what people keep and how much (UCLA's *Life at Home in the Twenty-First Century*, 2012, which cataloged the belongings of 32 Los Angeles families); garages and self-storage; yard sales, swap meets, *tianguis*; lending tools and books (tool libraries; Little Free Libraries); giving things away (Buy Nothing groups, begun on Bainbridge Island, Washington, in 2013; Freecycle; Craigslist's free section; curbside "free" piles); marking things as one's own (names on lunch boxes; *apartar* a seat or a parking spot with an object); what people carry every day; how guests treat a host's things; what people do with a lost wallet.
- **Life course:** the toddler's "mine!"; learning to share in preschool ("sharing is caring"); a child's own room and own things; allowances and the first things bought with one's own money; the first car; what each spouse brings to a marriage; downsizing in old age; "death cleaning"; estate sales; what happens to the belongings of the dead: kept, divided, sold, burned or given away (customs of burning or giving away a dead person's things among some Yuman and Great Basin peoples, as the nations themselves describe them *check*).
- **Ritual:** gift days (Christmas, birthdays, Mexico's Día de Reyes on January 6); housewarming, wedding and baby-shower gifts, and registries; the *arras*, thirteen coins given at a Catholic wedding; giveaways at powwows and potlatches, as the nations describe them; blessings of objects, vehicles and animals (the Blessing of the Animals on Olvera Street, Los Angeles); objects placed on *ofrendas* for the dead.
- **Institutions:** museums and their return of objects to the peoples they came from (the Royal BC Museum's repatriation work; Mexico's campaigns to stop sales of its archaeological pieces abroad, such as #MiPatrimonioNoSeVende, 2021 *check*); consumer agencies (Mexico's PROFECO); pawnshops (the Nacional Monte de Piedad), secondhand dealers, thrift stores and self-storage firms; lost-and-found offices; repair cafés and the right-to-repair movement *(check)*; professional organizers and their association (NAPO *check*); hoarding task forces (San Francisco's, 2009 *check*); storage for homeless people's belongings (the Bin on Los Angeles's Skid Row *check*).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the tidy and the thrifty; mockery of hoarders and of clutter; shame for losing things; the shaming of thieves, and everyday words for kinds of theft (*robo hormiga*, small repeated theft by employees; *cristalazo*, the car smash-and-grab); *ladrón que roba a ladrón tiene cien años de perdón* (a thief who robs a thief earns a hundred years' pardon); the return of lost property as a test of honesty.
- **Expression:** *Citizen Kane*'s Rosebud (1941); *Toy Story* (Pixar, 1995), where a child's name on a toy's boot marks it as his; the "mine" gulls of *Finding Nemo* (2003); George Carlin's routine "A Place for My Stuff" (1981); Tolkien's ring and "my precious"; reality shows about hoarders, decluttering (*Tidying Up with Marie Kondo*, 2019) and storage-unit auctions; songs about things lost and kept.
- **Exemplars:** collectors (William Randolph Hearst and Hearst Castle); Sarah Winchester's ever-growing house in San Jose; minimalists and renunciants; people known for giving everything away; contested figures: collectors of Native objects (museum founders; private collectors).
- **History:** the founders' attitudes (Yankee thrift; the missions' control over Native goods and labor; gift exchange and trade networks along the coast and across the Plateau, as the nations tell them); the fur trade; the gold rush (claims, caches, theft and lynching); the seizure of Native regalia and objects (the 1921 Cranmer potlatch seizure); the property losses of interned Japanese Americans and dispossessed Japanese Canadians (the University of Victoria's Landscapes of Injustice project); mass consumption after 1945.
- **Measures:** self-storage businesses and used-goods stores per capita (Census County Business Patterns, NAICS 531130 and 459510 *check*); household goods (INEGI's census asks which appliances and vehicles a dwelling has; the ACS asks about vehicles and computers; Statistics Canada's Survey of Household Spending on household equipment); spending on goods (CE, ENIGH, SHS); time spent shopping (ATUS); theft as households report it (INEGI's ENVIPE by state); lost-wallet return rates (Cohn et al., 2019, 40 countries); the endowment effect across cultures (Maddux et al., 2010 *check*; Apicella et al., 2014, among the Hadza *check*); the Material Values Scale (Richins and Dawson, 1992); hoarding prevalence *(check)*.
- **Variation:** class (having little against having too much); rural sheds and barns against urban storage units; immigrants who arrived with little; generation (heirlooms the young don't want); gender (who keeps the family's things and memories); renters and the homeless (whose belongings are most at risk).
- **Relations:** ownership (the recognized claim behind possession); possessiveness (its excess); wealth; generosity (Leo); reciprocity and sharing (Libra); inheritance and the commons (Scorpio); home (Cancer); independence (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** children's things and who controls them; animals and pets (pets held as possessions; animals' own caches and territories); the dead and their goods; spirits (possession in the other sense); digital possessions (files, games, accounts, photos); whether people think machines or AI could possess anything (present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** what people hide owning (guns, debts, stored clutter); the shame of hoarding; stolen or looted objects in family and museum collections; the belongings of the homeless.
- **Change:** renting and subscribing in place of owning (streaming, software); the sharing economy (Airbnb, 2008, and Uber, 2009, both from San Francisco); decluttering and minimalism; resale; digital goods and NFTs (the 2021 boom); the right-to-repair movement; panic buying (2020).

**Key questions:** Is having many things a good, a burden or a fault? What must be shared, and what may a person keep for themselves?

## 2. Ownership

**Working definition:** the recognized claim to have, use, keep others from, and give away or sell a thing, and what that claim rests on.
**Facets:** private, common, collective, state and open-access property; tenure (how land and goods are held); what can be owned (land, water, animals, houses, businesses, ideas, names, songs, crests, data, once people); who may own (women, children, foreigners, Native nations, corporations); how ownership begins (first arrival, labor, purchase, inheritance, grant, conquest, long use); how it ends (sale, gift, seizure, abandonment, loss).
**Neighbors and opposites:** property, title, tenure, stewardship, custodianship; dispossession, trespass, tenancy, the commons. **Excess:** enclosure; treating everything, people included, as property. **Deficiency:** insecure tenure; landlessness.


**The left-right axis lives here** (author, 2026-11-10). The author measures where a society sits between left and right by how much of what it produces its government spends: government spending as a share of GDP. 0% is the far right (anarchy: a government that owns nothing can't exist); 100% is the far left (communism: the government owns everything that is produced). The question it asks is one of ownership: how much of what a society produces stays in private hands, and how much is handed to the government?
- **Two figures:** the government's own level, compared within its own country; and all levels combined, compared across countries, with money passed between levels counted once. Spending means total expenditure.
- **The numbers** are collected at Stage 2 (`Shared/Sources_and_Datasets.md`, section G). Here they are read beside everything else the culture shows about ownership and wealth: whether people want more in public or in private hands, how they talk about taxes and public goods, and what they think the government should own.
- **Beside it:** the left-right readings of the law (Libra, the letter of the law) and other measures; where they disagree, the disagreement is recorded, not reconciled.
- **Words:** "owner", "property", "private property", "no trespassing", "my house, my rules", "homeowner", "landlord", "squatter", "deed", "title", "pink slip" (a car's title, in California), "Crown land" (British Columbia's everyday word for public backcountry), "unceded" (in land acknowledgments: "the unceded territories of the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh"), "own it" (meaning take responsibility), "stakeholder"; Spanish *dueño*, *propietario*, *propiedad privada*, *patrimonio* (the family's holdings), *escrituras* (title deeds), *ejido*, *ejidatario*, *comunero*, *avecindado*, *paracaidista* (a squatter), *invasión* (a land occupation), *la tierra es de quien la trabaja* (the land belongs to those who work it), *mi casa es su casa*; owned property in Northwest Coast tradition, as nations describe it: the Tlingit *at.óow* (owned clan treasures: crests, names, songs, stories, regalia *check*), the Nuu-chah-nulth *ḥahuułi* (a hereditary chief's territories and the resources in them *check*); Chinuk Wawa *potlatch*, "to give" *check*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** where ownership comes from: labor (Locke), first possession, purchase, a grant from God or the state, use, kinship, or a people's relationship with the land; is private property a natural right, a social bargain or a theft? The social function of property in Catholic teaching; ownership as stewardship for future generations, as nations state it; whether land can be sold at all (the speech attributed to Chief Seattle, much of it written by a screenwriter in 1971: a case of outsiders shaping the "Native view"); movements against federal ownership of Western land (the Sagebrush Rebellion; the Bundy standoffs); cooperative and socialist ideas; Juárez's "respect for the rights of others is peace" (1867).
- **Feeling and the body:** pride of ownership; feeling at home on one's own land; "psychological ownership" at work (Pierce et al., 2001); anger at trespass; the grief and rage of dispossession.
- **Everyday practice:** fences, gates and signs; property lines and quarrels with neighbors; homeowners' associations; renting against buying; holding papers for one's land, or living without them; squatting and informal settlements (*colonias irregulares*), and the government programs that later hand out titles (INSUS); timeshares in Los Cabos; foreign retirees and second-home owners on the Baja coast; cattle brands as marks of ownership; asking who owns a crest, song or story before using it, in Northwest Coast protocol as the nations describe it; cooperatives and employee ownership (REI; credit unions; *cooperativas*); split ownership: condominiums and strata buildings, with their councils and quarrels; mobile-home parks where people own the home but not the land; and homes on leased reserve land (the Musqueam leases in Vancouver *check*).
- **Life course:** the first home; the first car; children's sense of who owns what (studies of the first-possession rule in children *check*); wills (the share of adults with one *check*; Mexico's September "month of the will" campaign *check*); inheritance by equal shares, by eldest son, or by the parent's free choice, and what heirs think is fair; the youngest son who stays with the parents and inherits the house, a custom across much of Mexico (Robichaux's "Mesoamerican family system" *check*); passing a ranch, a farm or an *ejido* plot to one heir.
- **Ritual:** closing on a house and handing over keys; public handovers of title deeds by Mexican officials; groundbreakings; land acknowledgments; feasts where names, crests and territories are witnessed and confirmed, as the Gitxsan, Wet'suwet'en and other nations describe them; branding days on ranches; Mexico's March 18, the anniversary of the 1938 oil expropriation.
- **Institutions:** banks and housing lenders, and the mortgage as the road to owning (Mexico's INFONAVIT, the workers' housing fund; Canada's CMHC; the American thirty-year mortgage); the real-estate trade and its associations (Mexico's AMPI *check*); homeowners' associations and strata councils; cooperatives and credit unions; the *ejido* assembly, where members decide on common land; Native nations' own institutions for holding territory (houses, clans and the feast hall, as the Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en describe them); land trusts and conservancies (the Sogorea Te' Land Trust; the Nature Conservancy); cattlemen's associations (Oregon's and Washington's *check*); 4-H and county fairs, where children raise and sell their own animals; museums and cultural centers that return objects to the nations that own them (the U'mista Cultural Centre).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** respect for owners and landholders; the stigma of renters and squatters; "trespassers will be shot" signs and anger at trespassers; the shame of being evicted or of losing the family land; tenant and land-defense movements as the counter-sanction.
- **Expression:** *The Grapes of Wrath* (Steinbeck, 1939); *Chinatown* (1974) on land and water; *There Will Be Blood* (2007); Rulfo, "Nos han dado la tierra" (1953), on land reform; *Pedro Páramo* (Rulfo, 1955), where one man owns everything; the slogan *tierra y libertad*; "This Land Is Your Land" (Guthrie, 1940); land-back art and murals.
- **Exemplars:** John Sutter, whose land was overrun in the gold rush; Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo and other Californios who lost their ranchos; Emiliano Zapata; Lázaro Cárdenas; Frank Calder (Nisga'a); Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en hereditary chiefs, as their nations honor them; Willa and Charles Bruce, whose Manhattan Beach land was taken by the city in the 1920s and returned to their heirs in 2022; contested figures: land speculators, railroad barons, mission founders.

## 3. Self-worth

**Working definition:** the value a person holds themselves to have, and what that value is believed to rest on.
**Facets:** worth given at birth (dignity); worth granted by others (honor, face, reputation); worth earned by deeds, work or wealth; worth from God; self-esteem and self-respect; worth after loss (unemployment, illness, old age, disgrace); the worth of a people as its members feel it.
**Neighbors and opposites:** self-esteem, self-respect, self-regard, dignity, pride; worthlessness, shame, inferiority, insecurity. **Excess:** arrogance, vanity, narcissism, entitlement. **Deficiency:** low self-esteem, self-loathing, being a doormat.

- **Words:** "self-esteem", "self-respect", "self-worth" against "net worth" ("he's worth five million"), "know your worth", "you're worth it", "worthless", "good-for-nothing", "insecure", "imposter syndrome", "people-pleaser", "doormat", "self-love"; Spanish *autoestima*, *amor propio*, *dignidad*, *valer* (*tú vales mucho*), *sentirse menos*, *hacer menos* (to belittle), *acomplejado*, *apocado*, *el qué dirán* (what people will say); Mexican *valer* in not-caring (*me vale*, *vale madre*: worth and indifference share a verb); *no eres monedita de oro* (you're not a gold coin, to please everyone); *tanto tienes, tanto vales* (you're worth what you have).
- **Beliefs and standing:** dignity, honor and face as three logics of worth (Leung and Cohen, 2011); is worth inherent or earned? - **Feeling and the body:** pride and shame and how they show (posture, gaze, voice); body image and worth; depression and suicide after the loss of a role; dress and grooming as self-respect; the felt sense of being looked down on.
- **Everyday practice:** what people boast of and what they hide; self-promotion online; "what do you do?" as a test of worth; how service workers are treated; accepting or refusing charity; how people speak of themselves (self-deprecation or self-praise as the norm); who sits where.

- **Ritual:** graduations, *quinceañeras*, confirmations and honoring ceremonies; naming and honoring ceremonies, as the nations share them; Orange Shirt Day and Canada's National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (September 30), from Phyllis Webstad's account of the new orange shirt taken from her at a residential school near Williams Lake, British Columbia; employee awards; Mother's and Father's Days.

- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise, titles, honors; being "taken down a peg"; the tall poppy; ridicule of the vain; the stigma of welfare and unemployment; *hacer menos* as a sanction.
- **Expression:** self-help books and affirmations; advertising ("Because I'm worth it", L'Oréal, 1971); José Alfredo Jiménez's *El Rey* (1971), whose singer has no throne or queen and is still king; *Nosotros los pobres* (1948) and the dignity of the poor; *pobre pero honrado* (poor but honest); country songs of the proud poor; survivors' memoirs.
- **Exemplars:** people who kept their worth in poverty or disgrace; John Vasconcellos (the self-esteem task force); César Chávez and the farmworkers' claim of dignity; Phyllis Webstad; Fred Rogers (elsewhere); anti-exemplars: the vain, the arrogant, the self-hating.
- **History:** Yankee worth through work and grace; Spanish *honra* and the colonial hierarchy of class and color (the caste paintings); Native peoples' standing in the colonial order and in the residential schools; the Chicano movement's "brown pride"; California's self-esteem wave of the 1980s.
- **Measures:** the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale across 53 nations (Schmitt and Allik, 2005); the Contingencies of Self-Worth Scale (Crocker et al., 2003), which shows what worth rests on (appearance, approval, school, family, God, virtue, competition); the Narcissistic Personality Inventory over time (Twenge); self-enhancement across cultures (Heine et al., 1999); honor values (Rodriguez Mosquera et al., 2000, Spain against the Netherlands); status anxiety and inequality (Wilkinson and Pickett, *The Inner Level*, 2018); suicide by county (CDC WONDER); YRBS hopelessness by state; INEGI's self-reported well-being survey (ENBIARE, 2021 *check*); the Canadian Community Health Survey's self-rated mental health.
- **Variation:** gender; class; race and colorism; immigrant generation; disability; whether men's worth is tied to providing; each Native nation's own account of what the residential schools did and what renewal restores.
- **Relations:** dignity, pride, recognition and humiliation (Leo); values; wealth (worth as net worth); identity (Aries); work and usefulness (Virgo); status and reputation (Capricorn); shame.
- **Who it applies to:** groups and peoples; workers replaced by machines; animals and nature (inherent worth); whether people grant machines and AI any worth or moral standing (present-day surveys *check*).
- **Silences:** men's low self-worth; worth after a fall from grace; the worth of unpaid care work; class shame.
- **Change:** social media and comparison; therapy language ("know your worth"); the backlash against "participation trophies"; debates over young men's sense of worth.

**Key questions:** Is a person's worth given at birth, granted by others, earned, or shown by what they own? Can it be lost, and how is it regained?

## 5. Security

- **Feeling and the body:** worry about money (the American Psychological Association's Stress in America surveys); sleep lost to money; the calm of a secure job; fear of crime and how it shapes movement at night; the relief of a full pantry.
- **Everyday practice:** saving and emergency funds; buying insurance; locks, alarms and bars on windows (*protecciones*, *rejas*), walls topped with broken glass; dogs; guns kept for protection; gated communities (*fraccionamientos cerrados*, *privadas*); preparing for earthquakes and fires; keeping cash or dollars at home; choosing a "safe" career; joining a union; mutual aid.

- **Expression:** Aesop's ant and grasshopper; "Fortress L.A." in Mike Davis's *City of Quartz* (1990); prepper media; insurance advertising; songs of hard times; Depression-era stories.
- **Exemplars:** Francis Townsend of Long Beach, whose old-age pension movement (1933) helped bring about Social Security; Henry Kaiser and the health plan born in his wartime shipyards (Kaiser Permanente, 1945); Upton Sinclair's End Poverty in California campaign (1934); savers and preppers; anti-exemplars: the spendthrift, the swindler who takes people's savings.
- **History:** famine and plenty in each people's history, as the nations tell it; frontier insecurity; the Depression and the Dust Bowl; Mexico's peso crises (1976, 1982, 1994-1995) and the lessons they taught about savings; the internment, which cost Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians nearly everything; the 2008 crash and its foreclosures; wildfires and insurance.
- **Measures:** the Federal Reserve's Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking (the ability to cover a $400 emergency); FINRA's National Financial Capability Study (by state); the CFPB Financial Well-Being Scale; food security (USDA's Current Population Survey supplement, by state; the Canadian Income Survey; the First Nations Food, Nutrition and Environment Study; the food-access measure in Mexico's official poverty data, made by CONEVAL and now by INEGI *check*); housing insecurity (the Census Household Pulse Survey); Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security and Canadian Financial Capability Survey; INEGI's ENIF and its survey of financial health (ENSAFI *check*); perceived insecurity (INEGI's ENVIPE by state and ENSU by city); feeling safe walking alone at night (Gallup's Law and Order Index, country); guns owned for protection (Pew); household gun ownership by state (RAND's estimates *check*); gated and walled communities (the American Housing Survey *check*); insurance coverage (Census; Mexico's insurers' association, AMIS *check*); job tenure (BLS); the Global Preferences Survey's patience item (country).
- **Variation:** class; age; gender (fear of crime); gig workers against salaried workers; renters against owners; Native nations and food security; regions exposed to quakes and fires; whether security is sought through family or through institutions.
- **Relations:** stability; wealth; ownership; protection and shelter (Cancer); fear (Pisces); trust (Scorpio); independence (Aries); freedom (Sagittarius).
- **Who it applies to:** the nation (national security); companies; the land and the food supply; data and computer security; whether people feel machines and AI make them more or less secure, at work and at home (present-day surveys).

**Key questions:** Security against what, and who owes it to whom: the self, the family, the employer, the state or God? How much freedom will people give up for it?

## 6. Stability

- **Life course:** routines for children; changing schools; the age of "settling down" (marriage, the first home, *sentar cabeza*); midlife changes; aging in place.

- **Expression:** songs of home and of staying ("Home on the Range") against songs of leaving; novels of family land; the old farmhouse in art and advertising.

- **Measures:** years in the current home and moves in the past year (ACS; Statistics Canada; INEGI); job tenure (BLS's biennial Employee Tenure supplement); divorce and the length of marriages; emotional stability by state (Rentfrow et al., 2008); tightness and looseness (Harrington and Gelfand, 2014); uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede; GLOBE); inflation (BLS CPI by metro; INEGI's INPC by city *check*); political stability (World Bank governance indicators, country); counts of century and centennial farms (Oregon; Washington *check*).
- **Variation:** rural and urban; old families and newcomers; class (stability as a luxury, or as being stuck); immigrants and refugees; generation.
- **Relations:** security; stubbornness; beginning and change (Aries); order (Capricorn); home and roots (Cancer); rebirth and crisis (Scorpio).
- **Who it applies to:** institutions, currencies and governments; ecosystems and the climate; the land; machines and systems (reliability and uptime).
- **Silences:** the costs of stability: people stuck in bad jobs, marriages and places.
- **Change:** housing costs and gig work making stability rarer; remote work; disaster displacement.

**Key questions:** Is staying the same a virtue or a rut? Which kinds of change does the culture welcome, and which does it resist?

- **Silences:** where family fortunes came from; inheritance; the hidden poor; envy; how much the rich actually have.

**Key questions:** What counts as wealth here, and is it a sign of virtue, luck or theft? Should it be kept, shown or given away?

## 8. Possessiveness

**Working definition:** holding on to what one sees as one's own, including people, beyond what the bond or the thing warrants, and the urge to keep others away from it.
**Facets:** over partners (jealousy, control); over children; over objects (refusing to share or lend); over territory (turf, surf breaks, parking spots, "my seat"); over knowledge and credit; over places and resources (localism).
**Neighbors and opposites:** jealousy, territoriality, clinging, guarding; generosity, sharing, letting go, trust. **Excess:** control, coercion, violence. **Deficiency:** indifference, neglect.

- **Words:** "possessive", "jealous", "clingy", "controlling", "territorial", "hog", "won't share", "mine", "locals only"; possessive endearments ("my girl", "my man"); Spanish *posesivo*, *celoso*, *celos*, *celotipia* (pathological jealousy), *encimoso* (clingy *check*), *codo* (stingy), *mamitis* (an adult's strong attachment to the mother), *mi vieja*, *mi gordo*, *mi reina*; *no eres mi dueño* (you're not my owner), a line in many songs; *el que no cela no ama* (who isn't jealous doesn't love *check*); *quien bien te quiere te hará llorar* (whoever loves you well will make you cry).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is jealousy a sign of love, of insecurity, or of control? Is a spouse or a child "one's own"? Teaching against coveting; wives seen as their husbands' property, and the end of that view; modern ideals of consent and autonomy; sharing as a duty in many Native teachings, as the nations state them; romantic love's claim to exclusiveness.
- **Feeling and the body:** jealousy and anger; the felt threat of loss; the toddler's grip; guarding; the display rules for jealousy, and who may show it.
- **Everyday practice:** checking a partner's phone; tracking apps; rules about friends of the other sex; sharing toys and tools; territorial claims (saving parking spots; *franeleros* who claim stretches of street and charge for them; surfing localism, such as the Lunada Bay Boys of Palos Verdes); gatekeeping in hobbies and workplaces; hoarding information at work.
- **Life course:** "mine!"; sharing between siblings; jealousy in teen dating; parents who won't let adult children go (*madre posesiva*); the empty nest; widowhood and a parent's remarriage.
- **Ritual:** weddings (giving away the bride; rings as signs of being taken); *pedida de mano* (formally asking for the bride's hand); elopement by "stealing" the bride (*robo de la novia*); fans defending "our house" at home games.
- **Institutions:** shelters, hotlines and counseling for people in controlling relationships (the National Domestic Violence Hotline *check*); Mexico's women's institutes (INMUJERES and the state institutes), whose campaigns name *violencia económica* and *violencia patrimonial* (control over a partner's money and belongings); school programs on healthy relationships; marriage preparation in churches (Catholic pre-Cana courses); couples therapy; advice columns and talk shows on jealous partners.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for protective partners and parents; mockery of the clingy; contempt for the stingy; the shunning of controlling or violent partners by family and friends.
- **Expression:** *Othello*; Gollum; rancheras and *corridos* of jealous love; telenovela jealousy; "Every Breath You Take" (The Police, 1983), heard as love or as surveillance; *Pedro Páramo*; Scrooge.
- **Exemplars:** the jealous husband of song; the protective mother; the miser; contested figures: the passionate, jealous lover as hero or as villain.
- **History:** the idea of the wife as her husband's property, and the women's movements that fought it; chaperoned courtship in Californio and Mexican families; gang turf; the guarding of land and fishing sites.
- **Measures:** the CDC's National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (state estimates; control items); INEGI's ENDIREH (by state; jealousy, control and economic violence); Statistics Canada's Survey of Safety in Public and Private Spaces (2018); jealousy scales (Pfeiffer and Wong, 1989); jealousy across cultures (Buss et al., 1992; Buunk and Hupka, 1987 *check*); sharing experiments with children across six societies (House et al., 2013); economic games across small-scale societies (Henrich et al., 2005).
- **Variation:** gender; generation (whether jealousy is still read as love); religion; immigrants; class.
- **Relations:** possession; wealth; love and family (Cancer); control and trust (Scorpio); envy; generosity (Leo); freedom (Sagittarius).
- **Who it applies to:** pets and their owners; companies and "their" customers; nations and territory; online data; machines and AI companions (present-day worries about possessive companion apps, and about people's attachment to them *check*).
- **Silences:** control within families; elder financial abuse; men's and women's jealousy as each is talked about.
- **Change:** tracking technology; changing norms about jealousy; open relationships; the spread of the term "coercive control".

**Key questions:** Where does keeping what is one's own become possessiveness? Is jealousy a sign of love or of control, and who may show it?

## 9. Stubbornness

**Working definition:** holding to a position, habit or course against persuasion, evidence or pressure.
**Facets:** intellectual (refusing to change one's mind); behavioral (refusing to change habits); moral firmness (holding to principle); tenacity (refusing to give up); refusing to admit error or apologize; refusing to sell or move (holdouts); collective (communities and nations that will not yield).
**Neighbors and opposites:** firmness, tenacity, perseverance, resolve, conviction, obstinacy; flexibility, open-mindedness, compromise, yielding. **Excess:** pig-headedness, dogmatism, the feud. **Deficiency:** spinelessness, the pushover, the "flip-flopper".

- **Words:** "stubborn", "bullheaded", "pig-headed", "mule-headed", "ornery", "cussed", "contrary", "set in his ways", "dig in one's heels", "won't budge", "holdout", "die on that hill", "my way or the highway", "double down"; in praise, "steadfast", "dogged", "tenacious"; "flip-flopper" as the insult for the opposite; Spanish *terco*, *necio* (in Mexico, stubborn or insistent rather than foolish), *cabezón*, *cabeza dura*, *testarudo*, *obstinado*, *aferrado*, *más terco que una mula*, *no dar su brazo a torcer* (not letting one's arm be twisted), *sostenella y no enmendalla* (hold to it and don't amend it *check*), *chaquetero* (a turncoat).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is holding firm a virtue (principle, constancy, loyalty) or a vice (pride)? Is changing one's mind wisdom or weakness? Admitting error as humility or as loss of face; martyrs and holdouts; honor cultures and not backing down; religious steadfastness.
- **Feeling and the body:** the felt certainty of being right; crossed arms, silence, refusal; anger when pressed; identity-protective cognition (Kahan); holding fast to a belief after it fails (Festinger et al., *When Prophecy Fails*, 1956).
- **Everyday practice:** negotiation and haggling; family feuds; quarrels between neighbors; refusing to sell (holdout homes); boycotts and strikes; refusing new technology or rules; meetings run by consensus, where one person can block.
- **Life course:** the "strong-willed child" (Dobson, *The Strong-Willed Child*, 1978); teenage rebellion; elders "set in their ways"; deathbed reconciliations or their refusal.
- **Ritual:** ritual refusals (vigils, sit-ins, hunger strikes, picket lines; the occupation of Alcatraz by Indians of All Tribes, 1969-1971); public apologies as the opposite (Canada's 2008 residential-schools apology; the 1988 apologies to Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians; Governor Newsom's 2019 apology to California Native peoples; Mexico's 2021 apologies to the Yaqui and Maya peoples).
- **Institutions:** unions and long strikes; standoffs with the state (Malheur, 2016; the Wet'suwet'en camps against the Coastal GasLink pipeline, 2019-2020, which supporters call defense of their territory and opponents call obstruction); church splits over doctrine; bodies that decide by consensus, where one member can block (Quaker meetings; co-op boards); mediators, as a profession for breaking deadlocks.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** honor for those who held out; mockery of the stubborn; the losses of impasse; the labels "principled" and "obstinate", and who hands them out.
- **Expression:** *The Story of Ferdinand* (Leaf, 1936); Melville's Bartleby and his "I would prefer not to" (1853); the mule in Western tales; rancheras of pride; holdout stories in the news.
- **Exemplars:** Edith Macefield of Ballard; Billy Frank Jr. (Nisqually), who kept fishing through the fish wars of the 1960s and 1970s; Hiroo Onoda, the Japanese soldier who held out until 1974 (elsewhere); contested figures: the Bundys; leaders who never apologized.
- **History:** Native peoples' long refusal to give up their land and fishing places, as the nations tell it; Yankee dissenters; Mexican traditions of resistance; long strikes; the Hetch Hetchy fight.
- **Measures:** Big Five openness and agreeableness by state (Rentfrow et al., 2008); need for cognitive closure across cultures (Webster and Kruglanski, 1994); dogmatism (Rokeach, 1960); grit (Duckworth et al., 2007); conformity across cultures (Bond and Smith, 1996); the length of strikes (BLS work-stoppage data); polarization (Pew); the WVS child quality "determination, perseverance"; how often people say they changed their mind on an issue *(check)*.
- **Variation:** gender (who is called stubborn and who determined); age; class; party; region; whether a group's firmness is read as principle or obstruction depending on who does the reading.
- **Relations:** stability; conviction and fundamentalism (Sagittarius); pride and honor (Leo); discipline (Capricorn); grudge and revenge (Scorpio); indecision and peace at any price (Libra), its opposites.
- **Who it applies to:** animals (the mule, the bull); institutions and bureaucracies; nations; machines that "won't" do what people want (present-day frustration with technology, and with AI that refuses requests).
- **Silences:** regret; mistakes never admitted; the cost of a feud to the family.
- **Change:** polarization; algorithms that reinforce views; changing norms of public apology.

**Key questions:** When is holding firm a virtue and when a fault, and who decides? Can a person change their mind or admit a mistake without losing face?

## 10. Sensuality

**Working definition:** enjoyment of the senses (taste, touch, smell, sound and sight) and of the body's comfort and pleasure.
**Facets:** food and drink; touch, warmth and texture; scent; music and sound; beauty to the eye; rest and comfort; bathing, massage and hot springs; dance; luxury; nature felt through the senses; sexuality as sensual pleasure (sex itself is Scorpio's).
**Neighbors and opposites:** pleasure, enjoyment, comfort, indulgence, hedonism; asceticism, self-denial, numbness, prudishness. **Excess:** gluttony, hedonism, addiction. **Deficiency:** joylessness, puritanism, sensory deprivation.

- **Words:** "sensual", "sensuous", "indulgent", "decadent", "guilty pleasure", "treat yourself", "self-care", "comfort food", "cozy", "comfy", "foodie", "hedonist"; Spanish *sensual*, *placer*, *gozar*, *disfrutar*, *a gusto* (at ease), *darse gusto*, *apapacho* (a warm cuddle; often traced to Nahuatl *check*), *chiquear* (to pamper), *antojo* and *antojitos* (cravings, and the snack foods named for them), *sabroso*, *rico*, *sobremesa*, *buen provecho*, *la gula* (gluttony), *la carne es débil*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** are the senses gifts, dangers or sins? - **Feeling and the body:** touch norms (contact and non-contact cultures; Jourard's counts of touching in cafés, 1966 *check*); interpersonal distance across 42 countries (Sorokowska et al., 2017); greeting with a kiss or a hug; comfort and texture in clothing; sensitivity to noise and smell.
- **Everyday practice:** meals and lingering; coffee, wine and beer cultures; spas, massage and hot springs (Esalen, Big Sur, 1962; Breitenbush, Oregon); *temazcal* sweat baths, as Indigenous practitioners offer them, against their resort versions; perfume and scent; the sale of white sage, a California plant, which some California nations object to *(check)*; gardens; dance halls; cannabis culture and cannabis shops; quarrels with neighbors over loud music.

- **Ritual:** feasts and *fiestas*; Carnival (La Paz; Ensenada); the wine harvest festivals of the Valle de Guadalupe; Thanksgiving; Seattle's Fremont Solstice Parade; San Francisco's Bay to Breakers.
- **Institutions:** restaurants, bars, wineries and cafés; spas, bathhouses and hot springs; cooking schools (the Culinary Institute of America at Greystone, in the Napa Valley); wine trade bodies and their festivals (the Napa Valley Vintners *check*); churches that worship through the senses; massage schools and the massage profession; naturist clubs; public-health campaigns on drinking and diet.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the good cook and the good host; censure of gluttony and indecency; diet culture; body shaming.
- **Expression:** M.F.K. Fisher, *The Gastronomical Me* (1943); *Como agua para chocolate* (Esquivel, 1989); Isabel Allende, *Afrodita* (1997); boleros; Henry Miller's Big Sur; food and wine writing; advertising for comfort and luxury.
- **Exemplars:** Alice Waters; James Beard; M.F.K. Fisher; Esalen's founders; anti-exemplars: gluttons on one side and joyless ascetics on the other.
- **History:** Native food traditions, as the nations share them; Spanish Catholic worship through the senses; Yankee plainness; the saloons of the gold rush; the counterculture; the rise of wine country (Napa, the Willamette Valley, the Valle de Guadalupe).
- **Measures:** Hofstede's indulgence against restraint (country *check*); ATUS time spent eating, sleeping and grooming; spending on food away from home, alcohol and personal care (CE; ENIGH; SHS); the World Happiness Report; Gallup's daily-enjoyment item; BRFSS sleep by state; massage therapists per capita (BLS OEWS, by metro); cannabis sales (state sales figures; Statistics Canada's National Cannabis Survey).
- **Variation:** religion; gender; class (luxury against simple pleasures); generation; immigrant cuisines and customs.
- **Relations:** joy, pleasure and play (Leo); sex (Scorpio); escapism (Pisces); health (Virgo); comfort (section 0).
- **Who it applies to:** animals (whether animals' pleasure counts); the land's scents and sounds in attachment to place; present-day attitudes to companion robots and to machines built for touch *(check)*.
- **Silences:** pleasure among the old and the disabled; sexual pleasure; pleasures people are ashamed of.
- **Change:** the wellness industry; cannabis; food delivery; screens and sensory numbing.

**Key questions:** Are the pleasures of the senses a good, a danger or a sin? Which pleasures are public and which private, and for whom?
