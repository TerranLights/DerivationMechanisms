# Avenues of investigation: 05 Leo (creativity and self-expression)

**What this file is.** For each Leo concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Leo concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** (*check*): a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. Indigenous-language terms are not supplied here. They come from the nations' own dictionaries and language programs (FirstVoices, run by the First Peoples' Cultural Council in British Columbia; the *Lushootseed Dictionary*, 1994; the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde's Chinuk Wawa dictionary, 2012). The exceptions are a few Chinuk Wawa words long since taken into regional English.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; it is settled with the author just before Leo's pass): creativity, self-expression, pride, recognition, being seen, dignity, humiliation, generosity, leadership, play, and the author's two named examples, honor and joy (placed under Leo by the author, 2026-11-02). Twelve entries.

**Questions to settle with the list:**
- **Recognition** is also in Capricorn's ten; its proposed home is Leo, and Capricorn keeps a cross-reference. It heads a near-synonym group: approval, praise, validation, acknowledgment, appreciation. This file researches all of them under recognition, together with the recognition of standing (titles, honors, credentials). The author may prefer to split esteem (praise for achievement) from standing (recognition of status).
- **Humiliation** is in a near-synonym group with ridicule, and the Leo notes folded shame into it. This file researches ridicule and embarrassment under humiliation, and shame only as the feeling humiliation produces. Shame has no home on any sign's list yet.
- **Pride, dignity and honor** overlap: one field of worth seen three ways (the feeling and the stance; the worth every person has; the worth held in others' eyes). They are kept apart because cultures split them differently (Leung and Cohen's dignity, honor and face cultures, 2011). Face, the fourth corner, has no entry.
- **Recognition, being seen and self-expression** overlap: being seen is perception and witness, recognition is valuation, self-expression is the act of showing. Kept apart here; they can be merged if the author prefers.
- **Play and joy** overlap (an activity and an emotion). **Leadership** borders authority (Capricorn); this file keeps leadership to the following that people give without formal command.

## 0. The domain: creativity and self-expression

**Traditional areas:** creativity, play, pleasure, romance, children. **The domain question:** how do people shine, play and take pleasure?

- **How much shining is wanted.** Whether the culture rewards standing out or blending in: survival against self-expression values (Inglehart and Welzel; the World Values Survey's cultural map); indulgence against restraint (Hofstede); tightness and looseness by state (Harrington and Gelfand, 2014); ideal affect, the excited or the calm good feeling a culture most wants (Tsai, 2007); "let your light so shine" (Matthew 5:16) against "the nail that sticks out"; the tall poppy (Aries).
- **How the culture groups the domain.** Whether its words split or fuse the Leo concepts: English *play* covers games, music and theater, where Spanish splits *jugar*, *tocar* and *actuar*; Spanish separates *honor* (standing) from *honra* (virtue and good name); Mexican *pena* spans shame, embarrassment and shyness; *orgullo* is praise and blame at once. Which of the twelve the culture treats as central, which as marginal, and which it has no word for.
- **Pleasure.** Whether pleasure is a good, a reward for work, or a danger: Puritan suspicion of festivity; the fiesta as release (Paz, *El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950, the chapter on the fiesta, a classic claim to test); Sunday rest and Sunday closing; how people see cannabis and psychedelics, as pleasure, medicine or vice (Gallup's polling on marijuana use *check*); vice tourism (Prohibition-era Tijuana and its Agua Caliente resort; Reno and Las Vegas; spring break in Cabo San Lucas); vacation, how much of it people take, and whether leaving days unused is a badge of devotion (*check* surveys); the World Values Survey item on how important leisure is; leisure time in the American Time Use Survey (ATUS). Food pleasure belongs to Taurus, sex to Scorpio, escape to Pisces.
- **Romance.** Courtship and dating scripts; who asks whom and who pays; romantic love as the reason to marry (Jankowiak and Fischer, 1992, found romantic love in 147 of 166 cultures sampled); the *serenata* and *llevar gallo* (*check*); *noviazgo* and *pedir la mano*; Valentine's Day and Mexico's *Día del Amor y la Amistad*, which includes friends; love songs (the *bolero*, the *ranchera*, the power ballad); romance novels and *telenovelas*; dating apps (Tinder, founded in Los Angeles, 2012) and Pew's surveys of online dating (*check*). Marriage belongs to Libra, sex to Scorpio.
- **Children as joy and display.** Whether children are a blessing, a cost, a project or a status display: the shift from the useful to the "priceless" child (Zelizer, *Pricing the Priceless Child*, 1985); baby showers and gender-reveal parties (one started California's El Dorado Fire, 2020); children's birthday parties and *piñatas*; "proud parent of an honor student" bumper stickers; whether children are welcome in restaurants, churches and workplaces; childlessness by choice (Pew, 2024 *check*); birth rates (CDC's National Vital Statistics System; Statistics Canada; INEGI). Child-rearing ways are Cancer's (runner-up here).
- **Fame and celebrity.** Hollywood and the influencer; what children say they want to be (a 2019 Harris Poll for LEGO found more U.S. and U.K. children wanting to be YouTubers than astronauts *check*); fame rising to the top value in tween television by 2007 (Uhls and Greenfield, 2011); fan cultures; local celebrity (the high-school star, the *charro* champion, the radio host); the Hollywood Walk of Fame.
- **Celebration and display.** Turning moments into occasions: what weddings, *quinceañeras* and graduations cost (The Knot's annual wedding survey *check*); sponsors (*padrinos*) and *mayordomías* who pay for fiestas; fireworks; house decorations for Christmas and Halloween; luxury and conspicuous display (Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899); lowriders and the car as display.
- **Gambling as a pastime** (risk itself is Aries): tribal casinos as destinations; state and provincial lotteries; card rooms; bingo halls and church raffles; Mexico's *Lotería Nacional*; betting on horse races and cockfights; the recent spread of sports betting on phone apps; how people see gamblers (the lucky winner, the high roller, the addict).
- **Sport ways** (Fischer's folkway for Leo):
  - **What is played and watched, by whom, in what spirit.** Fischer gave each British folkway its own sports (*check* details): town ball and football among the Puritans, horse racing and cockfighting in Virginia, the Quakers' suspicion of most games, shooting matches and rough-and-tumble fighting in the backcountry. For each unit: which sports lead in playing and in watching; individual against team sports (Aries); outdoor recreation as a way of life (hiking, skiing, climbing, surfing, fishing, hunting); baseball in Sonora and Sinaloa (the Liga Mexicana del Pacífico); soccer; boxing; American football; hockey and lacrosse (Canada's national winter and summer sports).
  - **Rodeo and *charrería*:** the Pendleton Round-Up (1910) and the Ellensburg Rodeo; *charrería*, named Mexico's national sport (1933 *check*) and inscribed by UNESCO (2016); women's *escaramuza* teams; *jaripeo*; cockfighting in *palenques*, an open and popular spectacle in parts of Mexico (*check*) and widely condemned in the U.S. and Canada.
  - **Indigenous sports, as the nations share them:** canoe racing and the Tribal Canoe Journeys (from the 1989 Paddle to Seattle); slahal (stick games) and hand-game tournaments on the coast, on the Plateau and among the Dene; the Dene games at the Arctic Winter Games; the All Native Basketball Tournament in Prince Rupert (since 1960 *check*) and "rez ball"; relay horse racing on the Plateau; the North American Indigenous Games (Cowichan, 2008); Rarámuri running, as the Rarámuri share it.
  - **Spectacle sports:** *lucha libre* (the mask, and the bet of mask against mask); bullfighting, and the quarrel between its fans and those who call it cruelty (*check* where it is still held, in Mexico City and the states); big-wave surfing; off-road racing (the Baja 1000, since 1967).
  - **The moral order of sport:** fair play and sportsmanship; trash talk; violence in and around sport (hockey fights; the Vancouver Stanley Cup riots of 1994 and 2011); fandom as identity (the Seahawks' "12s"; the Cascadia Cup, created by supporters in 2004 for the Seattle, Portland and Vancouver soccer clubs); Sabbath rules; the growth of girls' and women's sport since the 1970s; Mexico's women's league (Liga MX Femenil, 2017); class (golf and country clubs against rodeo and boxing); sport as a ladder for immigrants and the poor; the high-school game as the town's event.
  - **Institutions:** high-school athletic associations (California's CIF; Washington's WIAA; Oregon's OSAA; BC School Sports); the NCAA; Mexico's CONADE; Indigenous sports bodies; halls of fame (the BC Sports Hall of Fame).
  - **Measures:** ATUS (sports, exercise and recreation, by state with pooled years *check*); the BEA's Outdoor Recreation Satellite Account (by state); the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's National Survey of Fishing, Hunting, and Wildlife-Associated Recreation, and license counts by state; the Sports and Fitness Industry Association's participation reports; the Aspen Institute's Project Play; CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey (team sports, by state) and Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (physical activity); Statistics Canada's sport-participation data (*check* which survey); INEGI's module on sport and exercise (MOPRADEF); television audiences by market; sports-betting handle by state.
  - **History and change:** the 1932 and 1984 Los Angeles Olympics; Squaw Valley 1960 (the resort renamed Palisades Tahoe in 2021, dropping a slur); the 1968 Mexico City Olympics and the 1970 and 1986 World Cups; Vancouver 2010; the 2026 men's World Cup, shared by the three countries; esports; travel teams and the professionalizing of youth sport; worry over concussions in youth football; pickleball.
- **Dress ways** (runner-up; home sign Taurus): dress as display and costume (regalia, *quinceañera* gowns, prom, Halloween, drag, the *charro* suit); see `02 - Taurus.md`.
- **Child-rearing ways** (runner-up; home sign Cancer): praise, play and children as joy; see `04 - Cancer.md`.

## 1. Creativity

**Working definition:** making something new that others find valuable or fitting, in the arts, in crafts, in solving problems and in everyday life.
**Facets:** artistic creation; craft and skill; invention and innovation; everyday improvising (making do); creativity in speech (wit, wordplay); variation within a shared tradition against individual originality; who owns what is made.
**Neighbors and opposites:** originality, imagination, ingenuity, invention, craftsmanship; imitation, copying, convention, routine. **Excess:** novelty for its own sake, "disruption", eccentricity without craft. **Deficiency:** rigidity, conformity, sterility.

- **Words:** "creative", "artsy", "original", "genius", "gifted", "innovative", "disruptive", "maker", "tinkerer", "hack", "think outside the box", "creatives" (as a noun), "sellout", "derivative"; Spanish *creatividad*, *ingenio* and *el ingenio mexicano* (a popular phrase for clever improvised fixes), *hechizo* (Mexican: homemade, improvised), *chispa*, *inventiva*, *maña*, *artesanía* and *artesano*, *oficio*, *arte popular*, *rasquachismo* (the Chicano aesthetic of making do with scraps, named by Tomás Ybarra-Frausto, 1989); whether each Indigenous language has a separate word for "art" at all, or only words for making, a question for its own dictionary.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is creativity a gift (from God, the muse, the ancestors), an inborn trait, or a skill won by practice? Novelty against faithful variation within a tradition (Lubart, 1999, on Western and Eastern ideas of creativity); the Romantic genius and the tortured artist; whether art is self-expression, service to the community, prayer, or a house's inherited right (crests and designs owned by houses on the Northwest Coast, as the nations describe them); craft against fine art; whether art is real work ("get a real job"); Silicon Valley's creed of disruption.
- **Feeling and the body:** inspiration and flow (Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow*, 1990, and *Creativity*, 1996); the maker's hands; creative block; the "mad genius" belief and the research on creativity and mental illness; creativity and drugs (the psychedelic Bay Area of the 1960s).
- **Everyday practice:** hobbies and crafts (quilting, woodworking, knitting, sewing); cooking as invention; home decoration and gardens; customizing cars (lowriders, hot rods); costumes; do-it-yourself and improvised fixes (*hechizo*); music at home; community theater; open mics; posting made content online; garage startups.
- **Life course:** art and music in school, and whether they are the first classes cut; children's drawings on the refrigerator; arts camps; science fairs; whether parents push toward or away from creative careers; late-life creativity; elders as culture bearers and master artists, as the nations name them.
- **Ritual:** arts festivals and fairs (Burning Man; Maker Faire, begun in San Mateo, 2006; Seattle's Bumbershoot; the Vancouver Mural Festival); craft markets; *ofrendas* for Día de Muertos and their contests; Rose Parade floats; the raising of totem poles and the dedication of new carved works, as the nations share them.
- **Institutions:** arts councils (the National Endowment for the Arts; the California Arts Council; the Canada Council for the Arts; the BC Arts Council; Mexico's Secretaría de Cultura and its grants for creators, formerly FONCA); art schools (CalArts; Emily Carr University); museums and galleries; the film, music and game industries (Hollywood; game studios around Seattle and Vancouver); writers' and actors' guilds (the Writers Guild of America; SAG-AFTRA); maker spaces and community art centers; the nations' own protocols on designs and crests, and their cultural centers; the disputes between Indigenous weavers in Mexico and fashion brands over copied textile designs.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** prizes (the Oscars, the Grammys and Latin Grammys, Mexico's Ariel awards and national arts prizes); grants and fellowships (the MacArthur "genius" grants); sales and fame; sanctions on copying (plagiarism scandals; charges of cultural appropriation); ridicule of the pretentious artist; books pulled from school and library shelves; self-censorship by publishers and studios.
- **Expression:** novels, films and songs about artists and inventors; murals as public creation (the Chicano mural movement; Chicano Park, San Diego, 1970; the Great Wall of Los Angeles, begun by Judith Baca in 1976); Northwest Coast formline art, in the artists' own accounts and in outsiders' analyses (Bill Holm, 1965); Seri ironwood carving and basketry; Paipai pottery; Kumeyaay and Pomo basketry.
- **Exemplars:** Bill Reid and Robert Davidson; Emily Carr; Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera as national icons; Judith Baca; Juan Quezada and the potters of Mata Ortiz; Walt Disney; Steve Jobs and the garage founders; anti-exemplars: the plagiarist, the hack, the sellout.
- **History:** Mexican muralism after the Revolution; Hollywood from the 1910s; the 1960s San Francisco counterculture; the Chicano art movement; Silicon Valley; Seattle grunge; the suppression of the potlatch (1884-1951), which also struck at art made for feasts, and the revival of Northwest Coast art from the 1960s.
- **Measures:** the Arts and Cultural Production Satellite Account (BEA and NEA; by state); the NEA's Survey of Public Participation in the Arts (with the Census Bureau; state estimates *check*); artists in the workforce (ACS occupation data; NEA reports); Statistics Canada's Culture Satellite Account and census counts of artists (Hill Strategies' analyses by province and city); INEGI's satellite account for culture and its module on cultural events (MODECULT *check*); patents per capita, as a rough count of invention (USPTO by county); creative-class shares by metro (Florida, *The Rise of the Creative Class*, 2002, and later); trends in creative-thinking test scores (Kim, 2011 *check*); openness to experience by state (Rentfrow et al., 2013).
- **Variation:** class (fine art against folk art and craft); urban arts districts and rural crafts; gender (who is called brilliant: Leslie et al., 2015, on beliefs about brilliance across fields); ethnicity and the gatekeepers of art worlds; immigrant artists; each nation's own art traditions.
- **Relations:** self-expression (creativity's outlet); play (its practice ground); recognition (credit and authorship); work and craft (Virgo); ownership of works (Taurus); curiosity (Gemini); rebirth (Scorpio).
- **Who it applies to:** children ("every child is an artist"); animals (painting elephants, bowerbirds); communities and nations as collective makers; corporations ("innovation"); machines: present-day attitudes to AI-made art and authorship (whether people count a machine as an author; the 2023 Hollywood writers' and actors' strikes, which bargained over AI; polls on AI art *check*).
- **Silences:** the assistants, ghostwriters and craftspeople behind famous names; art taken from nations and held in museums, and its return (the U'mista Cultural Centre's return of confiscated potlatch regalia); creative failure and poverty.
- **Change:** generative AI; the creator economy; arts funding cut and restored; art and regalia returned to the nations; growing markets for Indigenous art; film production moving between Los Angeles, Vancouver and elsewhere.

**Key questions:** Is creativity new making by an individual, or faithful variation within a shared tradition? Who owns what is made: the maker, the family or house, the community, or anyone?

## 2. Self-expression

**Working definition:** showing one's inner self (thoughts, feelings, tastes, beliefs, identity) outwardly, in speech, appearance, art and action.
**Facets:** speaking one's mind; showing emotion; dress and body as expression; expression through art and things owned; expressing identity (gender, ethnicity, faith); political expression; restraint and masks.
**Neighbors and opposites:** authenticity, candor, voice, individuality, freedom of expression; reserve, restraint, conformity, self-censorship, the mask. **Excess:** oversharing, exhibitionism, self-indulgence. **Deficiency:** repression, a false self, silence.

- **Words:** "be yourself", "express yourself", "speak your truth", "authentic", "let it all hang out", "let your freak flag fly", "Keep Portland Weird" (after "Keep Austin Weird"), "TMI", "oversharing", "repressed", "uptight", "buttoned-up"; Spanish *expresarse*, *ser uno mismo*, *desahogarse* (to unburden oneself), *sin pelos en la lengua*, *decir las cosas como son*, *el qué dirán* (what people will say, a brake on expression), *aguantarse* (holding it in), *rajarse* (Paz's word for opening up, read as weakness; see Aries), *ser muy expresivo*, *ser reservado*, *las apariencias*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is expressing the self a need, a right, a duty or an indulgence? The "true self" of humanistic psychology (Rogers; Maslow's self-actualization) and therapy culture; the self kept behind a mask (Paz, "Máscaras mexicanas", 1950, a classic claim to test, not adopt); religious teaching on modesty, humility and the guarded tongue; whether restraint is read as dignity or as repression; whether talking helps thinking (Kim, 2002; Kim and Sherman, 2007, comparing East Asian American and European American students).
- **Feeling and the body:** how freely emotion is shown (Rychlowska et al., 2015, linked expressiveness to how many source countries a nation's population came from); display rules (Matsumoto); dress, hair, tattoos, piercings and makeup as expression; gender expression; hair rules at school and work, and the fights over natural Black hairstyles.
- **Everyday practice:** clothing and personal style; decorating rooms, cars and lockers; bumper stickers and yard signs; posting online; journaling; singing and dancing in public; karaoke; protest; how much people tell strangers (the "Seattle Freeze" as a local claim to test); venting at work and at home.
- **Life course:** teenage style and rebellion; dress codes and uniforms (uniforms in Mexican public schools; dress-code fights in U.S. schools); "use your words"; whether children may show anger, sadness or pride; coming out; the restraint expected of elders.
- **Ritual:** Pride parades (San Francisco, Seattle, Vancouver); Halloween and Día de Muertos costumes (*catrinas*); Carnival (La Paz, Ensenada); Burning Man's principle of "radical self-expression" (one of its Ten Principles, 2004); poetry slams and open mics.
- **Institutions:** Berkeley's Free Speech Movement (1964) and later fights over speech on campus; student newspapers, drama clubs and art programs; therapy and counseling, the talking cure as a profession; churches' teaching on modest dress and speech; dress and grooming codes at work; tattoo rules in the military and other uniformed jobs; school uniform policies; talk radio and call-in shows; social-media platforms and their moderation rules.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for authenticity and courage; sanctions for too much (the show-off, the drama queen, "attention-seeking") and for too little ("fake", "robotic"); the *qué dirán* and family shame; firing over posts; online pile-ons.
- **Expression:** confessional songwriting (the Laurel Canyon singer-songwriters); punk, grunge and hip hop; *corridos* that speak for their singers; Chicano poetry; memoir and the personal essay; zines; the selfie.
- **Exemplars:** self-expressive icons (Frida Kahlo; Kurt Cobain; Harvey Milk; Juan Gabriel, whose reply "lo que se ve no se pregunta" (what is seen is not asked) is itself a case of expression and silence *check*); counter-exemplars (the poseur, the conformist).
- **History:** the Beats and the hippies of San Francisco; the Chicano movement's self-naming; the *pachucos* and the zoot suit, punished in the 1943 Zoot Suit Riots; Native dress, hair and ceremony suppressed in boarding and residential schools and in the campaign against the potlatch, and their revival.
- **Measures:** self-expression values (Inglehart and Welzel; World Values Survey; country, some regions); indulgence (Hofstede); openness and extraversion by state (Rentfrow et al., 2013); tattoos (Pew, 2023 *check*); Google Trends for "authentic" and "be yourself"; attitudes to free expression (Pew; the Knight Foundation and Gallup; FIRE's campus surveys); Pride attendance (*check* sources).
- **Variation:** gender (men's emotional expression); generation; class (taste as expression: Bourdieu, *Distinction*, 1979); ethnicity and code-switching; religion; urban and rural; LGBTQ people.
- **Relations:** being seen (expression needs a witness); creativity; pride; identity (Aries); communication (Gemini); freedom (Sagittarius); nonconformity (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** children, employees, soldiers, the clergy; corporations (a brand's "voice"; companies taking public stands); animals ("expressive" pets); machines: whether people think AI "expresses" anything, or only imitates expression (present-day attitudes and debates).
- **Silences:** what families leave unsaid (*el qué dirán*); men's feelings; sexuality in some communities; politics at work and at family tables.
- **Change:** social media; "authenticity" as a marketing word; debates over gender expression; backlash against oversharing.

**Key questions:** Is showing the inner self a need, a right, or an indulgence? Is restraint a mask that hides the self, or a form of dignity?

## 3. Pride

**Working definition:** feeling and showing that oneself, one's work or one's own people have worth, from healthy self-respect to arrogance.
**Facets:** pride in achievement (authentic pride); arrogance (hubristic pride); pride in work and craft; pride in family and children; group pride (ethnic, national, regional, school, team); pride as a movement (LGBTQ Pride; Black, Chicano and Native pride); wounded pride.
**Neighbors and opposites:** self-respect, self-esteem, confidence, dignity, honor; humility, modesty, shame. **Excess:** arrogance, vanity, hubris. **Deficiency:** shame, self-abasement, no self-respect.

- **Words:** "proud", "too proud to beg", "pride goes before a fall", "swallow your pride", "full of himself", "cocky", "stuck-up", "humblebrag", "humble", "Pride Month"; Spanish *orgullo* (praise and blame), *soberbia* (the sin), *amor propio*, *creído*, *presumido* and *presumir* (to show off), *mamón* and *sangrón* (Mexican: arrogant, stuck-up), *se cree muy salsa*, *orgullo herido*, *orgullosamente* (as in "orgullosamente sonorense"), *humilde* (which in Mexico also means poor).
- **Beliefs and standing:** pride as the root sin (Augustine; Proverbs 16:18) against Aristotle's greatness of soul; Benjamin Franklin on pride as the passion hardest to subdue (*Autobiography*); authentic against hubristic pride (Tracy and Robins, 2007); collective pride as a remedy for stigma (Pride; Chicano and Native pride); whether a person may be proud of themselves, or only of family and people; Samuel Ramos's claim of a Mexican inferiority complex (*El perfil del hombre y la cultura en México*, 1934) and the replies to it, as a debate to read.
- **Feeling and the body:** the pride display (expanded chest, head tilted back, arms raised), shown even by congenitally blind athletes (Tracy and Matsumoto, 2008); swagger and standing tall; dressing up; pride in a strong, working body.
- **Everyday practice:** bragging norms; trophies, diplomas and children's report cards on display; flags on houses and trucks; regional and team merchandise; pride in the house, the garden, the clean truck; craftsmen's marks; telling or hiding one's achievements.
- **Life course:** praising children (praise for effort against praise for ability: Mueller and Dweck, 1998); school spirit; graduation sashes and *sarape* stoles; the first paycheck; pride in grandchildren.
- **Ritual:** Pride parades and Pride Month; heritage months; national days (September 16, July 1, July 4); homecoming; *honores a la bandera*; hometown fiestas.
- **Institutions:** state and national symbols and the care given them (flag etiquette taught in schools and Scouts; the Monday flag ceremony in Mexican schools); fights over flying Pride flags at city halls and schools; school spirit programs and alumni associations; heritage associations and ethnic clubs (*check* pilot examples); corporate Pride branding.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** pride admired when "earned"; arrogance cut down (the tall poppy; *mamón*); humbling by elders, coaches, peers; ridicule of "excessive" pride; groups punished for collective pride (the Zoot Suit Riots); backlash against Pride events.
- **Expression:** pride anthems ("Say It Loud - I'm Black and I'm Proud", 1968; "México lindo y querido"); *corridos* praising towns and states; Chicano art; hubris stories (Icarus; *The Great Gatsby*); boasting songs in hip hop and *corridos*.
- **Exemplars:** figures of proud bearing (boxers; *charros*; Muhammad Ali); proud craftsmen; Pride's founders (Harvey Milk; José Sarria in San Francisco); anti-exemplars: the arrogant who fall.
- **History:** San Francisco's and Vancouver's early Pride marches; "brown pride" in the Chicano movement; Red Power; "Black is beautiful"; Mexican national pride after the Revolution (*indigenismo*, muralism); Canadian pride defined against the United States (a claim to test).
- **Measures:** national pride (World Values Survey; ISSP National Identity modules *check* years; Gallup's annual question on pride in being American; Environics Focus Canada); state pride (Gallup's 50-state poll; see Aries); self-esteem across 53 nations (Schmitt and Allik, 2005); narcissism trends among the young (Twenge and Campbell, 2009) and the rebuttals (Trzesniewski, Donnellan and Robins, 2008).
- **Variation:** gender; generation; immigrants' pride in origin against pride in the new country; minority pride movements; class (working-class pride); region.
- **Relations:** dignity and honor (overlap to settle); humiliation (pride's wound); recognition; self-worth (Taurus); identity (Aries); humility and modesty (a near-synonym pair with no home sign yet); shame.
- **Who it applies to:** individuals, families, teams, towns, nations; companies (pride in the firm); animals (pride in show animals and working dogs); machines: pride in one's tools, car or code, and whether people credit robots with pride (present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** pride that refuses help; pride in contested heritage (pioneers against dispossession); shame hidden behind pride.
- **Change:** Pride's corporate sponsorship and backlash; falling national pride in U.S. surveys (Gallup *check*); bragging online.

**Key questions:** Is pride a sin, a virtue, or a right of the stigmatized? May a person be proud of themselves, or only of their family and people?

## 4. Recognition

**Working definition:** others' acknowledgment of a person's or group's worth, achievements or standing, from a word of credit to a formal honor or title.
**Facets:** credit and praise for achievement (esteem); recognition of equal standing or identity (the politics of recognition); formal recognition (titles, honors, diplomas and credentials); acknowledgment of wrongs (apologies, land acknowledgments); recognition withheld.
**Neighbors and opposites:** acknowledgment, credit, praise, approval, validation, appreciation, esteem; being ignored, neglect, misrecognition, erasure. **Excess:** craving approval, grabbing credit, flattery. **Deficiency:** ingratitude; unsung and thankless work.

- **Words:** "credit where credit is due", "shout-out", "props", "kudos", "pat on the back", "unsung hero", "thankless job", "glory hog", "steal the credit", "validation", "acknowledge", "honorable mention", "participation trophy"; titles of address (Dr., "sir" and "ma'am"); Spanish *reconocimiento* (in Mexico also the certificate or plaque itself), *mérito*, *palmadita en la espalda*, *lambiscón* and *barbero* (Mexican: flatterer), *hacer la barba*, *quedar bien*, *protagonismo*, professional titles as address in Mexico (*licenciado*, *ingeniero*, *maestro*, *doctor*), *don* and *doña*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is seeking recognition healthy or vain? Taylor's "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) and Honneth's three spheres of love, rights and esteem (*The Struggle for Recognition*, 1992); achieved against ascribed status (Trompenaars; Capricorn); good deeds done in secret ("let not thy left hand know", Matthew 6:3); whether credit goes to the individual, the team or the family.
- **Feeling and the body:** the glow of praise; the sting of being passed over; applause and standing ovations; whether public praise pleases or embarrasses (Mexican *pena* when singled out *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** thanking and crediting; employee recognition programs; awards banquets; likes and follower counts; reviews and ratings; thank-you notes; who is thanked for the meal at home.

**Key questions:** Who deserves recognition, and for what: achievement, existence or rank? Is seeking it a right or a vanity?

## 5. Being seen

**Working definition:** being noticed and perceived as one really is; the need to be witnessed, and the rules for who may be visible, when and to whom.
**Facets:** attention from others; being witnessed at life events and ceremonies; groups' visibility in public life and the media (representation); being known as oneself (intimacy, "feeling seen"); being watched (surveillance, the gaze, the evil eye); invisibility and erasure; anonymity chosen.
**Neighbors and opposites:** attention, visibility, witness, representation, the spotlight; invisibility, being ignored, anonymity, privacy. **Excess:** attention-seeking, exhibitionism, overexposure, surveillance. **Deficiency:** invisibility, neglect, erasure.

- **Words:** "I see you", "feel seen", "seen and not heard", "spotlight", "limelight", "attention-seeking", "pick me", "wallflower", "invisible", "visibility", "representation matters", "main character", "pics or it didn't happen"; Spanish *ningunear* (to treat someone as nobody; Paz on *ninguneo*), *invisibilizar* and *invisibilización*, *no me pela* (Mexican: pays me no attention), *hacerse notar*, *lucirse*, *apantallar* (to dazzle), *llamar la atención* (which in Mexico also means to scold, a telling double sense), *dar el avión* (Mexican: to humor someone without listening *check*), *protagonismo*, *mal de ojo*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is wanting to be seen natural or vain? "Children should be seen and not heard"; humility and anonymity (anonymous giving; Alcoholics Anonymous); the God who sees (Genesis 16:13); witnessing as a duty (the calling of witnesses at Coast Salish and other Northwest Coast gatherings, as the nations describe it *check*); whether visibility is power or danger (the closet; families who teach their children to keep their heads down).
- **Feeling and the body:** eye-contact norms (a direct gaze as respect or as challenge; lowered eyes as respect toward elders in some families and nations *check*); staring and "civil inattention" (Goffman, *Behavior in Public Places*, 1963); the evil eye and the *ojo de venado* amulet worn by children; stage fright and blushing; modesty and how much of the body may be seen.

- **Life course:** showing off babies; recitals and the parent in the audience; teenagers' "imaginary audience" (Elkind, 1967); being overlooked in old age; obituaries.
- **Ritual:** ceremonies with witnesses (marriage witnesses, *testigos*; witnesses at feasts, as the nations describe them); a child's *presentación* at church; the *quinceañera*'s entrance and the debutante ball; walking at graduation; vigils.
- **Institutions:** the census (undercounts of Hispanic people and of Native people on reservations in 2020 *check*); race and ethnicity categories (the 2024 revision of the federal standard, adding a Middle Eastern or North African category); missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls (Canada's National Inquiry, 2019; BC's "Highway of Tears"); media representation studies (UCLA's Hollywood Diversity Report; USC Annenberg's Inclusion Initiative; GLAAD); newsrooms and whom they cover; museums, school textbooks and heritage months that include or leave out groups.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** attention as reward (fame, likes, the spotlight); ignoring as sanction (shunning, the silent treatment, *ningunear*); public exposure as punishment (see humiliation); "attention-seeker" as an insult.
- **Expression:** Ralph Ellison's *Invisible Man* (1952); Matika Wilbur's Project 562, photographs of Native nations; murals that put a community on the wall; representation in film and television; Native-made film (*Smoke Signals*, 1998).
- **Exemplars:** those who made groups visible (Harvey Milk's call to come out); photographers (Edward S. Curtis, whose images of Native peoples are contested, and Native photographers' replies); influencers; anti-exemplars: the attention-seeker.
- **History:** Native peoples erased from maps, histories and the "vanishing" myth; Mexican American history left out of state histories; Asian Americans and Asian Canadians left out of local histories; coming out and "visibility" as a strategy of gay liberation; the MMIWG movement.
- **Measures:** census undercount estimates (the Post-Enumeration Survey); representation counts in media; social-media use (Pew, by group); loneliness surveys (*check* which); Gallup's "treated with respect" item; the items on being ignored in the Everyday Discrimination Scale (Williams et al., 1997).
- **Variation:** gender (who is looked at, and how); age; race (being overlooked or being watched); disability; sexuality; recent immigrants and their children; each Indigenous nation.
- **Relations:** recognition; self-expression; dignity; humiliation (unwanted visibility); secrecy and privacy (Scorpio); isolation and solitude (Tier 2); surveillance and control (Scorpio).
- **Who it applies to:** children; elders; the poor and the unhoused; the dead (remembered on Día de Muertos); animals; overlooked towns and regions; machines: whether people want machines to "see" them (cameras, facial recognition) and whether people feel seen by chatbots (present-day surveys *check*).
- **Silences:** the unhoused; domestic and farm workers; the disabled; the elderly in care homes.
- **Change:** social media and influencer culture; surveillance technology; representation campaigns; talk of a loneliness epidemic.

**Key questions:** Is being seen a need everyone has, or something to earn or avoid? Who may be visible, and when is visibility a danger?

## 6. Dignity

**Working definition:** the worth a person has as a person, and the bearing and treatment that honor it; usually held to be inherent rather than earned.
**Facets:** inherent human dignity; dignity in bearing (composure, self-possession); dignity at work and in poverty; dignity in illness, old age and death; the "dignity of risk" (choosing for oneself); dignity in public treatment (courtesy, respect); the dignity of peoples.
**Neighbors and opposites:** respect, self-respect, decency, decorum, worth; degradation, indignity, humiliation, objectification. **Excess:** stiffness, pomposity, "standing on one's dignity". **Deficiency:** degradation, self-abasement.

- **Words:** "dignity", "with dignity", "self-respect", "respect", "decent", "decency", "dignity of labor", "death with dignity", "dehumanizing", "treat people like people"; Spanish *dignidad*, *digno* and *vida digna*, *trabajo digno*, *vivienda digna* (decent housing, a common phrase in Mexican public life), *trato digno* (a standard in Mexican public services *check*), *respeto*, *darle su lugar* (to give someone their due place), *faltar al respeto*, *gente decente* (a phrase with a class history), *bien educado*, *la digna rabia* (a Zapatista phrase).
- **Beliefs and standing:** inherent dignity (Kant; the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 1; Catholic social teaching from *Rerum Novarum*, 1891, to *Dignitas Infinita*, 2024); dignity cultures against honor and face cultures (Leung and Cohen, 2011); Peter Berger's "On the Obsolescence of the Concept of Honor" (1970), on dignity replacing honor; whether dignity can be lost or only violated; dignity through work (Lamont, *The Dignity of Working Men*, 2000; Hodson, *Dignity at Work*, 2001); respect for all beings in Indigenous teachings, as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** composure in suffering; posture; being clean and well dressed when poor ("Sunday best"; *andar bien arreglado*); bodily dignity in hospitals and care homes (nakedness, being fed and washed); dignity in dying (Chochinov's dignity therapy, Manitoba).
- **Everyday practice:** forms of address (*usted*, titles, first names); courtesy to service workers; how the poor are treated at food banks (the "client choice" model); waiting in lines and waiting rooms; searches and ID checks; how elders are spoken to.
- **Life course:** teaching respect (*respeto* in Mexican families: Valdés, *Con Respeto*, 1996); children's dignity and spanking (whether parents and teachers still hit children, and what people think of it *check*); dignity in old age and in care homes; death with dignity.
- **Ritual:** funerals as dignity for the dead; burial of the unclaimed; reburial of ancestors returned by museums in the U.S. and Canada; the dignified transfer of soldiers' remains; caps and gowns.
- **Institutions:** hospitals, hospices and palliative care (dignity at the end of life; assisted dying where it is offered, and the doctors and patients who choose it or refuse it); care homes and home care for the old and the disabled; disability services and the "dignity of risk" (Perske, 1972); unions and the language of dignity at work (the United Farm Workers); shelters, food banks and church charities; Dignity Village, Portland's self-run camp for unhoused people (2000).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** admiration for grace under pressure; contempt for those who degrade others; "undignified" behavior mocked; apology and amends after an indignity.
- **Expression:** César Chávez's speeches on the dignity of farmworkers; Dorothea Lange's *Migrant Mother* (Nipomo, California, 1936); Chief Joseph's surrender words as recorded (1877; the transcription is disputed); *corridos* of the poor; the Zapatistas' language of dignity.
- **Exemplars:** César Chávez and Dolores Huerta; Fred Korematsu (see Aries); residential- and boarding-school survivors who testified; elders; anti-exemplars: those who degrade the weak.
- **History:** the farmworker movement; Japanese American and Japanese Canadian internment; residential and boarding schools, as survivors tell it (Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, 2015); the Bracero Program; the Dust Bowl migrants' treatment ("Okies"); the 1930s Mexican Repatriation.
- **Measures:** Gallup World Poll, "treated with respect all day yesterday" (country); Mexico's discrimination survey (ENADIS, INEGI; regions); the Everyday Discrimination Scale in U.S. surveys; Statistics Canada's discrimination questions (General Social Survey on Social Identity; Canadian Social Survey); assisted-dying reports (the Oregon Health Authority's annual reports; Health Canada's annual MAID reports).
- **Variation:** class ("decency" as a class marker); occupation; ethnicity; disability; age; each Indigenous nation; recent immigrants.
- **Relations:** honor (dignity's counterpart; overlap to settle); pride; humiliation (its violation); recognition (Honneth); justice (Libra); compassion (Pisces); autonomy (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** all humans or only some; the dead; animals (whether people grant animals dignity, as in the care of old working animals); nature (rivers and mountains as relatives in Indigenous teachings, as the nations state them); machines: present-day reactions to robots being abused (hitchBOT, a Canadian hitchhiking robot destroyed in Philadelphia in 2015; videos of robots being kicked).
- **Silences:** undignified care of the old; the dying poor; the daily indignities of poverty and of migrant life.
- **Change:** assisted dying becoming more common (Canada); dignity in the disability movement; "dignity" as a word in public life ("dignified" wages, housing and vacations; *vida digna*).

**Key questions:** Is dignity in every person by birth, or earned and kept by conduct? What may never be done to anyone, and who counts as "anyone"?

## 7. Humiliation

**Working definition:** being lowered in standing or worth against one's will, usually before others; also the act of lowering someone.
**Facets:** public shaming; ridicule and mockery; degradation (stripping, forced acts); defeat and how it is displayed; humiliation in love (being jilted or betrayed); collective humiliation of peoples and nations; lowering chosen (humility, penance); embarrassment, its mild form.
**Neighbors and opposites:** shame, embarrassment, ridicule, mortification, disgrace, degradation; honor, dignity, vindication, saving face. **Excess:** cruelty, bullying, a culture of public shaming. **Deficiency** (of the sense of shame): shamelessness (*sinvergüenza*).

- **Words:** "humiliate", "embarrass", "mortified", "put down", "roast", "dunk on", "own", "ratio", "walk of shame", "public shaming", "cancel", "loser", "eat crow", "lose face"; Spanish *humillar*, *vergüenza*, *pena* (Mexican: embarrassment, "me da pena"), *ridículo* ("hacer el ridículo"), *exhibir* (to expose publicly), *balconear* (Mexican: to expose someone's secrets), *quemar* (to ruin someone's name), *carrilla* (Mexican, especially northern: teasing, ribbing), the *albur* (a contest of double meanings with a loser), *chingar* and *el chingado* (Paz's reading of humiliation as violation, a classic claim), *ningunear*, *poner en su lugar*, *agachón*, *sinvergüenza*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is humiliating someone ever right (punishment, hazing that "builds character", humbling the proud)? Shame cultures against guilt cultures (Benedict, 1946, much criticized); Margalit, *The Decent Society* (1996): a decent society is one whose institutions don't humiliate; Christian humility as chosen lowering, and "turn the other cheek"; whether humiliation must be avenged (Scorpio) or forgiven; whether shaming is meant to cast a person out or to bring them back (shunning against forgiveness and return).
- **Feeling and the body:** blushing, lowered head, hidden face; "humiliated fury" (Helen Block Lewis, 1971); bodily humiliations (stripping, head-shaving, forced kneeling); the stress response to insult (Cohen et al., 1996).
- **Everyday practice:** teasing and *carrilla* among friends (where it is play and where it turns cruel); roasts; criticism at work, in public or in private; parents scolding in public; bullying at school and online; humiliation formats on reality television; online pile-ons; abuse of service workers.
- **Life course:** schoolyard bullying; hazing in fraternities, sports teams and the military; parents shaming children (including online); the humiliations of dependence in old age.
- **Ritual:** roasts; *lucha libre*'s bets of mask against mask and mask against hair, where the loser is unmasked or shaved; hazing rites; degradation ceremonies (Garfinkel, 1956), such as drumming out of a regiment and stripping of rank; Carnival's burning of "bad humor" (*quema del mal humor*, in Mexican carnivals *check* which); the charivari or "shivaree", the noisy mocking of a couple by their neighbors in rural North America.
- **Institutions:** schools and their anti-bullying programs; fraternities, sororities, sports teams and boot camps, where hazing lives; workplaces' rules on harassment and abusive bosses; social-media platforms and their rules on harassment and shared intimate images; reality television and the tabloid press as makers of humiliation; comedy clubs and the roast tradition (the Friars Club).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** humiliation is itself a sanction: who may use it (parents, teachers, coaches, drill sergeants, online crowds); sanctions on humiliators (punished bullies; "punching down" condemned); recovery after shaming (public apology; the comeback).
- **Expression:** *The Scarlet Letter* (1850); stand-up comedy and roast culture; "fail" videos and memes; *corridos* mocking rivals; insult duels in *albur* and in hip-hop battles; films of revenge for humiliation.
- **Exemplars:** those whose humiliation became a public cause (Amanda Todd of Port Coquitlam, BC; Olimpia Coral Melo of Puebla); comeback figures; the young men beaten and stripped in the Zoot Suit Riots; anti-exemplars: bullies, hazers.
- **History:** collective humiliations and how they are remembered: the 1846-1848 war and the loss of half of Mexico's territory, in Mexican schooling (*check* how it is taught); the Zoot Suit Riots (1943); internment; residential and boarding schools; the anti-Chinese riots and expulsions (Tacoma, 1885; Seattle, 1886; Vancouver, 1907); segregation signs aimed at Mexicans in the Southwest (*check* pilot cases); the suppression of the potlatch; contempt for the "Okies".
- **Measures:** bullying (CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey, by state; the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study in Canada; PISA's bullying measures for Mexico); INEGI's ENDIREH (emotional violence against women, including humiliation, by state) and its cyberbullying module (MOCIBA); the insult experiments (Cohen, Nisbett, Bowdle and Schwarz, 1996); workplace bullying surveys (*check* which); online harassment (Pew, 2021).
- **Variation:** gender (whether men's humiliation is tied to violence, women's to sexual reputation: questions to test); class; race; age; LGBTQ youth; where the line between joking and cruelty falls.
- **Relations:** honor and dignity (what it violates); pride (what it wounds); revenge and betrayal (Scorpio); anger (Aries); ridicule (near-synonym); shame (folded in here); being seen (unwanted visibility).
- **Who it applies to:** children, recruits, enemies, the defeated in sport and war; groups and nations; animals (the debates over bullfighting and rodeo); machines: people abusing robots and chatbots, and whether people feel humiliated by algorithms and automated rejections (present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** men's humiliation (job loss; sexual violence against men); humiliation within families; the humiliations of poverty and of migrant life.
- **Change:** online shaming and "cancel culture" debates; the sharing of intimate images, and the movements against it; apology and repair in schools and workplaces; anti-bullying programs.

**Key questions:** Is humiliation a fair punishment or a wrong in itself? Must it be avenged, endured or forgiven, and does that differ for men and women?

## 8. Generosity

**Facets:** charity to strangers; giving within family and kin (sharing, remittances); hospitality; gifts and their obligations; giving that makes standing (feasts, sponsorship, philanthropy); generosity of spirit (forgiving, praising, crediting); tipping.

**Key questions:** Is giving a free virtue, a duty of kinship, or a way to make standing? Should the giver be seen?

## 9. Leadership

**Working definition:** guiding a group so that others follow willingly, by example, persuasion, vision or standing; distinct from the formal right to command (authority, Capricorn).
**Facets:** charismatic leadership; servant leadership; leading by example; consensus and consultative leadership; hereditary and ceremonial leadership; elected leadership; informal leaders (the respected elder, the team captain, the eldest sibling); leadership in crisis; women's leadership.
**Neighbors and opposites:** guidance, influence, charisma, command, initiative; following, followership, leaderlessness. **Excess:** domination, the cult of personality, *caudillismo*, bossiness. **Deficiency:** passivity, abdication, a leadership vacuum.

- **Words:** "leader", "boss", "lead by example", "take charge", "servant leadership" (Greenleaf, 1970), "natural-born leader", "alpha", "bossy" (and the "Ban Bossy" campaign, 2014), "thought leader", "captain", "chief"; Chinuk Wawa *tyee* (chief, boss), still in regional English (the Tyee Club of Campbell River, for anglers *check*), and "high muckamuck" (said to come from Chinuk Wawa *check*); Spanish *líder*, *liderazgo*, *jefe* and *jefa* (in Mexican slang also one's father and mother), *patrón*, *el mero mero*, *mandamás*, *mandón*, *caudillo*, *cacique* and *caciquismo*, *dirigente*, *gallo*, *el que manda*, *autoridades tradicionales* and *gobernadores tradicionales* (the titles some nations in Mexico use for their own leaders, such as the Yaqui pueblos *check*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** are leaders born or made? Leaders who serve against leaders who command; consensus against decisive authority; humble leaders (Collins's "Level 5 leadership", *Good to Great*, 2001); the leadership profiles each society endorses in the GLOBE study (charismatic, team-oriented, participative, humane, autonomous, self-protective; House et al., 2004); *caudillismo* in Latin American political history, and the distrust of leaders who stay too long, voiced in the Revolution's slogan *Sufragio efectivo, no reelección*; religious models (the shepherd; Moses; the servant who washes feet); Indigenous governance as the nations describe it (hereditary chiefs whose names and privileges are confirmed at feasts; councils; consensus); whether women may lead, and where.
- **Feeling and the body:** the leader's bearing (voice, height, posture; research on height and leadership *check*); charisma; calm in crisis; the leader's dress (the suit, the *guayabera*, regalia).
- **Everyday practice:** who leads at work, in the family (who decides), in church, on teams, in neighborhood associations; how meetings run (consensus, Robert's Rules, the *asamblea*); group projects at school; who speaks first.
- **Life course:** student government; patrol leaders in Scouts; team captains; the eldest child as leader; the *jefe de familia*; leadership programs (corporate, Latino leadership institutes, tribal youth councils); elders as leaders.
- **Ritual:** inaugurations and swearings-in; the installation of chiefs and the passing of names, as the nations share them; change-of-command ceremonies; the staff of office (*vara de mando*) given to traditional authorities in Mexico; the presidential sash (*banda presidencial*); the State of the Union and the Mexican president's *informe*.

- **Rewards and sanctions:** status, pay and legacy; CEO pay ratios; sanctions on failed or abusive leaders (voting them out, removal of chiefs, shunning, mockery); "bossy" as a sanction on women; envy of leaders; loyalty and its betrayal.

## 10. Play

**Working definition:** activity done for its own sake, set apart from ordinary life by rules or make-believe: games, sport as play, pretending, joking, toying.
**Facets:** children's free play; games of skill, chance, make-believe and vertigo (Caillois's four kinds, 1958); adult play (hobbies, games, sport, gambling); verbal play (joking, teasing, wordplay); play in music and theater; digital play; playfulness as a trait.
**Neighbors and opposites:** fun, games, recreation, leisure, sport, humor; work, seriousness, boredom, duty. **Excess:** frivolity, addiction (gambling, gaming), immaturity. **Deficiency:** joylessness, the over-scheduled childhood, "all work and no play".

- **Words:** "play", "fun", "game", "recess", "goof off", "horse around", "play hooky", "playdate", "free play", "work hard, play hard", "all work and no play makes Jack a dull boy", "kid at heart", "gamer", "spoilsport"; English *play* covers games, music and theater, where Spanish splits *jugar*, *tocar* and *actuar*; Spanish *juego*, *relajo* (playful disorder; Jorge Portilla, *Fenomenología del relajo*, 1966), *desmadre*, *cotorrear* and *cotorreo*, *echar relajo*, *pachanga*, *mitote* (from Nahuatl *check*), *recreo*, *kermés*, *lotería*, *matatena*, *canicas*, *trompo*, *balero*, *papalote*, *piñata*, the *albur*, *carrilla*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is play waste or necessity? ## 11. Honor

**Working definition** (honor placed under Leo by the author, 2026-11-02): worth held in others' eyes and one's own, won and kept by conduct (keeping one's word, courage, loyalty, sexual propriety) and lost by insult or failure; it can be defended.
**Facets:** personal honor (good name, the word given); family honor (*honra*, often tied to women's conduct); masculine honor and the answer to insult; honor as integrity (honor codes, the honor system); honor as rank and title (honors; honorifics); collective honor (of a nation, a regiment, a team); honoring others (elders, the dead, guests).
**Neighbors and opposites:** reputation, integrity, respect, face, glory, chivalry; dishonor, disgrace, shame, infamy. **Excess:** feuds, violence over insults, honor used to control women. **Deficiency:** shamelessness (*sinvergüenza*), dishonesty, cowardice.

- **Words:** "honor", "honorable", "on my honor" (the Scout Oath), "word of honor", "a man's word is his bond", "honor system", "honor code", "honor roll", "dishonor", "disgrace", "respect" and "disrespect" ("dissing"), "save face", "chivalry", "gentleman"; Spanish *honor* and *honra* (standing against virtue and good name, a distinction worked through in Spanish Golden Age drama, such as Calderón's *El médico de su honra*, 1637), *deshonra*, *palabra de honor*, *hombre de palabra*, *cumplir*, *quedar bien* and *quedar mal*, *dar la cara*, *sinvergüenza*, *caballero* and *caballerosidad*, *hombría*, *honores a la bandera*, *rendir honores*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** honor, dignity and face cultures (Leung and Cohen, 2011); the herding theory of Southern honor (Nisbett and Cohen, *Culture of Honor*, 1996) and its critics; Mediterranean honor and shame (Peristiany, ed., *Honour and Shame*, 1965, with Pitt-Rivers); honor in colonial Spanish America (Johnson and Lipsett-Rivera, eds., *The Faces of Honor*, 1998; Gutiérrez, *When Jesus Came, the Corn Mothers Went Away*, 1991, on New Mexico); Berger's thesis that dignity replaced honor (1970); Appiah, *The Honor Code* (2010); traditional machismo against *caballerismo* (Arciniega et al., 2008); honor in the street (Anderson, *Code of the Street*, 1999); honoring and being honored among Indigenous nations, as they describe it.

- **Exemplars:** people known for keeping their word; soldiers (the Nisei of the 442nd, whose motto was "Go for Broke"); Chief Joseph; *charros*; Joaquín Murrieta (contested); anti-exemplars: traitors, cheats, oath-breakers; La Malinche as a contested figure of betrayal in Mexican memory.
