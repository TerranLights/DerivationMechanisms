# Avenues of investigation: 12 Pisces (the hidden and transcendence)

**What this file is.** For each Pisces concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Pisces concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. Pisces reaches into ceremony, healing and the sacred more than any other sign, so the rule bites hardest here: where a nation keeps a practice closed, the avenue is the nation's own public statement about it, and nothing more.

**Two notes on scope.** Crime is researched as a cultural concept: what people count as wrong, and how they see those who do wrong. Folk magic, divination and astrology appear only as things people do and believe (magic ways); their claims are never evidence.

**The concept list** (not yet settled; it is settled with the author just before Pisces's pass): compassion, empathy, self-sacrifice, dissolution, boundary, escapism, spirituality, surrender, intuition, crime, fear. Crime and fear are the author's named examples, placed under Pisces by the author (2026-11-02). Questions to settle:
- **Boundary** is in a near-synonym group with *boundaries*. One entry here covers both personal boundaries and the lines a culture draws between groups and places.
- **Surrender** is in a group with *release* and *letting go*. All three are researched under surrender here.
- **Compassion and empathy** are two entries here: empathy is feeling or grasping what another feels; compassion is being moved to relieve suffering. Whether to keep both is the author's call.
- **Fear** borders the *worry* and *anxiety* group. Both are researched here as fear's diffuse forms unless the author places them elsewhere.
- **Dissolution** needs the author's working definition: self-loss and merging (the reading used first below), or the ending of any fixed form (marriages, groups, institutions), which is covered too.
- **Escapism:** addiction is researched under it here; the author may want addiction as its own entry.
- **Secrecy** is in Pisces's ten too; its proposed home is Scorpio (cross-reference at the end).

## 0. The domain: the hidden and transcendence

**Traditional areas:** the hidden and the unconscious, confinement and institutions, sacrifice, compassion, transcendence. **The domain question:** how does the culture meet suffering, the unseen and the wish to escape?

- **Suffering and its meaning.** Whether suffering is punishment, test, redemption, bad luck, injustice or a medical problem; "everything happens for a reason"; Catholic redemptive suffering ("offer it up"); *Dios aprieta pero no ahoga*; *aguantar* as a virtue (shared with Aries's courage); stoicism against therapy culture; who may complain of pain, and to whom. Measures: the Gallup World Poll's negative-experience items (worry, sadness, pain; country); frequent mental distress (CDC PLACES, census tract); suicide (CDC WONDER by county; Statistics Canada; INEGI's suicide statistics); "deaths of despair" (Case and Deaton, 2020); opioid dispensing by county (CDC's dispensing-rate maps *check*).
- **The hidden and the unconscious.** Whether people believe in an unconscious at all, and in what form (the soul, the heart, the subconscious, spirits); dream talk: whether dreams are told at breakfast, read as messages, used to pick lottery numbers, or dismissed; dream teachings as the nations share them; the reach of psychoanalysis (Erich Fromm's years in Mexico, 1950-1973, and the Mexican psychoanalytic institute he founded *check*; the C. G. Jung Institute of San Francisco *check*); the public face against the private self.
- **Mental health.** Words for madness and distress ("crazy", "nuts", "psycho", "mental", *loco*, *nervios*, *estar mal de los nervios*, *depre*); folk illnesses (see magic ways); stigma and help-seeking; who the distressed are sent to (priest, *curandera*, counselor, psychiatrist, Elder, no one). Measures: NSDUH state and substate estimates of mental illness; Mental Health America's state rankings; Statistics Canada's Mental Health and Access to Care Survey (2022); INEGI's self-reported well-being survey (ENBIARE, 2021); the Mexican psychiatric epidemiology survey (Medina-Mora et al., 2003); psychologists and psychiatrists per capita.
- **Sleep.** Sleep hours (CDC PLACES short-sleep estimates by tract; the American Time Use Survey); naps and the *siesta*; "I'll sleep when I'm dead"; co-sleeping (Cancer's child-rearing ways); night as dangerous or sacred.
- **Confinement and institutions.** Goffman's "total institutions" (*Asylums*, 1961): who is confined, where, and in what words.
  - psychiatric hospitals and their closing (deinstitutionalization, from the 1960s): Oregon State Hospital, where *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest* was filmed (1975); BC's Riverview Hospital (closed 2012); the link people draw to homelessness;
  - wartime internment: Manzanar, Tule Lake and Minidoka; Hastings Park and the BC interior camps;
  - residential and boarding schools, in the nations' own voices first: the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (2015); the Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc announcement at Kamloops (2021); Chemawa Indian School (Salem), Sherman Institute (Riverside), Stewart Indian School (Carson City); the U.S. Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative reports (2022, 2024) and the presidential apology (2024);
  - quarantine and leprosaria (D'Arcy Island off Victoria, 1891-1924 *check*); nursing homes; monasteries and convents as chosen confinement;
  - measures: U.S. Census group-quarters counts by county (nursing homes, psychiatric hospitals and other institutions); Statistics Canada's collective dwellings; INEGI's population in collective housing (*viviendas colectivas*, Censo 2020).
- **The unseen.** Belief in ghosts, spirits, angels, demons, the intercession of saints, ancestors, the evil eye; haunted places and their tourism (the Winchester Mystery House in San Jose; the Queen Mary in Long Beach; Portland's "Shanghai tunnels" legend); *aparecidos* and *espantos*. Measures: Pew's 2023 survey of spirituality (*check*); the paranormal items of the Chapman University Survey of American Fears; Gallup's paranormal poll (2005); Angus Reid on paranormal belief (*check*); the Mexican survey of religious beliefs and practices (ENCREER, RIFREM, 2016 *check*).
- **Transcendence outside religion.** Awe (Keltner's research at Berkeley); nature as sacred: John Muir, the Sierra Club (San Francisco, 1892), wilderness as cathedral, old-growth forests, the ocean; the sublime in Western landscape painting and photography (Bierstadt, Ansel Adams); peak experiences (Maslow) and flow (Csikszentmihalyi, later at Claremont); surfing and mountaineering as spiritual; transcendence through technology (transhumanism in the Bay Area; present-day attitudes only). Main treatment: spirituality, dissolution, surrender.
- **Sacrifice and compassion** are researched under self-sacrifice and compassion.
- **Magic ways** (Fischer's folkway for Pisces). How the culture draws the line between religion, magic and science, and what people do about luck, harm and hidden forces:
  - folk healing: *curanderismo* (Trotter and Chavira, *Curanderismo*, 1981), *limpias* with eggs or herbs, *sobadores*, *parteras*; folk illnesses (*susto*, *mal de ojo*, *empacho*, *caída de mollera*, *aire*); *botánicas* and *yerberías* and what they sell; Native medicine only as the nations share it;
  - protective objects and acts: crucifixes and rosaries on rearview mirrors, the *ojo de venado* charm, horseshoes, lucky numbers, knocking on wood, Friday the 13th;
  - divination: tarot, palm-reading, psychics, Ouija boards, the I Ching, and astrology studied as a practice people use (how many consult it, who, why), never as a source;
  - witchcraft beliefs and accusations: *brujería*, *mal puesto*, hexes; witchcraft accusations in colonial New Spain, as historians have studied them; Wicca and modern paganism (the Reclaiming tradition and Starhawk's *The Spiral Dance*, 1979; the Feri tradition; both in the Bay Area);
  - folk saints and unofficial devotions: Santa Muerte; Jesús Malverde; Niño Fidencio (Nuevo León); Juan Soldado (Tijuana);
  - New Age: Mount Shasta (Lemuria lore; the "I AM" Activity of the 1930s); crystals; energy healing; psychic fairs;
  - the unexplained: Kenneth Arnold's 1947 sighting near Mount Rainier (the origin of "flying saucer"); the McMinnville photographs (1950) and the town's UFO festival; Sasquatch, a name taken from Halq'eméylem as the Stó:lō publish it (*check* spelling), and "Bigfoot country" in northern California;
  - measures: Pew on astrology, tarot and fortune-tellers (2025 *check*); Chapman's paranormal items; Gallup (2005); botánicas and psychic businesses per capita from business directories (*check*).
- **Order ways** (runner-up; home sign Capricorn): how people keep order by custom and handle disorder among themselves (shaming, shunning, gossip, mediation by elders or clergy). Crime (section 10) carries the Pisces side; see `10 - Capricorn.md`.

## 1. Compassion

**Working definition:** being moved by another's suffering and wanting to relieve it; mercy toward those in one's power.
**Facets:** compassion for the suffering stranger; mercy toward offenders, debtors and enemies; pity; charity and almsgiving; hospitality to the needy; compassion for animals; self-compassion; compassion fatigue; compassion as public policy (the safety net, programs for the poor and the sick).
**Neighbors and opposites:** mercy, pity, sympathy, kindness, charity; cruelty, callousness, indifference, contempt. **Excess:** enabling, pity that humiliates, sentimentality, burnout. **Deficiency:** hard-heartedness; compassion fatigue turned cold.

- **Words:** "compassion", "mercy", "pity", "sympathy", "kindness", "heart" ("has a big heart", "heartless"), "bleeding heart" (a sneer), "do-gooder", "tough love", "there but for the grace of God go I", "be kind"; Spanish *compasión*, *misericordia* (the religious word: *obras de misericordia*), *lástima* (*me da lástima*: a pity that can look down; *no quiero que me tengan lástima*), *piedad*, *pobrecito* (the diminutive of pity, constant in Mexican Spanish), *caridad*, *compadecerse*, *de buen corazón*, *echar la mano*, *apapachar* (to comfort with embraces; often traced to Nahuatl *check*). Where each word sits on the line between respect and condescension.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is compassion a duty, a virtue, a weakness or a political stance? The works of mercy; the Good Samaritan; Matthew 25 ("the least of these"); Catholic social teaching and the "preferential option for the poor" of Latin American liberation theology; Jewish *tzedakah*; mercy in Islam; compassion in East Asian Buddhism (Guanyin and Kannon; Thich Nhat Hanh's Deer Park Monastery in Escondido); the deserving and the undeserving poor; belief in a just world (Lerner) as a brake on compassion; Adam Smith's sympathy (*The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, 1759); Nietzsche's and Ayn Rand's attacks on pity; mercy against justice (Libra).
- **Feeling and the body:** tears, touch, warmth; who may cry for strangers; research on compassion and the body at Berkeley's Greater Good Science Center; compassion fatigue and burnout in nurses, social workers, first responders and clergy (Figley's work *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** giving to panhandlers or not; food banks, soup kitchens and church pantries; crowdfunding medical bills (GoFundMe); meals brought after a death; visiting the sick and prisoners (works of mercy); rescuing animals; helping strangers after disasters; how neighbors treat unhoused people and encampments.
- **Life course:** "be kind" teaching; canned-food drives; the Scouts' good turn; First Communion and the works of mercy; service hours that some schools require for graduation (*check*); midlife volunteering; hospice volunteering; whether the old are pitied or respected.
- **Ritual:** Christmas giving and Thanksgiving food drives; Las Posadas (re-enacting Joseph and Mary's search for shelter); Lenten almsgiving; Ramadan's charity; candlelight vigils for victims; memorial walks; feasts where hosts give to guests, as the nations describe them (generosity's home is Leo).
- **Institutions:** the welfare state and whom it serves; charities (Catholic Charities, the Salvation Army, Feeding America, Food Banks Canada, the Mexican food-bank network BAMX); parish social ministries and church pantries; homelessness services (Housing First programs; shelters; street outreach); refugee sponsorship (Canada's private sponsorship program, since 1979); congregations that shelter migrants; animal welfare (the San Francisco SPCA, 1868; the no-kill movement); hospital charity care; hospices; nursing and social work as professions of care; the Charter for Compassion and the cities that signed it (Seattle *check*); Vancouver's Insite (2003), North America's first supervised injection site; disaster appeals and telethons in the media.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise ("salt of the earth", *buena gente*), humanitarian awards; sanctions on too much ("bleeding heart", "enabler", "virtue signaling", "soft on crime"); on too little ("heartless", "Scrooge", *sin corazón*); the stigma of being pitied.
- **Expression:** the Good Samaritan in sermons; Dickens's *A Christmas Carol*; Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939); Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973); proverbs (*hoy por ti, mañana por mí*); *corridos* and *rancheras* about the poor; animal-rescue advertising; kindness memes.
- **Exemplars:** Dorothy Day and the Catholic Worker houses; Dolores Huerta and César Chávez; Father Greg Boyle (Homeboy Industries, Los Angeles); José Andrés (World Central Kitchen); nurses and hospice workers; Fred Rogers; anti-exemplars: the miser, the cruel boss; contested: missionaries (Junípero Serra; the missions' charity and coercion).
- **History:** the missions; Depression relief and the Dust Bowl migrants, and the "bum blockade" that turned the poor back at California's borders (1936); neighbors who kept farms for interned Japanese Americans (Bainbridge Island; Guterson's *Snow Falling on Cedars*, 1994, is set on a fictional island like it); Canada's 1979-1980 welcome of Southeast Asian refugees; the 1985 Mexico City earthquake and the civil society that rose from it; the 1980s sanctuary movement; the Downtown Eastside's harm-reduction movement; COVID-19 mutual aid.
- **Measures:** altruism (Global Preferences Survey, country); the World Giving Index (CAF, country); GLOBE's humane orientation; Moral Foundations care scores; charitable deductions by county and ZIP code (IRS Statistics of Income); volunteering (the Census and AmeriCorps civic-engagement supplement); Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on Giving, Volunteering and Participating; Mexico's national survey of solidarity and volunteering (ENSAV, ITAM *check*); field experiments on helping strangers in U.S. cities (Levine et al., 1994) and across countries (Levine, Norenzayan and Philbrick, 2001); organ-donor registration by state (Donate Life America); the Self-Compassion Scale (Neff) in regional samples (*check*); GSS items on spending for the poor.
- **Variation:** religious and secular; politics (whom compassion is for: Waytz et al., 2019, on the moral circle); class (Berkeley studies of class and generosity, Piff et al., 2010, since debated *check*); gender; rural and urban giving; immigrants giving through remittances and hometown associations.
- **Relations:** empathy (feeling with); self-sacrifice; care and protection (Cancer); generosity (Leo); justice and mercy (Libra); crime (mercy toward offenders); boundary (compassion fatigue); forgiveness (no home sign yet).
- **Who it applies to:** strangers, migrants, wrongdoers, addicts, enemies; animals (rescue; factory-farming debates); the land; whether people pity robots (reactions to robots being kicked; HitchBOT, a Canadian hitchhiking robot destroyed in Philadelphia in 2015); AI offered as comfort (present-day attitudes to chatbots in mental-health support).
- **Silences:** compassion for people who have committed sex crimes; men in need; the "undeserving"; caregivers' burnout; pity felt as insult.
- **Change:** encampment sweeps and the backlash to them; Housing First against treatment-first; crowdfunding as a safety net; secular charity replacing church charity; the "toxic empathy" debate (see empathy).

**Key questions:** Is compassion owed to everyone, or only to the deserving? Does it flow through the family, the church, the state or one's own hand? Does it reach wrongdoers?

## 2. Empathy

**Working definition:** feeling or grasping what another person feels or thinks, from their side.
**Facets:** emotional empathy (feeling with); cognitive empathy (perspective-taking, reading minds); empathic concern; empathy across group lines; empathy as a professional skill (medicine, nursing, teaching, design, customer service); empathy in politics and public life; empathy for animals and fictional people.
**Neighbors and opposites:** sympathy, understanding, perspective-taking, attunement, sensitivity; coldness, indifference, callousness. **Excess:** emotional contagion, overwhelm, losing one's own view, partiality (empathy for the near over the far). **Deficiency:** obliviousness, being "tone-deaf".

- **Words:** "empathy" (coined in English in 1909 by Titchener for German *Einfühlung*), "empath", "put yourself in someone's shoes", "walk a mile in their shoes" (and its "moccasins" form, often attributed to Native sources without evidence *check*), "I feel you", "relatable", "read the room", "emotional intelligence", "snowflake" and "too sensitive" (sneers); Spanish *empatía*, *ponerse en los zapatos del otro*, *ponerse en su lugar*, *ser sensible*, *buena onda*, *me sentí feo por él* (*check*); *simpatía* as a cultural script of warmth and smooth relations (Triandis, Marín, Lisansky and Betancourt, 1984), not the English "sympathy".
- **Beliefs and standing:** is empathy a moral foundation, a skill, a weakness or a bias? Hume and Smith on sympathy; the Golden Rule; Paul Bloom's *Against Empathy* (2016); recent claims among some U.S. Christian writers that empathy can be a trap or a sin (*Toxic Empathy*, 2024 *check*); therapy culture's "validation"; Carl Rogers's client-centered therapy (later based in La Jolla).
- **Feeling and the body:** mirroring of faces and posture; emotional contagion; tears at others' pain; whether men may show it; reading faces; burnout from feeling too much.
- **Everyday practice:** active listening; "I" statements and Nonviolent Communication (Marshall Rosenberg); customer-service scripts ("I understand how frustrating that is"); design thinking's "empathize" step (IDEO and Stanford's d.school); bedside manner; trauma-informed care; trigger warnings; perspective-taking in arguments; reading novels; online pile-ons as failed empathy.
- **Life course:** "how would you feel if..."; social-emotional learning (Second Step, from Seattle's Committee for Children; CASEL); Roots of Empathy (Toronto, 1996; in BC schools *check*); Mexico's *Construye T* (SEP); empathy training in medical school and its measured decline during training (the Jefferson Scale of Empathy *check*); empathy in old age.
- **Ritual:** moments of silence; vigils; "walk a mile" events (*check*); simulations (poverty simulations, aging suits); restorative circles in schools and communities, where those who did harm hear those they harmed; the TRC's national events (2010-2014).
- **Institutions:** crisis lines (988 in the U.S., 2022; 9-8-8 in Canada, 2023; Mexico's Línea de la Vida); counseling, social work and chaplaincy as professions trained in empathy; hospitals' patient-experience surveys, which ask whether nurses and doctors listened carefully (HCAHPS *check*); hiring for "emotional intelligence"; customer-service metrics; human-interest journalism.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise ("emotional intelligence", "a good listener"); sanctions ("too soft", "snowflake", "bleeding heart"); men's empathy mocked as unmanly or praised as a new manhood; "tone-deaf" as the sanction on its absence.
- **Expression:** the novel as training in empathy (Lynn Hunt, *Inventing Human Rights*, 2007; Suzanne Keen, *Empathy and the Novel*, 2007); Steinbeck; Dorothea Lange's *Migrant Mother* (Nipomo, 1936); documentary photography and film; virtual reality sold as an "empathy machine" (2015); songs of others' lives (Woody Guthrie's "Deportee", on the 1948 plane crash near Los Gatos).
- **Exemplars:** Fred Rogers; Carl Rogers; Dorothea Lange; teachers and nurses; counter-exemplars: the cold boss; Ted Bundy as the culture's image of empathy's absence.
- **History:** the eighteenth-century culture of sensibility and the abolition movement; the Dust Bowl photographs; televised war (Vietnam); the residential-school testimony at the TRC and what non-Native Canadians were asked to feel; the shift from "sympathy" to "empathy" in common speech (Google Books Ngram).
- **Measures:** empathic concern and perspective-taking in 63 countries (Chopik, O'Brien and Konrath, 2017); the decline in U.S. students' empathy (Konrath, O'Brien and Hsing, 2011); the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (Davis, 1980) in regional samples; the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test across countries; racial bias in judging others' pain (Hoffman et al., 2016); GLOBE's humane orientation; Google Trends for "empathy" and "empath" by state.
- **Variation:** gender (self-report gaps larger than measured ones *check*); class and empathic accuracy (Kraus, Côté and Keltner, 2010 *check*); politics (whom people empathize with); age; neurodiversity (the "double empathy problem", Milton, 2012); high-context settings where reading others is expected, against low-context ones where people say what they feel (Hall; Gemini).
- **Relations:** compassion (empathy feels; compassion acts); intuition (reading people); boundary (contagion and burnout); dissolution (merging with another); trust (Scorpio); communication (Gemini); justice (Libra).
- **Who it applies to:** outgroups and enemies; wrongdoers and those they hurt; animals; fictional people; robots (Kate Darling's studies of empathy for robots; the response to HitchBOT's destruction); AI that sounds empathic (a UC San Diego study rated chatbot answers to patients more empathic than doctors', Ayers et al., 2023; present-day attitudes only).
- **Silences:** empathy for perpetrators; men's feelings; empathy that stops at the border or the color line.
- **Change:** "empath" as an identity online; empathy in corporate leadership talk; the backlash against empathy in politics and religion; AI companions.

**Key questions:** Whose feelings is one expected to share, and whose not? Is empathy a virtue, a skill, or a danger to judgment?

## 3. Self-sacrifice

**Working definition:** giving up one's own interests, comfort, health or life for another person, a group, a cause or God.
**Facets:** parents' sacrifice for children (and the immigrant parents' story of sacrifice); the mother's self-denial; sacrifice for country (soldiers, first responders); martyrdom, religious and political; rescue at the risk of death; hunger strikes and fasts; religious self-denial (fasting, Lent, *mandas*); sacrifice for the company or the team; taking another's blame or punishment; organ and blood donation; family caregiving.
**Neighbors and opposites:** selflessness, devotion, abnegation, martyrdom, altruism; selfishness, self-interest, self-care. **Excess:** self-erasure, the martyr complex, codependence, sacrifice that breeds resentment or demands repayment. **Deficiency:** selfishness, "looking out for number one".

- **Words:** "sacrifice", "selfless", "martyr" (praise, and the sneer "don't be a martyr"), "the ultimate sacrifice", "laid down his life", "take one for the team", "take the fall", "fall on one's sword", "give the shirt off one's back", "people-pleaser", "doormat"; Spanish *sacrificio*, *sacrificarse*, *abnegación* and *madre abnegada* (the self-denying mother, a key word of Mexican family ideals), *sufrida*, *entrega*, *darlo todo*, *dar la vida por*, *partirse el lomo*, *echarse la culpa*, *hacerse la mártir* (to play the martyr).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is sacrifice love, duty, or a sign of low self-worth? Christian sacrifice (the Cross; John 15:13 on laying down one's life for one's friends); Lent; redemptive suffering; Catholic martyrdom (the Cristero martyrs, canonized in 2000); Latter-day Saint tithing, fast offerings and missions; Ayn Rand's rejection of sacrifice (*The Virtue of Selfishness*, 1964); effective altruism in the Bay Area (*check*); *marianismo* as Stevens defined it (1973) and the critiques of the term; Díaz-Guerrero's studies of Mexican family premises, with the mother's self-sacrifice among them (*check*); the soldier's ethos; therapy culture's suspicion of sacrifice.
- **Feeling and the body:** fasting, kneeling, walking on one's knees to a shrine; exhaustion as proof of love; the caregiver's worn body; hunger strikes; giving blood and organs; pride and resentment.
- **Everyday practice:** parents working two jobs; sending remittances; eldest daughters raising siblings; caring for aging parents at home; unpaid overtime; volunteering in disasters; giving blood; giving up a career for a spouse's; Lenten sacrifices ("what are you giving up?").
- **Life course:** what children are told about their parents' sacrifice (the "immigrant bargain", repaid with success; Smith, *Mexican New York*, 2006 *check*); eldest children; enlistment at eighteen; Latter-day Saint missions at eighteen or nineteen; parenthood; caregiving at midlife; grandparents raising grandchildren; obituaries that praise one who "lived for her family".
- **Ritual:** Memorial Day, Remembrance Day, and Mexico's Day of the Niños Héroes (September 13); Mother's Day (May 10 in Mexico) and its serenades; Holy Week and the *Viacrucis*; *mandas* fulfilled at shrines (pilgrims at the Basilica of Guadalupe on December 12); memorials for fallen firefighters and police; the monthly fast among Latter-day Saints.
- **Institutions:** military honors for those killed or wounded (the Purple Heart; Canada's Memorial Cross); line-of-duty death benefits; the Carnegie Hero Fund's awards to rescuers, including those who died; organ- and blood-donation programs and campaigns (Donate Life; Canadian Blood Services; Mexico's national transplant center, CENATRA *check*); paid family leave programs (California's, 2004, the first state's); Mexico's satellite account valuing unpaid household work against GDP (INEGI); caregiver-support programs; the military's customs around sacrifice (casualty notification, the folded flag).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** honor, medals, posthumous praise, sainthood; "she's a saint"; sanctions on selfishness ("selfish", *egoísta*, *mal hijo*); and on too much ("martyr complex", "codependent"); the debt that sacrifice creates (*después de todo lo que hice por ti*).
- **Expression:** Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973), on a city's happiness bought by one child's suffering; war memorials; *corridos* of soldiers and revolutionaries; telenovelas of the long-suffering mother; the self-denying mother of Mexican golden-age cinema (Sara García's roles); Hollywood war films; sermons.
- **Exemplars:** César Chávez's fasts (1968, 1972, 1988); the 2013 California prison hunger strike that began at Pelican Bay; Pat Tillman, who left the NFL and was killed in Afghanistan in 2004 (contested after the cover-up of his death; a San Jose native *check*); wildland firefighters killed on the line; living kidney donors; Father Damien of Molokai; the Cristero martyrs; counter-exemplars: the selfish heir, the absent father.
- **History:** the missions' ideal of sacrifice; the Bracero Program (1942-1964) and the men who left families to earn for them; WWII rationing and the Nisei soldiers; the *soldaderas* of the Mexican Revolution; Latter-day Saint settlement of the Great Basin; COVID-19 health workers called "heroes".
- **Measures:** willingness to fight for one's country (World Values Survey); altruism (Global Preferences Survey); living organ donors by state (OPTN); military deaths by home state (the Defense Casualty Analysis System); caregiving (AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving, *Caregiving in the U.S.*; ATUS eldercare); Statistics Canada's GSS on Caregiving and Care Receiving (2018); INEGI's time-use survey (ENUT) and unpaid-work satellite account; remittances by state and municipality (Banco de México); blood-donation rates (*check* for regional data); Carnegie Medal awards.
- **Variation:** gender (who is expected to sacrifice; the *madre abnegada*); immigrant generation; religion; class; military families; birth order; each Native nation's teachings on giving for the people, as the nation states them.
- **Relations:** compassion; surrender (giving oneself up); dissolution; duty (Capricorn); care, motherhood and family (Cancer); honor and generosity (Leo); courage (Aries); reciprocity (Libra: sacrifice that expects a return).
- **Who it applies to:** soldiers, mothers, children, workers; animals (service animals; animal sacrifice in religion, a contested topic); machines sent in place of people (bomb-disposal robots; present-day attitudes to "sacrificing" machines); companies asking workers for sacrifice.
- **Silences:** resentment of sacrifice; the costs to caregivers; sacrifices asked of daughters and not sons; sacrifices left unhonored (Native and Black soldiers in some eras); suicide framed as sacrifice.
- **Change:** "self-care" against sacrifice; "eldest daughter" talk online; fewer people serving in the military; "quiet quitting" as refusal to sacrifice for work.

**Key questions:** Who is expected to sacrifice, and for whom? Is sacrifice the proof of love, a duty, or self-erasure?

## 4. Dissolution

**Working definition** (proposed; the author decides): the coming apart of a fixed form. First, the loss of the self's edges in something larger (another person, a crowd, nature, the divine); second, the ending of bonds, groups and identities.
**Facets:** ego loss in mystical, ecstatic or psychedelic experience; merging in love; losing oneself in a crowd, in music or in ritual; flow; the self coming apart in dementia and death; the ending of marriages, partnerships, companies, congregations, towns and nations; assimilation and the loss of a language or identity.
**Neighbors and opposites:** merging, union, oneness, "ego death", letting go, melting, assimilation; individuation, integrity (holding together), boundaries, identity. **Excess:** loss of self, cults, dissociation, collapse. **Deficiency:** rigidity; a self that can never let go.

- **Words:** "lose oneself", "become one", "merge", "melt", "ego death", "oceanic feeling" (Freud, *Civilization and Its Discontents*, 1930, after Romain Rolland), "come undone", "fall apart", "the melting pot" (from Zangwill's 1908 play), "assimilate", "breakup", "split"; Spanish *disolverse*, *fundirse*, *perderse en*, *desintegración familiar* (a common Mexican phrase for family breakdown), *mestizaje* and Vasconcelos's *La raza cósmica* (1925) as an ideal of fusion, *media naranja* (one's other half).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is losing the self holy, healthy, dangerous or sick? ## 5. Boundary

## 9. Intuition

**Working definition:** knowing or judging without conscious reasoning: the gut feeling, the hunch, the sense for people and situations.
**Facets:** gut feelings in decisions; reading people (social intuition); expert intuition (the firefighter's or the nurse's sense that something is wrong); premonitions and second sight; intuition against data; spiritual discernment; "women's intuition"; street smarts.
**Neighbors and opposites:** instinct, hunch, insight, discernment, common sense, street smarts; analysis, deliberation, evidence, data. **Excess:** jumping to conclusions, prejudice, superstition, paranoia. **Deficiency:** naivety, overthinking, ignoring warning signs.

- **Words:** "gut feeling", "gut instinct", "hunch", "sixth sense", "vibe" and "bad vibes", "red flag", "street smarts", "read the room", "women's intuition", "trust your gut", "follow your heart", "I knew it in my bones", "Spidey sense", "second sight"; Spanish *intuición*, *corazonada*, *presentimiento*, *sexto sentido*, *olfato*, *me late* (Mexican: I have a hunch), *me da mala espina*, *tener buen ojo*, *ojo clínico*, *tener colmillo* (Mexican: shrewd from experience), *malicia* (street savvy).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is intuition a gift, a skill, a bias or the voice of God? Enlightenment reason against Romantic feeling; Pascal's "the heart has its reasons"; Ignatian discernment of spirits (taught at the Jesuit universities: Santa Clara, the University of San Francisco, Loyola Marymount, Gonzaga, Seattle University); Quaker "leadings"; Damasio's somatic markers (*Descartes' Error*, 1994; Damasio is at USC); Kahneman's two systems (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011); Klein's studies of expert intuition (*Sources of Power*, 1998); "two-eyed seeing" (Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall) as an example of a nation's published way of joining knowledge systems; "intuitionism" in American political thinking (Oliver and Wood, *Enchanted America*, 2018).
- **Feeling and the body:** the gut; goosebumps; hair standing up; "something felt off"; a racing heart; whether bodily signals are trusted or dismissed.
- **Everyday practice:** hiring by gut ("culture fit") against structured interviews; dating ("chemistry", "red flags"); parents' sense that something is wrong; nurses' "worried" calls; mechanics' and pilots' feel for trouble; investing and gambling hunches; scouts' "eye test" against analytics (the Oakland Athletics' "Moneyball" season, 2002); choosing whom to trust on the street.
- **Life course:** "kids and dogs know"; mothers' intuition; the grandmother who knows (*la abuela*); expert intuition built through years of work; elders' wisdom.
- **Ritual:** divination as folk practice (see magic ways); praying for guidance; telling dreams; discernment retreats; consultations with *curanderas*.
- **Institutions:** evidence-based medicine (from McMaster University, 1990s) against clinical judgment; hospitals' rapid-response teams, which a nurse may call because she is worried (*check*); structured interviews in hiring against "culture fit"; sports teams' analytics departments against their scouts; the Jesuit universities' teaching of discernment; business schools' teaching on decisions; psychic hotlines and television mediums as businesses.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for "good instincts" and "street smarts"; "trust your gut"; sanctions: "paranoid", "superstitious", "woo", "you're overthinking"; blame when the hunch was prejudice.
- **Expression:** Gladwell's *Blink* (2005); detective fiction and noir hunches; Star Wars ("trust your feelings"; Lucasfilm in Marin County); television psychics and mediums; proverbs (*más sabe el diablo por viejo que por diablo*); *corridos* of men who sensed betrayal.
- **Exemplars:** fictional detectives, firefighters (Klein's studies), nurses, grandmothers, *curanderas*; counter-exemplars: con-artist psychics; Theranos (Palo Alto), where investors trusted a founder over data.
- **History:** nineteenth-century Spiritualism and mediums in San Francisco (*check*); the rise of statistics and management science; Silicon Valley's data culture; evidence-based medicine; the analytics turn in sport.
- **Measures:** the Cognitive Reflection Test (Frederick, 2005) in national and regional samples (*check*); the Faith in Intuition scale (Pacini and Epstein, 1999); Gallup's 2005 poll on ESP; Chapman's items on psychics; Pew on consulting psychics (*check*); holistic and analytic thinking (Nisbett, *The Geography of Thought*, 2003; Talhelm et al., 2014, on rice and wheat regions in China); trust in scientists against one's own judgment (Pew); intuitionism items (Oliver and Wood); "algorithm aversion" (Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey, 2015).
- **Variation:** gender ("women's intuition"; Judith Hall's studies of reading nonverbal cues *check*); education and class (book smarts against street smarts); politics (intuitionism crosses left and right); age; profession; religion.
- **Relations:** empathy (reading others); spirituality; fear (gut fear); trust (Scorpio); truth (Sagittarius); analysis and discernment (Virgo), its opposite pole; courage and recklessness (Aries: acting on a hunch).
- **Who it applies to:** animals (dogs said to sense illness or earthquakes, in folklore and research); children; women; elders; the dying (deathbed visions); present-day attitudes to trusting algorithms over the gut, and to AI said to have "intuition".
- **Silences:** gut feelings that are bias (sizing up strangers by race); confident eyewitnesses who were wrong; experts' hunches that failed.
- **Change:** data-driven decision-making; AI recommendations; wellness culture's "trust your intuition"; "manifesting".

**Key questions:** When the gut and the data disagree, which wins, and in whose hands? Is intuition a gift of women, elders, experts or God, or a source of prejudice?

## 10. Crime

**Working definition** (named by the author: what it means for something to be a crime): what a culture counts as a serious wrong against the community, deserving public condemnation and punishment, and how it sees those who commit it.
**Facets:** what counts as crime (the wrongs people condemn hard, and the ones they shrug off); the image of the criminal (born, made, chosen, sick, desperate, evil); victims; crime and sin; shame and guilt; crimes of the powerful (white-collar, corporate, official); the outlaw as hero; fear of crime (see fear); the wish to punish, forgiveness and redemption; the stigma that follows a wrongdoer; impunity; crime in stories and songs.
**Neighbors and opposites:** wrongdoing, offense, sin, vice, delinquency, corruption; uprightness, innocence, virtue. **Excess** (too much crime in the culture's eyes): moral panic, seeing wrongdoers everywhere. **Deficiency:** impunity, tolerated wrongdoing.


**Law:** DCI researches law only in Capricorn (`10 - Capricorn.md`, section 8) and Libra (`07 - Libra.md`). Crime is researched here as the culture's idea of wrongdoing and of the wrongdoer, citing that law research rather than repeating it.
- **Words:** "crime", "criminal", "crook", "lowlife", "convict", "ex-con", "thug", "outlaw", "gangster", "perp", "bad apple", "white-collar crime" (Sutherland, 1939), "victimless crime", "snitch"; Spanish *delito* (the general, formal word) against *crimen* (a grave crime, often murder in everyday Mexican usage *check*), *delincuente*, *maleante*, *malandro*, *ratero*, *rata*, *transa* and *transero*, *chueco*, *fayuca* (contraband goods), *huachicol* (stolen fuel), *mordida* (a bribe), *narco*, *sicario*, *halcón* (a lookout), *levantón* (an abduction), *cobro de piso* (extortion), *coyote* and *pollero* (people smugglers), *impunidad*, *cifra negra* (crime never reported or investigated), *autos chocolate* (cars smuggled in from the U.S.); terms in Native languages only as nations publish them (in their dictionaries and teachings).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is crime sin, sickness, choice, or the product of conditions? "Born criminal" theories (Lombroso) and their echo in talk of "bad seeds"; whether people can change ("once a thief, always a thief" against the reformed sinner); shame and guilt as checks on wrongdoing (Benedict's contrast of shame and guilt cultures, *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword*, 1946, a claim to test, not a finding); Catholic teaching (visiting prisoners as a work of mercy); evangelical prison ministry (Prison Fellowship, 1976) and its conversion stories; the tolerance in *el que no tranza no avanza* (Aries); social bandits (Hobsbawm, *Bandits*, 1969); each nation's teachings on wrongdoing and its repair, as the nation shares them; wrongs people condemn hard (wage theft, price gouging, elder abuse) and wrongs many shrug off (jaywalking, file-sharing, small drug use).
- **Feeling and the body:** moral outrage, disgust, fear, fascination; shame and guilt; tattoos read as criminal (gang tattoos; the teardrop); clothing read as criminal (zoot suits, 1943; *cholo* style; hoodies); the body of shame (lowered eyes, a bowed head, blushing).
- **Everyday practice:** locking doors and cars; doorbell cameras, Nextdoor and the Citizen app; window bars and walls; private guards; gossip about who did what, and shaming in the neighborhood and online; whether victims report a crime at all, and why not (ENVIPE asks: wasted time, distrust); tolerated petty wrongs (speeding, fare-dodging, tax cheating, the small *mordida*); pirated goods; street vending and how neighbors see it; cannabis growing in the Emerald Triangle; poaching (abalone, timber).
- **Life course:** what children are taught counts as wrong (stealing, lying, cheating) and how; "kids will be kids" against the "superpredator" scare of the 1990s; how schools treat troublemakers; the *Scared Straight!* film (1978) and its idea that fear keeps youths in line; youths recruited by gangs and cartels; aging out of crime; whether families and neighbors take a wrongdoer back.
- **Ritual:** vigils and roadside memorials for victims of violence; public shaming (the stocks in colonial times; online shaming now); confession and penance; testimonies of redemption in churches (the former gang member's story); offerings at Jesús Malverde's chapel in Culiacán and at his shrines elsewhere; narco funerals and mausoleums (Culiacán's Jardines del Humaya cemetery *check*); *linchamientos* (mob punishment) in Mexico as rituals of popular justice (*check* the scholarship).
- **Institutions:** churches and ministries that work with gang members and their families (Catholic and evangelical outreach; Homeboy Industries, Los Angeles); victims' groups (Mothers Against Drunk Driving, founded in California in 1980; the *madres buscadoras*, mothers' collectives that search for Mexico's disappeared); Mexico's National Search Commission for the disappeared (2018 *check*); neighborhood watch groups and online neighborhood forums; street outreach and violence-interruption programs (*check* local names); victim-services programs (*check* names by place); the press's crime beat and the *nota roja* papers; schools' anti-gang and anti-drug programs.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** informal sanctions: family shame, shunning, gossip, retaliation, "snitches get stitches" and codes of silence; the shame that falls on a wrongdoer's family; whether employers, landlords and churches give a second chance; the street prestige of the outlaw; rewards for informers (Crime Stoppers); redemption (Homeboy Industries: "nothing stops a bullet like a job").
- **Expression:** true crime (books, podcasts, television); Los Angeles and San Francisco noir (Hammett, Chandler, Ellroy); Westerns and their outlaws; outlaw country (Merle Haggard and the Bakersfield sound); *corridos* and *narcocorridos* (Los Tigres del Norte, from San Jose, "Contrabando y traición" *check* year; Chalino Sánchez); *corridos tumbados* and the public fight over narco-music (*check*); *nota roja* journalism (*¡Alarma!*); narco telenovelas; *Grand Theft Auto*'s Los Santos; murals; gang films.
- **Exemplars:** contested outlaws: Joaquin Murrieta, Tiburcio Vásquez, D. B. Cooper (1971), Bill Miner (BC, 1904 *check*), Jesús Malverde; Juan Soldado, a soldier executed in Tijuana in 1938 and venerated since; villains: the Green River Killer, the Zodiac killer, Robert Pickton; the redeemed (Danny Trejo, the Los Angeles actor known for his turn from crime to film and drug counseling); victims the public mourned (Polly Klaas, 1993; Rebecca Schaeffer, 1989).
- **History:** San Francisco's Committees of Vigilance (1851, 1856); the expulsions of Chinese residents from Tacoma (1885) and Seattle (1886), crimes that went unpunished; the panic over Mexican American youth in wartime Los Angeles (the Sleepy Lagoon murder, 1942; the Zoot Suit Riots, 1943); Prohibition rum-running across the BC and Mexican borders; the Hells Angels (Fontana, 1948) and the Mexican Mafia (1957); the crack era and the 1992 Los Angeles uprising; Tijuana's cartel era and the 1994 assassination of Luis Donaldo Colosio there; Mexico's drug war from 2006; violence in Los Cabos (*check* years); the regalia taken after the 1921 Village Island potlatch and their return home (the U'mista Cultural Centre); missing and murdered Indigenous women (BC's Highway of Tears; the National Inquiry, 2019; Washington's alert system *check*).
- **Measures:** the World Values Survey's items on whether cheating on taxes, dodging a fare or taking a bribe can ever be justified (country; region where coded *check*); the National Crime Victimization Survey; Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on Canadians' Safety; INEGI's ENVIPE (victimization, the *cifra negra*, trust; by state) and ENSU (city, quarterly); INEGI homicides from death records (municipal); INEGI's ENCIG on corruption in dealings with government; Gallup on whether people think crime is rising; Transparency International (country); true-crime audiences (podcast charts; Google Trends by state).
- **Variation:** race (who gets called a criminal); class (white-collar against street crime); immigrants (the "immigrant crime" belief, tested against data); gender; Native peoples, as the nations speak of harm and healing; urban and rural; border towns; generation; politics.
- **Relations:** justice and fairness (Libra); revenge, secrecy, betrayal and power (Scorpio); order and authority (Capricorn); honesty (Sagittarius); fear; compassion (mercy); boundary (trespass); escapism (drug use); self-sacrifice (taking the fall).
- **Who it applies to:** children; people with mental illness; corporations (PG&E, blamed for the 2018 Camp Fire that destroyed Paradise); the state (California's 2019 apology for violence against Native peoples, and Governor Burnett's 1851 call for a "war of extermination"); animals (the "bad dog", and whether an animal can be guilty); present-day attitudes to who is to blame when a driverless car hurts someone (Cruise in San Francisco, 2023).
- **Silences:** domestic violence once kept private; clergy abuse and the long silence around it (the Archdiocese of Portland); crimes against Native people; Mexico's disappeared (over 100,000 registered *check*); unreported crime; wage theft.
- **Change:** the shifting view of drug use, from crime to illness and back (cannabis; drug use in Oregon and BC); retail-theft debates; fentanyl; cybercrime; deepfakes; the turn to repair over punishment (restorative practices in schools and communities).

**Key questions:** What does the culture count as real crime, and whose wrongs does it overlook? Is the wrongdoer a sinner, a patient, a product of circumstance or an enemy? Can he come back?

## 11. Fear

**Working definition:** the emotion felt at danger, real or imagined, and a culture's ways of naming, showing, managing and using it. Worry and anxiety are researched here as its diffuse forms.
**Facets:** physical fear; fear of crime; fear of the supernatural; fear of God; social fear (shame, ridicule, rejection); anxiety and worry; collective fear and moral panics; fear of outsiders; fear of disaster; fear of the future (the economy, climate, technology); fear as pleasure (horror); fear as a tool (in child-rearing, advertising and politics).
**Neighbors and opposites:** dread, terror, anxiety, worry, panic, awe; courage, calm, security, trust. **Excess:** panic, paranoia, phobia, xenophobia. **Deficiency:** recklessness (Aries), complacency.

- **Words:** "fear", "afraid", "scared", "freaked out", "creepy", "spooky", "dread", "anxiety", "worry", "panic", "terror", "God-fearing", "stranger danger", "FOMO", "chicken" (Aries); Spanish *miedo*, *temor* (and *temor de Dios*), *susto* (a fright, and a folk illness), *espanto* (a fright, and in Mexico a ghost), *pavor*, *pánico*, *angustia*, *ansiedad*, *nervios*, *me da cosa*, *me dan ñáñaras* (the creeps *check*), *miedoso*, *coyón* (a coward), *gallina*; *la migra* as a word that carries fear.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is fear wisdom, weakness, sin or illness? "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom"; Franklin Roosevelt's "nothing to fear but fear itself" (1933); "No Fear" as a Southern California brand and slogan (*check*); stoicism; therapy and exposure; *susto* as soul loss in Mesoamerican folk medicine (Rubel, O'Nell and Collado-Ardón, *Susto, a Folk Illness*, 1984); fear of God against love of God; Paz's claim that Mexicans mock death (a claim to test); teachings about danger as the nations share them.
- **Feeling and the body:** startle, freezing, fleeing; goosebumps (*piel de gallina*); pallor; display rules for fear (Matsumoto); men's hidden fear; anxiety disorders; *susto* as illness; sleeplessness.
- **Everyday practice:** locking doors; guns kept for protection; alarm systems; avoiding places at night; not letting children walk alone; earthquake kits and wildfire go-bags; insurance; checking the news; horror films and haunted houses; avoiding *la migra*.
- **Life course:** frightening children into obedience (*el Coco*, *el Cucuy*, *La Llorona*, the bogeyman, the *robachicos*); "stranger danger" teaching (from the 1980s); children's fears; teenage anxiety; elders' fear of crime and falls; fear of death.
- **Ritual:** Halloween and haunted houses; Día de Muertos; earthquake drills (the Great ShakeOut, begun in Southern California in 2008; Mexico's national drills on September 19 *check*); fire and lockdown drills; tsunami-siren tests; exorcisms and *limpias* to remove fright.
- **Institutions:** emergency management (FEMA, Cal OES, Emergency Management BC, *Protección Civil*); early-warning systems (Mexico's SASMEX; the West Coast's ShakeAlert, public from 2019); Amber Alerts; the color-coded terror alerts (2002-2011); earthquake insurance (the California Earthquake Authority and its low take-up *check*); home-security companies and doorbell-camera networks (Ring); schools' safety drills and counselors; anxiety treatment (exposure therapy; the Anxiety and Depression Association of America *check*); churches' preaching on the fear of God; local news and its crime and disaster coverage.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for facing fear; "fearmongering"; "snowflake" and *coyón* as sanctions; caution rewarded in some settings, mocked in others.
- **Expression:** the horror film and its Hollywood industry; *La Llorona* and other *leyendas*; ghost stories of missions and mines; urban legends; local television news ("if it bleeds, it leads"); Mike Davis's *City of Quartz* (1990) and *Ecology of Fear* (1998) on Los Angeles; Barry Glassner's *The Culture of Fear* (1999); *Twin Peaks*; *The Shining* (exteriors at Timberline Lodge, Oregon); *ex-votos* thanking saints for rescue from danger.
- **Exemplars:** first responders; those who spoke out despite fear; fearmongers; Chicken Little; *La Llorona* as a figure of dread.
- **History:** earthquakes (the 1700 Cascadia earthquake, recorded in Japanese tsunami records and in oral histories as the nations share them; 1906; 1971; Mexico City, 1985; 1989; 1994; central Mexico, 2017); wildfires (Paradise, 2018); the "Yellow Peril" and wartime internment; the Red Scare and the Hollywood blacklist; nuclear fear and the Nevada Test Site's downwinders; the Satanic Panic (*Michelle Remembers*, written in Victoria, 1980; the McMartin preschool accusations in Manhattan Beach, from 1983); 9/11; COVID-19.
- **Measures:** the Chapman University Survey of American Fears (annual since 2014); Gallup's "afraid to walk alone at night" (U.S.; the Gallup World Poll's Global Safety Report); INEGI's ENVIPE and ENSU on perceived insecurity (state and city); Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on feelings of safety; anxiety in CDC's Household Pulse Survey (state, 2020-2024); NSDUH; the National Survey of Children's Health; the YRBS item on persistent sadness; Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance; the Gallup World Poll's worry item; gun sales, estimated from background-check counts (NICS, by state); earthquake-preparedness surveys; Pew on concern about AI (2023); Google Trends for "anxiety".
- **Variation:** gender (the "fear-victimization paradox"); age; race (who is feared, and who fears being taken for a threat); immigrants and fear of deportation; urban and rural; politics (fears of different things); class.
- **Relations:** courage and recklessness (Aries); crime; escapism; surrender; security (Taurus); trust (Scorpio); protection (Cancer); worry and anxiety (near-synonym group); faith (Sagittarius).
- **Who it applies to:** wildlife (cougars, bears, sharks; wolves returning to Oregon and Washington); outsiders and migrants; the state; nature (the "Big One"); present-day fear of robots and AI (Pew; Chapman).
- **Silences:** men's fear; children's hidden fears; fear of deportation; fear that is never spoken of.
- **Change:** youth anxiety (Haidt, *The Anxious Generation*, 2024); climate anxiety (Hickman et al., 2021); AI anxiety; fear of mass shootings; fear of crime against measured crime.

**Key questions:** What does the culture teach people to fear, and what does it teach them not to fear? Is fear shown, hidden, laughed at or treated?
