# Avenues of investigation: 01 Aries (self and identity)

**What this file is.** For each Aries concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Aries concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (settled by the author, 2026-11-10): self-assertion, identity, autonomy, initiative, beginning, courage, independence, anger, competition, recklessness. Autonomy and independence are two entries.

## 0. The domain: self and identity

**Traditional areas:** the self, the body, appearance, beginnings, initiative. **The domain question:** what is a self here, and how does it assert itself?

- **What a self is.** Independent or interdependent selves (Markus and Kitayama, 1991); horizontal and vertical individualism and collectivism (Triandis); where the self is located: the individual, the family, the lineage or house, the clan and its crests, the land, the faith community, the trade. Studies that ask people to complete "I am..." twenty times show what people name first (*check* for regional studies).
- **Personhood.** When a person becomes a person (birth, naming, baptism, a first birthday, a first ceremony) and when they stop being one; the standing of the unborn, the dead, ancestors, animals and spirits.
- **The body.** Bodily ideals; fitness and outdoor culture; manual labor and the pride of a working body; modesty and nudity norms; tattoos and piercing (Pew's tattoo surveys *check*); cosmetic surgery (the American Society of Plastic Surgeons' regional figures *check*); sun, skin and colorism; hair.
- **Appearance.** Dress codes at work, school and worship; first impressions; how far appearance should show the self against hide it; regional dress (outdoor gear as everyday wear in the Pacific Northwest; *vaquero* and *norteño* dress); pageants; makeup and grooming norms by gender.
- **Naming ways** (Fischer's folkway for Aries):
  - first names: how common or rare (the Social Security Administration's baby names by state; British Columbia's and other provinces' name lists; Mexico's civil registry data *check*); Varnum and Kitayama (2011) found rarer names in frontier regions, a measure of individualism;
  - naming after kin, saints, virtues, places, celebrities; Spanish double surnames; whether women keep their names at marriage;
  - Indigenous names and naming ceremonies, as the nations share them; hereditary names that carry rank and privileges on the Northwest Coast;
  - nicknames and pet names; renaming (immigrants anglicizing names; reclaiming names; transitions);
  - names of businesses, teams, streets and places as identity statements.
- **Sport ways** (runner-up; home sign Leo): individual sports against team sports as a statement of the self.
- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* personal names and naming customs; kin terms (what one is called by each relative); how the language says "I" (dropped, softened, or replaced by a title or kin term); greetings and self-introductions; the people's origin stories; body decoration (tattoo, paint, piercing, scarring) and what each mark says; hairstyles and their meanings; dress and ornaments that mark age, status, marriage or mourning; masks; portraits in carving and painting; beliefs about the shadow, the soul and reflections; dream accounts (who one is in dreams); rites that make a new self; praise and boasting songs; songs owned by one person; personal crests and emblems, as the nations share them; the gesture people use to point to themselves (chest or nose); posture and bearing; children's role-play.
  - *Writing:* autobiographies and memoirs; diaries; letters, and how people sign them and describe themselves; spiritual autobiographies; confessions; epitaphs; personal mottoes; commonplace books; conduct books on self-presentation; poetry in the first person; family crests and heraldry.
  - *Public records:* census forms and the categories they offer; the fields on identity cards (what the state thinks defines a person); name-frequency statistics; school records; national costumes; portraits on money and stamps; citizenship study materials.
  - *Mass media:* magazine profiles and interviews; celebrity culture; fashion and fitness magazines; makeover shows; confessional scenes in reality TV; personality quizzes in magazines; portrait conventions in photography (smiling or solemn); baby-name books.
  - *Online:* bios; profile pictures and avatars; selfies and filters; usernames; "about me" pages; shared personality-test results; dating profiles; the characters people build in games; influencers' self-presentation.
  - *Commerce:* monograms and personalized goods; beauty and cosmetics sales; tattoo studios' portfolios; fashion retail; cosmetic-surgery marketing; personal-branding consultants; baby-name websites; school picture day and portrait studios.
  - *The people who handle it:* tattoo artists; barbers and hairdressers (and the talk in their chairs); portrait photographers; stylists; cosmetic surgeons; therapists (self-concept); career coaches; registrars of births; clergy at naming and baptism.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' first impressions of how people present themselves; guides telling newcomers how to introduce themselves; old ethnographic portraits, read critically.
  - *Scholarship and data:* self-construal scales; "I am..." tests; personality surveys by region; name-frequency studies; body-image surveys; ethnographies of personhood; studies of self-enhancement and modesty; linguistic studies of pronoun dropping and honorifics.

## 1. Self-assertion

**Working definition:** declaring and acting on one's own existence, wishes and claims in the presence of others.
**Facets:** speaking up; saying no; claiming credit; standing one's ground; self-promotion; complaining; asking for what one wants.
**Neighbors and opposites:** assertiveness, confidence, voice; deference, modesty, self-effacement. **Excess:** domination, arrogance, entitlement. **Deficiency:** passivity, being a pushover.

- **Words:** "assertive", "pushy", "speak up", "stand your ground", "hold your own", "humble", "entitled"; "aggressive" used as praise in business; Spanish *igualado* (someone who acts as the equal of their betters: a key Mexican word for assertion against hierarchy), *dejado* (someone who lets themselves be pushed around), *atrevido*, *aguantar*; Canadian politeness markers ("sorry").
- **Beliefs and standing:** is speaking up a right, a duty or rude? Religious teaching on humility and meekness; Quaker plainness; the squeaky wheel against the nail that sticks out; assertion as self-respect or as disrespect.
- **Feeling and the body:** eye contact, voice volume, handshake, interrupting, personal space (Hall's proxemics), pointing, posture.
- **Everyday practice:** negotiating salaries and prices; returning goods and complaining; disputes with neighbors; who talks in meetings and classrooms; who asks whom out; haggling in markets (*tianguis*) against fixed prices.
- **Life course:** "use your words"; teaching children to stand up to bullies; participation grades; what daughters and sons are told.
- **Ritual:** occasions where speaking up is formalized: town halls and public comment, debates and speech contests, roasts and toasts, testimony in church or meeting (Quaker meeting, evangelical testimony), protest marches; speakers at Northwest Coast feasts, as the nations describe them.
- **Institutions:** performance reviews that reward "leadership"; customer-service norms ("the customer is always right"); consumer-complaint systems (Better Business Bureau; Mexico's PROFECO).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praised when standing up to bullies; punished as "uppity", *igualado*, the tall poppy; passive-aggression as a sanction; the "Karen" meme as a sanction on entitled assertion.
- **Expression:** proverbs; self-help and assertiveness-training books; anthems of self-assertion ("My Way"); advertising tone.
- **Exemplars:** people who spoke up (civil-rights and farmworker leaders; Native leaders' recorded speeches); anti-exemplars (bullies, the entitled).
- **History:** the Yankee town meeting (every man's voice); Spanish colonial hierarchy and deference; rank asserted at Northwest Coast feasts, as the nations describe them (colonial-era accounts, such as Codere's *Fighting with Property*, 1950, are contested).
- **Measures:** GLOBE assertiveness (country); Hofstede's masculinity; extraversion by state (Rentfrow); consumer-complaint rates per capita (*check*); salary-negotiation surveys by gender.
- **Variation:** gender; class; code-switching between ethnic and workplace norms; regional politeness ("the Seattle Freeze"); newcomers against old families.
- **Relations:** anger, competition, autonomy; politeness (Gemini); hierarchy (Capricorn).
- **Who it applies to:** children toward adults; workers toward bosses; women; minorities; customers.
- **Silences:** salary and money talk; challenging elders or clergy.
- **Change:** "setting boundaries" and therapy language; social-media self-promotion; backlash against both.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* recorded conversation (turn-taking, interruptions, overlapping talk); formulas for requests and refusals; boasting and self-praise traditions; verbal duels and insult contests; market haggling; children's quarrels and how adults settle them; who may speak at gatherings, and in what order (speaking-stick customs, as the nations share them); who greets first; gestures of dominance and deference (bowing, eye contact); proverbs on speaking up and keeping quiet; folktales of the bold and the meek; songs of defiance.
  - *Writing:* etiquette manuals (what counts as pushy); advice columns ("how do I stand up to..."); assertiveness and "saying no" self-help; negotiation books; complaint letters in archives; petitions and open letters; memoirs of standing up; sermons on humility and pride; conduct books that taught deference.
  - *Public records:* minutes of town meetings, school boards and church councils (who speaks, and how); transcripts of public comment at council meetings; consumer-complaint records; report-card comments ("needs to speak up"); classroom participation policies; student-council records.
  - *Mass media:* talk shows and call-in radio; conflict in reality TV; confrontations in soap operas and telenovelas; political debates and their limits on self-praise; trash talk in sport and at press conferences; letters to the editor; advertising tone (soft sell or hard sell).
  - *Online:* reviews and their complaint styles; "am I wrong?" forums; call-out posts; comment sections; viral confrontation videos; memes that punish entitlement; self-promotion on professional networks.
  - *Commerce:* customer-service scripts; sales training; negotiation workshops; salary surveys (who negotiates); return policies and return rates; tipping disputes.
  - *The people who handle it:* customer-service and restaurant workers (their "customers from hell" stories); negotiation trainers; human-resources staff; teachers of shy pupils; therapists who teach assertiveness; coaches; flight attendants; union organizers.
  - *Outsiders:* culture-shock and business-culture guides on directness; expatriates' blogs; orientation materials for immigrants ("here you must speak up").
  - *Scholarship and data:* conversation analysis; politeness research; cross-cultural studies of requests and apologies; the ethnography of speaking; assertiveness surveys; studies of gender and negotiation.

**Key questions:** Is asserting oneself self-respect or disrespect? Toward whom is it allowed, and toward whom never?

## 2. Identity

**Working definition:** what a person or group takes itself to be: the answer to "who are you?"
**Facets:** personal, family, ethnic, regional, national, religious, occupational, generational and gender identity; chosen against given; hyphenated identities; identity stamped by deeds rather than found by looking inward.
**Neighbors and opposites:** self, belonging, heritage, roots; anonymity, alienation, identity crisis. **Excess:** tribalism, exclusion. **Deficiency:** rootlessness.

- **Words:** what people answer to "who are you?": a name, a job ("what do you do?"), a hometown, a family (*¿de quién eres hijo?*), a nation, house and clan (Northwest Coast introductions, as the nations' protocols describe them); regional self-labels ("Pacific Northwesterner", "West Coast", "Californian", "Cascadian", "Lower Mainland", *norteño*, *sudcaliforniano*, *choyero* *check*); "Chicano", "Mexican-American", "Latino", "Hispanic", *pocho* (a Mexican's word for a Mexican-American who has lost Spanish), *malinchismo* (preferring the foreign).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is identity given (blood, birth, land) or made (choice, deeds)? Reinvention (California as the place to remake oneself); authenticity ("be yourself"); ancestry against place.
- **Feeling and the body:** identity worn on the body: tattoos, dress, hair, regalia, jewelry; skin color and colorism.
- **Everyday practice:** introductions (what people say first); bumper stickers and license plates; regional merchandise (the Cascadia "Doug flag"); sports fandom; food as identity; heritage language at home.
- **Life course:** heritage-language schools; learning one's clan, house and lineage, as the nations share it; *quinceañeras*, confirmations, bar and bat mitzvahs; the teenage identity crisis; college as identity-making.
- **Ritual:** heritage festivals; powwows and potlatches as the nations describe them; Mexican Independence Day (September 16) against Cinco de Mayo in the U.S.; Canada Day; the Fourth of July; Pride; Día de Muertos.
- **Institutions:** census self-identification (U.S. race and ethnicity questions; Canada's ethnic origin and Indigenous identity; INEGI's self-identification as Indigenous or Afro-Mexican, against counts of speakers of Indigenous languages); citizenship classes and naturalization ceremonies; ethnic and hometown associations (Mexican hometown clubs, *clubes de oriundos*; Nordic lodges such as the Sons of Norway *check*); churches that serve one people or language; ethnic and diaspora media; heritage museums and cultural centers; sports clubs and leagues tied to a people or a hometown.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** charges of inauthenticity ("whitewashed", *pocho*); false claims to Native identity and how nations respond; "passing"; pride and shame about origins.
- **Expression:** Chicano and borderlands literature (Anzaldúa, *Borderlands/La Frontera*, 1987); Northwest regional writing; songs of place ("California Dreamin'", *corridos*); slogans ("Keep Portland Weird"); flags and seals.
- **Exemplars:** figures a place says embody "us"; contested icons.
- **History:** statehood and provincehood; the Chicano movement; the Red Power and fish-wars era; Expo 86 in Vancouver; each migration wave's identity.
- **Measures:** census self-identification; surveys of primary identity (Environics, Angus Reid on regional identity in Canada; Pew on national identity); Gallup's 50-state poll on state pride (2013-2014 *check*); polls on Cascadian identity or independence (*check*); language retention across generations.
- **Variation:** immigrant generation; urban and rural; nation membership against pan-Indigenous identity; multiracial identity.
- **Relations:** belonging (Cancer); community (Aquarius); recognition (Leo); heritage and tradition (Capricorn).
- **Who it applies to:** places and companies (brand identity); whether people ascribe identity to AI and robots (present-day attitudes only).
- **Silences:** "where are you really from?"; ancestry questions; hidden origins.
- **Change:** rising multiracial identification; secular identities; online identities; debates over gender identity.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* what a people calls itself, and what it calls others; nicknames for a region's people; introduction customs (lineage, place, clan); recited genealogies; origin stories; crests and emblems, as the nations share them; dress and food that mark belonging; songs of place; dialect as a badge; jokes about neighboring groups; rituals of attachment to a place.
  - *Writing:* memoirs of mixed, hyphenated or changed identity; coming-of-age novels; family histories; diaspora literature; regional literature; editorials in ethnic newspapers; local and county histories (including the old county histories with settlers' biographies).
  - *Public records:* census questionnaires over time (the categories offered, and how they changed); national symbols, anthems and holidays; school heritage curricula; museum exhibits on "who we are"; official tourism branding.
  - *Mass media:* diaspora radio and television; regional news personalities; regional advertising; sports fandom; regional magazines; documentaries about "who we are".
  - *Online:* hashtags and regional communities; memes about where one is from; "you know you're from here when..." lists; identity quizzes; ancestry-test forums; heritage-language learners' communities; flags in bios.
  - *Commerce:* regional merchandise (T-shirts, decals, bumper stickers); ancestry DNA kits and their marketing; heritage tourism; ethnic grocery stores; regional food brands; the place names local businesses choose.
  - *Organizations:* ethnic and hometown associations; heritage and lineage societies; cultural centers; powwow committees, as the nations share them; language-revival programs; sports supporters' clubs.
  - *The people who handle it:* genealogists; curators of heritage museums; cultural-center staff; teachers of ethnic studies; tour guides (how they tell a place's story).
  - *Outsiders:* the names outsiders give the group, and how the group answers them; stereotypes in outside media; the "the people" sections of travel guides.
  - *Scholarship and data:* identity surveys; census self-identification over time; language retention across generations; ethnographies of ethnicity; studies of regional identity and dialect pride.

**Key questions:** Is identity found inside, inherited, or made by deeds? What comes first when a person introduces themselves?

## 3. Autonomy

**Working definition** (author, 2026-11-10): deciding for oneself: freedom from being controlled.
**Facets:** personal life choices; bodily autonomy; moral autonomy; autonomy at work; the autonomy of youth; collective autonomy: towns and Native nations governing themselves.
**Neighbors and opposites:** freedom, self-determination, sovereignty, consent; control, paternalism, obedience. **Excess:** refusal of every obligation. **Deficiency:** dependence on being directed.

- **Words:** "my choice", "my body, my choice", "it's a free country", "live and let live", "mind your own business", "don't tread on me", "self-determination", "sovereignty"; Spanish *autonomía*, *libre albedrío*, *cada quien su vida*, *usos y costumbres* (Indigenous self-government in Mexico).
- **Beliefs and standing:** who should choose a spouse, a career, a home: the person or the family? Attitudes to paternalism; ideals of consent; the ethics of self-determination.
- **Feeling and the body:** bodily autonomy (abortion, vaccination, assisted dying, drugs, tattoos, gender); who decides in medicine: the patient or the family (Blackhall et al., 1995, on Mexican-American and European-American attitudes to telling patients a diagnosis *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** choices of career, spouse and home; adult children living with parents; teenagers' privacy; homeschooling; living under homeowners' association rules; off-grid living.
- **Life course:** the age at which children choose their clothes, bedtimes, friends; curfews; "free-range" parenting and the neighbors who object to it; the age of leaving home (measurable: young adults living with parents, U.S. Census, Statistics Canada, INEGI).
- **Ritual:** the moments that grant self-rule: the eighteenth birthday, the first car, the first vote, leaving for college; independence days as celebrations of collective self-rule (July 4, September 16, Canada Day); sovereignty days as the nations observe them; Pride as a celebration of choosing one's own life.
- **Institutions:** local control of towns and schools (town meetings, school boards); the self-governing bodies of Native nations (elected councils, hereditary chiefs), as the nations describe them; school choice and homeschooling networks; church governance (congregations that run themselves against bishops who run them); remote and self-directed work; worker cooperatives; professions that set their own standards; self-directed care programs for disabled people and elders.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** mockery of "helicopter parents" and "control freaks"; busybodies; and on the other side, the family's sanction on a child who chooses alone.
- **Expression:** Oregon's motto *Alis volat propriis*, "she flies with her own wings"; the State of Jefferson and Cascadia independence movements; songs ("Don't Fence Me In").
- **Exemplars:** figures of personal and collective self-rule (sovereignty leaders; dissenters); contested ones.
- **History:** the Yankee town's self-government; the Spanish crown's and the missions' control over Native peoples; the West's rhetoric of autonomy against its dependence on federal money and water (Limerick; White); the fish wars; the *ejido*, communal farmland run by its own assembly.
- **Measures:** the World Values Survey item on how much freedom of choice and control people feel over their lives (by region where samples allow *check*); young adults living with parents; homeschooling share; share living under homeowners' associations (*check*); Pew privacy surveys; familism scales among Latino families (Sabogal et al., 1987).
- **Variation:** family-centered against individual decision-making; gender; age; religion.
- **Relations:** independence (its own entry); freedom (Sagittarius); duty (Capricorn); care and family (Cancer); community (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** children, the elderly, people with disabilities, Native nations as collectives, cities, companies; autonomous machines (present-day attitudes to self-driving cars, which run in San Francisco and Los Angeles; surveys by state *check*).
- **Silences:** unspoken family expectations; control inside families.
- **Change:** surveillance technology; remote work; fights over bodily autonomy; AI systems acting on their own.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* who chooses a spouse, and how courtship works; who speaks for the family; elders' councils and how they decide; stories of escape, defiance and runaways; proverbs on obedience and freedom; rites that grant adulthood; how freely children roam and play; taboos that limit personal choice.
  - *Writing:* religious teaching on free will, obedience and conscience; writing on liberty; memoirs of breaking away (from a faith, a family business, a community); coming-out stories; accounts of leaving communes and closed groups; disability self-advocacy writing; parents' letters of advice to grown children.
  - *Public records:* household surveys on who makes decisions (purchases, schooling, health care); time-use diaries (control over one's own time); records of who chooses a child's school path.
  - *Mass media:* advice columns on interfering parents; teen magazines; coming-of-age films; forbidden love and arranged marriage in telenovelas; the popularity of dystopian fiction; advertising that sells choice.
  - *Online:* forums on controlling parents, partners and employers; communities of people who left a faith or group; privacy debates; gig workers' forums; remote-work discussions.
  - *Commerce:* matchmaking and dating services; homeowners' association rules and newsletters; workplace-monitoring software and how it is sold; made-to-order and customized goods.
  - *The people who handle it:* family therapists; school counselors; hospital ethics consultants (who decides at the bedside: the patient or the family); social workers; matchmakers and marriage go-betweens; clergy who counsel families.
  - *Outsiders:* newcomers' guides on family decision-making; expatriates' observations.
  - *Scholarship and data:* surveys of felt freedom of choice; household decision-making modules in international surveys; young adults living with parents; ethnographies of family decision-making; medical anthropology of patient autonomy; self-determination research.

**Key questions:** Who decides about a person's life: the person, the family, the community or the state? Does the culture grant autonomy to groups (tribes, towns) as well as to individuals?

## 4. Initiative

**Working definition:** starting things and acting without being told; taking the lead unasked.
**Facets:** at work; in business (entrepreneurship); in civic life (starting groups, volunteering first); in politics (citizens starting petitions and ballot campaigns); in the home (helping without being asked).
**Neighbors and opposites:** enterprise, drive, being proactive; waiting for orders, inertia. **Excess:** overstepping, "cowboy" behavior. **Deficiency:** passivity.

- **Words:** "self-starter", "go-getter", "proactive", "can-do", "hustle", "take charge", "Just Do It" (Nike, founded in Oregon); Spanish *iniciativa*, *echarle ganas*, *ponerse las pilas*, *acomedido* (a Mexican word for someone who helps without being asked).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is acting unasked admirable or presumptuous? Is it expected of subordinates, children, women? Its link to the work ethic.
- **Feeling and the body:** energy and readiness as valued traits; restlessness.
- **Everyday practice:** suggestion systems and employee voice; volunteering first; starting clubs and nonprofits; starting businesses; gathering signatures for petitions and ballot campaigns; mutual aid; neighborhood watch; children who help unasked.
- **Life course:** chores done without asking; children's businesses; student government; Scouts.
- **Ritual:** groundbreakings, launch parties and ribbon-cuttings; startup demo days and pitch competitions; days of service (Martin Luther King Jr. Day); barn raisings and work bees.
- **Institutions:** chambers of commerce; small-business development centers and other public help for founders; startup incubators and accelerators; volunteer centers; service clubs (Rotary, Lions); tribal economic-development corporations; school programs in enterprise and leadership (Junior Achievement); workplaces that reward ideas from below.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** promotion, "employee of the month", volunteer awards; "who asked you?" as the sanction for overstepping.
- **Expression:** startup lore and garage myths (Hewlett-Packard, Apple); pioneer tales; *corridos* of self-made men; advertising slogans.
- **Exemplars:** founders of firms, towns, schools and language-revival programs; anti-exemplars: the meddler.
- **History:** the frontier's necessity; the gold rush; the Progressive-era movement for direct democracy in Oregon; Silicon Valley; border industry.
- **Measures:** the Census Bureau's Business Formation Statistics (state and county); the Kauffman Indicators of Entrepreneurship; self-employment (ACS); nonprofits per capita (IRS files); volunteering (the Census and AmeriCorps civic-engagement supplement); patents per capita (USPTO by county); ballot initiatives filed per state; Statistics Canada's business openings; INEGI's directory of new establishments (DENUE) and economic censuses; the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (country, some regions).
- **Variation:** entrepreneurship from need against from opportunity (GEM's distinction); immigrants; gender; class.
- **Relations:** beginning, courage, competition; work (Virgo); ambition and duty (Capricorn).
- **Who it applies to:** machines that act unasked (present-day attitudes to proactive AI).
- **Silences:** failures that followed initiative.
- **Change:** the gig economy; startup culture's spread and backlash.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* tales of the enterprising youngest child or the clever worker; proverbs about acting first or waiting; communal work parties and who calls them (barn raisings, *tequio*, *faena*); praise for children who help unasked; who leads a hunt, a catch, a harvest.
  - *Writing:* biographies of founders, pioneers and inventors; company founding stories; settlers' diaries of starting farms and businesses; religious teaching on providence against effort; conduct books on diligence; do-it-yourself manuals.
  - *Public records:* business-formation statistics; patent counts; volunteer-program records; disaster reports on who responded first; petitions and citizen campaigns; military doctrine (whether soldiers may act on their own judgment); youth-organization badge requirements; school leadership awards.
  - *Mass media:* "local hero" stories; business magazines and startup media; founders' myths in advertising (the garage); job ads ("self-starter"); pitch shows.
  - *Online:* crowdfunding launches; maker communities; side-hustle forums; volunteer sign-up platforms; who starts open-source projects, by region.
  - *Commerce:* job descriptions and performance-review criteria; employee awards; company values statements; suggestion schemes; pitch competitions and demo days; incubators.
  - *Organizations:* chambers of commerce; service clubs (Rotary, Lions); volunteer fire departments; mutual-aid networks; church volunteer rosters.
  - *The people who handle it:* recruiters and hiring managers (their interview questions); military trainers; volunteer coordinators; investors; teachers; coaches.
  - *Outsiders:* business-culture guides on hierarchy and initiative; expatriate managers' accounts.
  - *Scholarship and data:* entrepreneurship surveys; volunteering rates; workplace ethnographies; studies of proactive personality; studies of open-source participation.

**Key questions:** Is acting unasked a virtue or a presumption? Ahead of whom may one step?

## 5. Beginning

**Working definition:** how a culture treats starting: origins, firsts, new starts, and starting over after failure.
**Facets:** origins and founding myths; firsts (first settlers, first ascents); new years and seasons; birth; inaugurations and groundbreakings; the fresh start after failure, divorce, disgrace, addiction or migration.
**Neighbors and opposites:** origin, renewal, a clean slate; endings, continuity, tradition. **Excess:** restlessness, never finishing. **Deficiency:** inability to start over.

- **Words:** "fresh start", "clean slate", "turn over a new leaf", "start over", "pioneer", "go West", "reinvent yourself"; Spanish *empezar de cero*, *borrón y cuenta nueva*, *año nuevo, vida nueva*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** are second chances a right or is starting over running away? Being born again (shared with Scorpio's rebirth); progress against tradition; who was here first ("time immemorial") and why it matters.
- **Feeling and the body:** the excitement or fear of a new start; homesickness after one.
- **Everyday practice:** New Year customs (twelve grapes at midnight); housewarmings; groundbreakings and ribbon-cuttings; moving often; changing careers; divorce and remarriage; bankruptcy and its stigma.
- **Life course:** birth customs; naming (see the domain); first day of school; first communion; first haircut; first salmon ceremonies and other firsts, as the nations share them; first hunt; first job.
- **Ritual:** New Year's Day; Lunar New Year; Nowruz in Iranian Los Angeles; Indigenous new-year and solstice observances as shared; spring festivals; opening days (fishing season, baseball); statehood and founding days.
- **Institutions:** naturalization ceremonies; refugee-resettlement and newcomer services; community colleges and adult schools for people starting over; job-retraining programs; churches that baptize and receive converts; recovery fellowships (Alcoholics Anonymous); public help for first-time founders.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** "fail fast" against the stigma of failure; second-chance hiring; suspicion of newcomers.
- **Expression:** origin stories (creation stories as the nations share them; gold-rush lore; the California Dream); songs ("Go West"); films of starting over; immigrant arrival stories (Angel Island).
- **Exemplars:** pioneers, founders (contested ones, such as Junípero Serra), immigrants who rebuilt, comeback figures.
- **History:** each wave that came to start over (the Dust Bowl migrants, wartime workers, tech arrivals); Indigenous continuity against the frontier's story of a "new" land.
- **Measures:** moves in the past year and share born in another state or country (ACS; Statistics Canada; INEGI); bankruptcy filings by district; divorce and remarriage; business formation; fear of failure (Global Entrepreneurship Monitor, country).
- **Variation:** newcomers against old families; resentment of transplants ("Don't Californicate Oregon"; Governor McCall's "visit, but don't stay"); Indigenous against settler time.
- **Relations:** initiative, courage, recklessness; tradition (Capricorn); rebirth (Scorpio); hope (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** companies (rebrands), places (urban renewal), people living down their past.
- **Silences:** what was left behind; family secrets of origin.
