# Avenues of investigation: 06 Virgo (work and service)

**What this file is.** For each Virgo concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Virgo concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; Virgo's list is settled with the author just before its pass): work, service, usefulness, analysis, discernment, perfectionism, criticism, health, improvement. **Order** is also in Virgo's ten; its proposed home is Capricorn (cross-reference at the end).

**Questions to settle with the list:**
- **Analysis and discernment** overlap: both separate and judge. Proposed split: analysis is taking a thing apart and reasoning from evidence; discernment is telling things apart and judging well. **Judgment** (in five signs' tables) would be researched under discernment.
- **Gullibility, naivety and credulity** (a near-synonym group in the concept index) are discernment's deficiency. Research them here?
- **Criticism, perfectionism and improvement** form one cluster. Proposed split: criticism is pointing out faults, in others and in oneself (self-criticism sits here); perfectionism is the demand for flawlessness (standards nobody set sit here); improvement is the belief in, and practice of, making things better.
- **Health** is proposed to take in healing (four signs), illness (two), hygiene and diet as facets rather than separate entries.
- **Service** borders three settled concepts: duty (Capricorn), care (Cancer) and self-sacrifice (Pisces). Proposed boundary: service is work done for another (a person, a public, a cause, God), paid or unpaid; duty is what is owed; care is tending to someone's needs.
- **Usefulness** borders self-worth (Taurus), meaning (Sagittarius) and the near-synonym group "help nobody asked for / interference / intrusion / remaking others". Proposed: unasked help is researched here as usefulness's excess.
- **Work** is proposed to take in employment, diligence, drudgery, idleness and workaholism as facets.
- **"Discrimination"** means both fine judgment and prejudice, in English and in Spanish. Prejudice is touched here only where it bears on judging; its home may be fairness (Libra) or equality (Aquarius).
- **What the domain may need that the list lacks:** daily routine (a Tier 2 concept, in seven signs, and a traditional area with no concept); cleanliness (with purity and contamination); craft and skill (competence, craftsmanship); rest and leisure (with idleness, work's opposite); modesty or humility (a near-synonym group); reliability (Tier 2). Section 0 covers routine, cleanliness, craft and rest as areas until the author decides.

## 0. The domain: work and service

**Traditional areas:** work, service, daily routine, health. **The domain question:** what is good work and a well-kept life?

- **Where work sits in a life.** Is work the center of a life, a means to the rest of it, or a burden to keep small? The World Values Survey's items on how important work is in life and whether work should come first even at the cost of spare time (*check* wording and waves); the job, career and calling orientations (Wrzesniewski et al., 1997, building on Bellah et al., *Habits of the Heart*, 1985); what people answer to "what do you do?" (see Aries: identity); obituaries, which show what a community praises in a finished life (the job held, the years served, "a hard worker").
- **The culture's own grouping.** Does it file health with work (fitness to work, sick leave), with religion (the body as a temple; illness as a trial), with family (who nurses the sick) or with the land (balance with place and community)? Is criticism part of good work or a social matter? Which of these concepts are central, which marginal, and which does the culture fuse or split differently?
- **Ethnography for any culture:** the Human Relations Area Files (eHRAF), searched by the Outline of Cultural Materials categories for labor (46), living standards and routines (51), health and welfare (74) and sickness (75) (*check* numbers); the unit's subsistence lineage (README §3b: herding, rice, wheat, fishing, plantation, frontier ranching) as a frame for its work ways; immigrant orientation guides and business-culture guides on work norms (practitioner).
- **Daily routine** (a traditional area with no concept on the list):
  - the shape of the day: rising time, meal times and the main meal (the evening dinner of the U.S. and Canada against the Mexican midafternoon *comida* and the *sobremesa*), the working day, the commute, bedtime;
  - time-use surveys: the American Time Use Survey (BLS); Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on time use; INEGI's National Time Use Survey (ENUT) (*check* years for each);
  - commuting times (ACS; Statistics Canada's census; INEGI's census); sleep (CDC's short-sleep estimates by state and county from BRFSS and PLACES);
  - the pace of life: walking speed, clock accuracy and post-office service time in 31 countries (Levine and Norenzayan, 1999); Levine's U.S. city comparisons in *A Geography of Time* (1997) (*check*);
  - the weekly and yearly round: the Sabbath, market and *tianguis* days, Saturday chores, school calendars, fishing and harvest seasons; whether the afternoon *siesta* survives;
  - Indigenous seasonal rounds as the nations describe them (salmon runs, root digging on the Plateau, acorn and pine-nut harvests in California and the Great Basin, Dene hunting and trapping cycles); E. P. Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism" (1967), on task time against clock time;
  - the clock and the calendar as such belong to time ways (Capricorn).
- **Cleanliness and the kept house:**
  - ideas of dirt and order: Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966), dirt as "matter out of place"; "cleanliness is next to godliness" (popularized by a John Wesley sermon, *check*); the colonial *limpieza de sangre* ("purity of blood"), where cleanliness, lineage and race met;
  - household practice: who cleans, how often, how a house is judged by visitors (housework minutes by gender in ATUS and ENUT); sweeping and washing the sidewalk in front of the house; spring cleaning; decluttering (Marie Kondo's method from Japan; "Swedish death cleaning"); professional organizers; spending on cleaning supplies (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey by region *check*);
  - public cleanliness: litter and graffiti (Keep America Beautiful's litter studies *check*); street sweeping; Japanese pupils cleaning their own classrooms (*o-sōji*) as an example from elsewhere of cleanliness taught as a shared duty;
  - personal hygiene: bathing, deodorant, handwashing (observational studies in public restrooms *check*), the shame of smell, hygiene advertising.
- **Chores and errands:**
  - who does the household's work: the gendered division (ATUS; ENUT; Statistics Canada); Hochschild, *The Second Shift* (1989); children's chores (see usefulness);
  - paid domestic work: Mexico's *trabajadoras del hogar*, their own union (SINACTRAHO, 2015 *check*) and IMSS's program to enroll them (*check*); domestic workers' groups in California (the National Domestic Workers Alliance *check*); what employers call domestic workers (*la muchacha*; the slur *chacha*) and what the workers call themselves;
  - errands: the market trip, the bank line, the Mexican *trámite* (paperwork errand) and the *gestor* paid to do it.
- **Colleagues and the workplace:** friendship at work (Gallup's "best friend at work" item *check*); office potlucks, holiday parties and the workplace *posada*; the break room; how coworkers address each other (first names; *licenciado*, *ingeniero*, *maestro* as titles; rank itself is Capricorn).
- **Craft and skill:** apprenticeship and mastery (union apprenticeships; trades learned under a *maistro*); the Arts and Crafts movement (Pasadena's Greene and Greene; Craftsman bungalows in Berkeley, Portland and Seattle); Mexico's *artesanías* and the federal craft fund (FONART); Northwest Coast carving and weaving and California basketry as the nations and their artists present them; Mata Ortiz pottery in Chihuahua; craft fairs and maker spaces; Richard Sennett, *The Craftsman* (2008).
- **Rest and leisure,** work's counterpart: paid vacation, and how many days people expect and take (Expedia's yearly Vacation Deprivation study, by country *check*); unused vacation days (U.S. Travel Association surveys *check*); the weekend and the Sabbath; the *puente* (a bridge holiday); retirement; Josef Pieper, *Leisure: The Basis of Culture* (1948); Juliet Schor, *The Overworked American* (1991).
- **Work ways** (Fischer's folkway for Virgo):
  - Fischer's four founding work ethics in *Albion's Seed* (the Puritan duty of "improving the time"; the Virginia gentry's leisure resting on servants' and enslaved people's labor; Quaker honest industry; the backcountry's alternation of hard work and idleness) (*check* his exact terms), and which of them reached the pilot (the Left Coast's Yankee founders);
  - the division of labor by gender, age and inherited status; unfree labor (mission labor, the *encomienda* and *repartimiento*, debt peonage and the hacienda's company store, the *tienda de raya*; convict labor; contract labor);
  - communal labor: barn raisings and work bees; Mexico's *tequio* and *faena* (unpaid communal work owed under *usos y costumbres*), which Oaxacan hometown associations in California still organize (*check*); the Latter-day Saints' welfare farms and Deseret Industries;
  - the rhythm of work: harvests, fishing and cannery seasons; Saint Monday (*San Lunes*, the artisans' old custom of taking Monday off; Gutman, *Work, Culture, and Society in Industrializing America*, 1976);
  - workplaces, tools and work clothes: the farm, cannery, mill, mine, shipyard, *maquiladora* and tech campus; overalls, hard hats, scrubs, the hoodie;
  - work songs and work lore: sea shanties, loggers' songs, migrant workers' *corridos*, Woody Guthrie's Columbia River songs for the Bonneville Power Administration (1941);
  - for Canada, the staples thesis (Innis, *The Fur Trade in Canada*, 1930): fur, fish, timber and minerals shaped work in British Columbia.
- **Neighboring folkways:** no runner-up folkway is placed in Virgo. Time ways and order ways (both home sign Capricorn) border the daily round and the kept house; the household side is researched here, public order and the clock in `10 - Capricorn.md`.

## 1. Work

**Working definition:** purposeful effort that produces, keeps up or provides something of value, paid or unpaid, and the place that effort holds in a life.
**Facets:** paid employment; unpaid work in the home; subsistence work (fishing, hunting, gathering, farming for the table); communal work; craft and skilled trades; manual and mental work; the work ethic; idleness and laziness; overwork; unemployment.
**Neighbors and opposites:** labor, toil, job, trade, career, calling, industry, diligence; idleness, leisure, rest, play, unemployment. **Excess:** workaholism, overwork, burnout, death from overwork (Japanese *karōshi*). **Deficiency:** idleness, sloth, shirking.

- **Words:** "work", "job", "labor", "career", "calling", "trade", "hustle", "grind", "side hustle", "hard worker", "workhorse", "slacker", "freeloader", "a good day's work", "earn a living", "blue-collar", "white-collar", "work ethic" (its spread can be dated in Google Books Ngram), "essential worker" (2020); Pacific Northwest and British Columbia English "skookum" (strong, well made; from Chinuk Wawa); Chinuk Wawa *mamuk* ("to do, make, work") in the dictionary published by the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde (2012 *check*); Spanish *trabajo* (dictionaries trace it to a Latin word for torture, *check*), *obra* and *mano de obra*, *oficio* (a trade; *sin oficio ni beneficio*, idle and of no use), *chamba* and *chambear* (Mexican: a job, to work), *jale* (northern Mexican and Chicano: a job), *talacha* (grunt work), *friega*, *jornada*, *jornalero*, *peón*, *bracero*, *godín* (Mexican: an office worker), *mil usos* (a jack-of-all-trades), *maistro* (how tradesmen are addressed), *flojo* and *huevón* (lazy), *echarle ganas*, *de planta* (permanent) against *eventual*, *la informalidad*, *el home office* (borrowed English for remote work); English "labor" and Spanish *trabajo de parto* both name childbirth. **Names:** occupational surnames (Smith, Taylor; *Herrero*, *Zapatero*); team names from work (the San Francisco 49ers; the Portland Timbers; Mexico's Cruz Azul, founded by cement-cooperative workers).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is work a curse (Genesis 3:19; *ganarás el pan con el sudor de tu frente*), a calling (Weber, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, 1905, and tests of it such as Becker and Woessmann, 2009), a duty to society, the source of dignity (*el trabajo dignifica*), or a means to leisure? Catholic social teaching on the dignity of labor (*Rerum Novarum*, 1891; *Laborem Exercens*, 1981); Latter-day Saint teaching on industry; "if any would not work, neither should he eat" (2 Thessalonians 3:10; *el que no trabaja no come*); Indigenous teachings on work, land and reciprocity, as the nations state them; whether hard work brings success or luck and connections do; whether manual work is honorable or lowly; whether unpaid work in the home counts as work.
- **Feeling and the body:** the tired body; calluses and "working hands"; sweat and dirt as pride or shame; injury and chronic pain; boredom and drudgery; flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990, written at Chicago and later Claremont); stress and burnout; the satisfaction of finishing; work clothes that announce a trade; how a body reads as working-class or office-class.
- **Everyday practice:** hours worked; second jobs and side hustles; working while sick; the lunch break; the commute; finding work through connections (*palancas*); street vending and *tianguis* stalls; day-labor corners and worker centers; shop-floor culture and unions; peer pressure against working too hard (the "rate-buster" of the Hawthorne studies; Roethlisberger and Dickson, 1939); remote work.
- **Life course:** chores and allowances; children helping in family businesses, farms and market stalls; first jobs (teen employment by state, BLS); working through school; apprenticeship and trade school; Mexico's youth apprenticeship program *Jóvenes Construyendo el Futuro* (2019); choosing a career; retirement ages and pensions (Social Security; the Canada Pension Plan; Mexico's AFORE accounts, 1997); working past 65; eulogies and obituaries that list a person's work.
- **Ritual:** Labor Day (U.S. and Canada, September); May Day, Mexico's *Día del Trabajo* (May 1), and its marches; the *Día de la Santa Cruz* (May 3), when Mexican builders raise a decorated cross on the site and the owner feeds the crew; topping-out ceremonies; San Isidro Labrador's day (May 15), with blessings of oxen and tools; blessings of the fishing fleet; harvest festivals (the *Fiesta de la Vendimia* in Baja California's Valle de Guadalupe); loggers' sports and timber carnivals; *charrería*, whose events grew from ranch work; first-salmon and first-harvest ceremonies as the nations share them; retirement parties.
- **Institutions:** unions (the ILWU; the Culinary Workers Union in Las Vegas; the UFW; Mexico's CTM); worker centers and day-labor centers; employers' groups and chambers of commerce (Mexico's COPARMEX *check*); professional associations and trade guilds; trade schools, community colleges and union apprenticeship programs; public job services (WorkSource in Washington; WorkBC; Mexico's Servicio Nacional de Empleo) (*check*); workers' compensation (Washington's state-run fund); unemployment insurance; paid family leave programs (California's, 2002); welfare-to-work programs; temporary-worker programs (H-2A; Canada's Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program, open to Mexicans since 1974); church job help (the Latter-day Saints' employment centers *check*; parish job boards); the workplace itself, with its handbooks, HR departments and trade press.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise as "a hard worker", a staple of obituaries (a content analysis by region would test how central it is); raises, promotions, bonuses and Mexico's year-end *aguinaldo*; employee of the month; the gold watch; sanctions on idleness: "lazy", "bum", *flojo*, *huevón*, "welfare queen" (1976, racialized), the stigma of unemployment; sanctions on overwork ("no life", *matado*); peer sanctions on rate-busters.
- **Expression:** proverbs (*a quien madruga, Dios le ayuda*; "the early bird gets the worm"; "idle hands are the devil's workshop"; "many hands make light work"; *más vale maña que fuerza*); union songs (Joe Hill; "Solidarity Forever"); migrant workers' *corridos*; Steinbeck, *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939); McWilliams, *Factories in the Field* (1939); Studs Terkel, *Working* (1974); films (*Salt of the Earth*, 1954, on a New Mexico miners' strike; *Nosotros los pobres*, 1948, with Pedro Infante as a carpenter; *El Milusos*, 1981 *check*); Paul Bunyan tales; murals of labor (Diego Rivera's San Francisco murals, 1931 and 1940); company mission statements and slogans.
- **Exemplars:** Rosie the Riveter (the Richmond, California, shipyards); César Chávez, Dolores Huerta and Larry Itliong; the *braceros*; the Chinese railroad workers of the Central Pacific; loggers, fishers, miners and cannery workers; Jesús García, the railroad worker who drove a burning dynamite train out of Nacozari, Sonora (1907); Henry J. Kaiser; Harry Bridges; master carvers, weavers and canoe builders as their nations honor them; counter-exemplars: the idler, the heir who never worked, the workaholic who dies at the desk.
- **History:** Indigenous economies before contact and Indigenous wage labor after it (Lutz, *Makúk*, 2008; Knight, *Indians at Work*, 1978; Raibmon, *Authentic Indians*, 2005, on hop picking) (*check* years); mission labor; the Hudson's Bay Company's mixed workforce; the gold rush and the nativist drive against Mexican, Chilean and Chinese miners (*check*); the anti-Chinese movement among Western workers (the Workingmen's Party of California, 1877 *check*); timber, fishing and mining towns; the IWW; the Seattle General Strike (1919); the 1934 waterfront strike; the wartime shipyards; Dust Bowl migrants; the Bracero Program (1942-1964); the Cananea strike in Sonora (1906); the *maquiladoras* (from 1965); Monterrey's industrial families; the Delano grape strike (1965); Silicon Valley; the spotted-owl timber wars (1990s); the free-trade era after 1994 (NAFTA) and the work it moved across borders; the San Quintín farmworkers' strike in Baja California (2015); the 2023 Hollywood strikes over AI; Baja California Sur's pearl fishery, the Santa Rosalía copper mine and the Guerrero Negro saltworks.
- **Measures:** the World Values Survey's work items (importance of work; work as a duty to society; "people who don't work turn lazy"; hard work as a quality to teach children) (*check* waves); the GSS (hard work against luck or help; whether people would keep working if they were rich); the ISSP Work Orientations modules (1989, 1997, 2005, 2015; *check* which pilot countries took part); Gallup's State of the Global Workplace (engagement, country); BLS hours, multiple jobholders, union membership by state and the Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries; ACS labor-force participation, usual hours and class of worker by county; Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey (province, metro); INEGI's ENOE (employment and the informality rate by state and city); IMSS-registered jobs by state; OECD hours worked (country); ATUS and ENUT (paid and unpaid work); the Protestant work ethic scale (Mirels and Garrett, 1971) and the Multidimensional Work Ethic Profile (Miller et al., 2002); conscientiousness by state (Rentfrow et al., 2013).
- **Variation:** class (Lamont, *The Dignity of Working Men*, 2000, on the "disciplined self" of working-class morality); gender and unpaid work; immigrant generation (a research question, never a finding); religion (Protestant, Catholic, Latter-day Saint); region inside the pilot (tech hubs, resource towns, farm valleys, border factory cities, tourist economies such as Las Vegas and Los Cabos); formal against informal work; union against nonunion; each Indigenous nation; party divides over welfare and whether aid should depend on work.
- **Relations:** service, usefulness and perfectionism (this sign); duty, discipline, ambition and merit (Capricorn); wealth and possessions (Taurus); initiative and independence (Aries); play and leisure (Leo); community and mutual aid (Aquarius); escapism (Pisces) as flight from work; time ways (Capricorn).
- **Who it applies to:** children (work on family farms and in family businesses); women (whether housework is "work"); elders; disabled workers (sheltered workshops); undocumented workers (their share of farm work in the National Agricultural Workers Survey); working animals; machines and AI doing work (present-day attitudes: Pew's surveys on automation, 2017, and on AI and jobs *check*).
- **Silences:** wage theft; undocumented labor that whole industries depend on; deaths at work; unpaid care work; sex work; the informal economy; pay (see Aries: self-assertion); what overwork costs families; the people who clean offices at night (Justice for Janitors, Los Angeles, 1990).
- **Change:** remote work and return-to-office mandates; the "Great Resignation" (2021) and "quiet quitting" (2022); gig work (app-based driving and delivery); generative AI; four-day-week trials; Mexico's public debate over a 40-hour week (*check*); new unions in coffee shops and retail.

**Key questions:** Is work a curse, a calling, a duty or a means? Does a person's worth rise with how hard they work, and whose work counts as work at all?

## 2. Service

**Working definition:** work or effort done for another (a person, a household, a customer, the public, a nation, a cause or God), as a role or as an act, paid or unpaid.
**Facets:** personal and domestic service; customer service and hospitality work; public and civil service; military and national service; volunteering and community service; religious service and ministry; servant leadership; service to elders, the community and the nation in Indigenous communities, as the nations describe it.
**Neighbors and opposites:** helping, serving, ministry, duty, volunteering, hospitality, devotion; being served, mastery, self-interest. **Excess:** servility, obsequiousness, self-erasure, being exploited. **Deficiency:** unhelpfulness, entitlement, the expectation of being served.

- **Words:** "service", "serve", "servant", "public servant", "civil service", "customer service", "service industry", "server", "at your service", "thank you for your service" (to veterans), "servant leadership" (Greenleaf, 1970), "community service", "the help" (dismissive), "servile", "menial"; "service" comes from the Latin for slavery (*servitium*, from *servus*); Spanish *servicio*, *servir*, *servidor público*, *servidumbre* (servants; servitude), *servicial* (obliging, as praise); Mexican courtesy formulas *para servirle*, *a sus órdenes*, *mande* (the reply when called; literally "command"), and *su servidor* (a speaker's humble name for himself); *servicio social* (the service Mexican students do before they graduate); *lambiscón* and *barbero* (Mexican: a toady); Spanish *servir* also means to work or be of use (*no sirve*: it's broken), which ties service to usefulness.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is serving honorable or demeaning? Does that depend on whom one serves (God, the nation, the public, a customer, an employer) and on whether one is paid? Christian teaching that the first must be servant of all (Mark 9:35) and the washing of feet (John 13); the Jesuit ideal of "men for others" (Pedro Arrupe, 1973); Rotary's "Service Above Self"; the republican ideal of public service against the spoils system; Latter-day Saint teaching that serving others is serving God (Mosiah 2:17); whether service work is a career or a stopgap; whether waiting tables carries different standing in Mexico, Canada and the U.S. (a research question).
- **Feeling and the body:** emotional labor (Hochschild, *The Managed Heart*, 1983, written at Berkeley, on flight attendants); the required smile (Safeway's 1998 "superior service" rules, which clerks said invited harassment *check*); standing while others sit; voice, posture and eye contact toward customers and employers; the helper's warm glow and the resentment of the unthanked; servers' feet and backs; uniforms and name tags.
- **Everyday practice:** tipping (how much, to whom, whether it is owed); how customers call a server (*joven*, *señorita*, "excuse me", a raised hand); complaints and returns; hospitality to guests; helping neighbors; volunteering at food banks, schools and churches; serving at church (ushers, altar servers, Latter-day Saint callings); hiring household help; asking for and offering help in public.
- **Life course:** service-hour requirements in schools (by district and province; Ontario's 40 hours as an example from elsewhere); service hours for Catholic confirmation; the Scouts' daily good turn and Eagle projects; Latter-day Saint missions at 18 or 19; military enlistment after high school; Mexico's *Servicio Militar Nacional* for young men and its *cartilla*; Mexico's university *servicio social* and the rural year for new doctors (*pasantes*); the Peace Corps after college; retirees' volunteering; elders' service to their nation and community as the nations describe it; eulogies that praise service.
- **Ritual:** Veterans Day, Remembrance Day and Memorial Day; the Martin Luther King Jr. Day of Service (since 1994); Mexico's *Día del Maestro* (May 15), *Día de la Enfermera* and *Día del Médico*; National Nurses Week; Public Service Recognition Week; oaths of office and swearing-in; enlistment and commissioning; the handover of *cargos* and *mayordomías* at saints' festivals; Latter-day Saint missionary farewells and homecomings; Tribal Canoe Journeys (since the 1989 Paddle to Seattle *check*), in which host nations serve visiting canoe families, as the nations describe the protocol.
- **Institutions:** civil services (the U.S. federal civil service; Canada's Public Service Commission; Mexico's *Servicio Profesional de Carrera*, 2003 *check*); national and youth service (AmeriCorps; the Peace Corps; the Civilian Conservation Corps, 1933; Canada's Katimavik, 1977); military service (the U.S. draft until 1973; Mexico's lottery for military service); veterans' groups and services (the American Legion; the Royal Canadian Legion; the VA; Veterans Affairs Canada); the Mesoamerican *cargo* system of rotating, unpaid community offices; service clubs (Rotary, Kiwanis, Lions, Soroptimist, Elks); religious service bodies (Catholic orders; the Salvation Army; the American Friends Service Committee; the Latter-day Saints' welfare system); volunteer centers and food banks; hospitality schools, employers and their training; Mexico's *Ángeles Verdes* (free federal roadside help).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** thanks, tips, medals and volunteer awards (the President's Volunteer Service Award; Canada's Sovereign's Medal for Volunteers and the Order of Canada; Mexico's Belisario Domínguez Medal); a name as helpful or *servicial*; sanctions on bad service (low tips, online reviews, complaints); sanctions on refusing service (the stigma, or the honor, of draft resistance: tens of thousands of American draft resisters went to Canada during the Vietnam War, many to British Columbia *check*); sanctions on servility (*lambiscón*, "brown-noser").
- **Expression:** proverbs (*haz el bien sin mirar a quién*; *hoy por ti, mañana por mí*; *candil de la calle, oscuridad de su casa*, of one who serves outsiders and neglects home); Alfonso Cuarón's *Roma* (2018), about a Mixtec domestic worker; Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* (1989) as an example from elsewhere of the perfect servant; recruiting slogans; nurses' and teachers' memoirs; hymns of service; hotel and restaurant slogans ("service with a smile").
- **Exemplars:** Mother Antonia Brenner, the American-born nun who lived in Tijuana's La Mesa prison to serve its inmates (1977-2013); Dolores Huerta; firefighters and nurses; Mexico's Squadron 201 in World War II; the rural teachers of Mexico's 1920s cultural missions; the *Topos* rescue volunteers after Mexico City's 1985 earthquake; Peace Corps volunteers; Junípero Serra (contested: canonized in 2015, his statues pulled down in 2020); counter-exemplars: the official who serves himself, the rude clerk, the flatterer.
- **History:** mission labor and the *encomienda*; Chinese and Japanese houseboys and domestic workers in the West; Pullman porters on Western lines; World War II conscientious objectors in Civilian Public Service camps in Oregon and California (Camp Waldport *check*); Japanese-American internment and military service (see Aries: courage); the Vietnam draft and its resisters; the rise of the service economy; Mexico's 1985 and 2017 earthquakes, when citizens served where the state failed; the army's growing role in Mexican public works (airports, trains) under López Obrador.
- **Measures:** volunteering (the Census Bureau and AmeriCorps Civic Engagement and Volunteering supplement, by state and metro; Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on Giving, Volunteering and Participating, by province; INEGI's satellite account of nonprofit institutions, which values volunteer work *check*); the CAF World Giving Index (helping a stranger, giving, volunteering; country); Peace Corps volunteers per capita by state and metro (published yearly); veterans' share of population (ACS, county); public employment (BLS QCEW; Statistics Canada; INEGI); service-sector employment shares; tipping (Lynn's studies of tipping across countries and U.S. states; payment-processor tip data by state *check*); GLOBE's humane orientation (country); the GSS helping and altruism items.
- **Variation:** class (who serves and who is served); gender (service and care work as women's work); race and immigration (who fills service jobs; a research question); region (casino and resort economies such as Las Vegas and Los Cabos against tech cities); religion (Latter-day Saint callings and missions; Catholic service; secular volunteering); generation; each Indigenous nation's own idea of serving the people.
- **Relations:** duty and hierarchy (Capricorn); care (Cancer); self-sacrifice and compassion (Pisces); reciprocity (Libra); generosity (Leo); faith (Sagittarius); work and usefulness (this sign).
- **Who it applies to:** servants and employers; customers; citizens and the state; soldiers; clergy; animals that serve (service and guide dogs); service robots and AI assistants (present-day attitudes: sidewalk delivery robots, robotaxis in San Francisco and Los Angeles, chatbots replacing call centers; Pew surveys *check*).
- **Silences:** the labor behind seamless service; harassment of hotel housekeepers and servers; servility's roots in slavery and peonage; the shame some feel in serving; "service" that serves the server (patronage, corruption).
- **Change:** tipping prompts on payment screens; self-checkout; AI customer service; on-demand gig service; the 2020 label "essential workers"; proposals for national service; the decline of service clubs (Putnam, *Bowling Alone*, 2000).

**Key questions:** Is serving honorable or demeaning, and does that change with whom one serves and whether one is paid? Who is expected to serve whom?

## 3. Usefulness

**Working definition:** being of practical value to others or to a purpose. For a person, contributing and being needed; for a thing or a piece of knowledge, practical value.
**Facets:** a person's usefulness to family, employer, community and nation; feeling useful and feeling useless; useful knowledge and practical education; useful objects, repair and thrift; being a burden; the standing of the old, the sick and the disabled; jobs felt to be useful or pointless; help nobody asked for.
**Neighbors and opposites:** utility, contribution, purpose, being needed, practicality, handiness; uselessness, being a burden, ornament, dead weight. **Excess:** worth measured only by output; letting oneself be used; officious help nobody asked for. **Deficiency:** idleness; dependence (see Aries: independence); being "just for show".

- **Words:** "useful", "handy", "practical", "make yourself useful", "pull your weight", "earn your keep", "pitch in", "lend a hand", "a big help", "useless", "good for nothing", "dead weight", "a burden", "busybody"; "skookum" (BC and Northwest English: sturdy, reliable); Spanish *útil*, *inútil*, *servir* (*¿para qué sirves?*; *no sirves para nada*, "you're good for nothing"), *bueno para nada*, *acomedido* (one who helps without being asked), *comedido*, *estorbo* (someone in the way), *arrimado* (a hanger-on living in someone's house; *el muerto y el arrimado a los tres días apestan*), *estar de adorno* or *de bulto* (to be just for show, just taking up space), *mantenido* (see Aries), *ser una carga*, *hacer el quehacer* (to do the housework), *metiche* (a meddler); Japanese *ikigai* (a reason for being) as an example from elsewhere.
- **Beliefs and standing:** must a person be useful to have worth, or is worth given (by God, by birth, by belonging)? - **Feeling and the body:** the satisfaction of being needed; the pain of feeling useless after job loss, retirement, illness or injury (the classic study of an unemployed village, Jahoda, Lazarsfeld and Zeisel, *Marienthal*, 1933); the able body as the useful body; strength and handiness; the idle body; the shame of needing help.
- **Everyday practice:** children's chores, and whether children help without being asked (Rogoff and colleagues at UC Santa Cruz on "learning by observing and pitching in" in Mexican and Mexican-heritage families *check*); children translating for immigrant parents (Orellana, *Translating Childhoods*, 2009); grandparents minding or raising grandchildren; home repair and do-it-yourself; repair cafés; buying things that last; thrift stores and yard sales; giving away what is no longer used; useful gifts against decorative ones; the neighborhood handyman; offering help at gatherings (bringing food, washing dishes).
- **Life course:** the first chore; toddlers who want to "help"; the teenager who is "no help"; becoming a provider; the empty nest; retirement and the search for a role; elders' usefulness as grandparents, storytellers, advisers and keepers of language; the fear of becoming a burden at the end of life.
- **Ritual:** retirement ceremonies (the end of a useful role, or its honoring); handing down tools, recipes and skills; the blessing of animals on Saint Anthony the Abbot's day (January 17) in Mexico, which honors working and household animals; Japan's memorial for broken sewing needles (*hari-kuyō*) as an example from elsewhere of thanking useful objects; career days and job fairs; honored roles for elders at gatherings, as the nations describe them.
- **Institutions:** practical and vocational education (land-grant colleges, from 1862; Mexico's Instituto Politécnico Nacional, 1936; the Tec de Monterrey, 1943, founded by Monterrey industrialists); 4-H and FFA, which teach rural young people practical skills; the American Philosophical Society, founded "for promoting useful knowledge" (1743); pensions and retirement; senior centers, senior employment programs and volunteer programs for retirees (AmeriCorps Seniors *check*); sheltered workshops for disabled workers and vocational rehabilitation services; repair cafés and tool libraries; hospice and palliative care, where patients voice the fear of being a burden.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the *acomedido* and the "real help"; being needed as its own reward; sanctions: "useless", "lazy", the *arrimado* shown the door, ageism and ableism; the sanction on officious help ("who asked you?", *metiche*); redemption through retraining and new roles.
- **Expression:** William Morris's rule to keep nothing you don't know to be useful or believe to be beautiful (1880); Shaker furniture; the *Whole Earth Catalog* (Menlo Park, 1968) and its motto "access to tools"; Craftsman bungalows; Mexican household crafts (the *molcajete*, the *comal*, clay cooking pots); *MacGyver* (1985-1992); proverbs (*el muerto y el arrimado*; "jack of all trades, master of none"; "if you want something done, ask a busy person"); David Graeber, *Bullshit Jobs* (2018), on work its own doers think pointless.
- **Exemplars:** Benjamin Franklin; the handyman and the farm wife who can fix anything; *el mil usos*; elders who keep a language alive; Ed Roberts (see Aries: independence); counter-exemplars: the idle heir, the courtier, "dead weight", the *arrimado*.
- **History:** Locke's argument that unimproved land was waste, used to justify taking Indigenous land (see improvement); county poor farms and the "deserving poor"; eugenic sterilization (California's program, about 20,000 people from 1909 to 1979 *check*; Alberta's and British Columbia's programs); old-age pensions (U.S. Social Security, 1935; Canada's first old-age pensions, 1927; Mexico's universal pension for older adults, 2019, after Mexico City's in 2001); the move from farm economies, where children were useful hands, to cities, where they became emotionally "priceless" instead (Zelizer, *Pricing the Priceless Child*, 1985).
- **Measures:** whether workers feel their job is useful to society (ISSP Work Orientations items; Dur and van Lent, 2019, on "socially useless jobs" *check*); the GSS item on work that gives a feeling of accomplishment; the values-survey item that taking money without working for it is humiliating (*check*); Gallup's "purpose" well-being by state; older adults' labor-force participation (BLS; Statistics Canada; ENOE); grandparents responsible for grandchildren (ACS, county); volunteering by age; disability employment rates (ACS; Statistics Canada's Canadian Survey on Disability; INEGI's census); "burden" concerns in the Oregon Health Authority's yearly Death with Dignity reports, Washington's reports and Health Canada's MAID reports; suicide among older men by state (CDC WONDER), read with great care as one possible sign of lost roles.
- **Variation:** gender (useful as provider or as homemaker); class; age; disability; rural practical skill against urban specialization; immigrant families' expectations of children's help (a research question); religion; each Indigenous nation's idea of each person's role.
- **Relations:** work, service and improvement (this sign); self-worth (Taurus); meaning (Sagittarius); recognition and dignity (Leo); independence (Aries); care (Cancer); death (Scorpio), where the fear of being a burden enters end-of-life choices; exploitation (Tier 2), being used.
- **Who it applies to:** children, elders, the disabled, the sick, the unemployed; animals (working animals against pets; "useful" species against "pests"); land ("waste" against "productive" land); objects; knowledge and languages ("useful" languages against heritage languages); machines and AI judged by usefulness alone, and present-day fears that AI will make people's skills useless.
- **Silences:** the fear of being a burden; elders' loneliness; the worth of disabled people; the many who think their own job pointless; who decides what counts as useful.
- **Change:** aging populations; automation and AI; the right-to-repair and buy-it-for-life movements; minimalism; refusals to measure worth by work ("lazy girl jobs", 2023).

**Key questions:** Must a person be useful to be worthy? What happens to the standing of those who can no longer contribute, and who decides what counts as useful?

## 4. Analysis

**Working definition:** taking a whole apart to understand it: reasoning from parts, evidence, measurement and detail.
**Facets:** logical and mathematical reasoning; the scientific method; measurement and record keeping; accounting and auditing; diagnosis; data and statistics; critical thinking; analytic against holistic thinking; self-analysis and therapy; analysis in government and business; overanalysis.
**Neighbors and opposites:** reasoning, logic, scrutiny, measurement, evidence, data; intuition, holistic thinking, faith, gut feeling, experience, synthesis. **Excess:** overanalysis, "paralysis by analysis", reductionism, losing the forest for the trees, coldness. **Deficiency:** rashness, credulity, muddle.

- **Words:** "analyze", "break it down", "crunch the numbers", "do the math", "data-driven", "evidence-based", "critical thinking", "overthink", "paralysis by analysis", "number-cruncher", "bean counter", "nerd", "geek", "egghead", "book smart" against "street smart", "common sense", "the devil is in the details"; "analysis" is Greek for a loosening or breaking up, and shares the sense of separating with "criticism" and "discernment"; Spanish *análisis* (in Mexico also the everyday word for lab tests: *hacerse unos análisis*), *analizar*, *razonar*, *sacar cuentas*, *echar números*, *atar cabos* (to tie up loose ends: to deduce), *darle vueltas* (to keep turning something over), *buscarle tres pies al gato* (to look for three feet on a cat: to overcomplicate), *ñoño* and *cerebrito* (Mexican: nerd, brain), *sabelotodo* (know-it-all), *matado* (Mexican: a grind), *cuentas claras* (*las cuentas claras y el chocolate espeso*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is reason the road to truth, or one road beside faith, tradition, intuition and experience? - **Feeling and the body:** cool detachment as the analyst's display; the furrowed brow; mental fatigue; the stereotype of the analytic person as cold; whether analysis is gendered male; how the culture reads autistic people's analytic styles (autistic self-advocates' own accounts first).
- **Everyday practice:** budgets and household accounts; reading labels and comparing prices; spreadsheets; fantasy sports and sports statistics; checking facts; second opinions; tracking one's own health data; inspections before buying a house or car; whether families and firms decide by analysis or by the elder's or boss's word.
- **Life course:** school math and science (the Next Generation Science Standards in California, Oregon and Washington *check*); British Columbia's curriculum naming critical and reflective thinking as a core competency (2016 *check*); critical thinking (*pensamiento crítico*) in Mexico's Nueva Escuela Mexicana (*check*); science fairs and debate teams; the thesis and Mexico's *examen profesional* for the professional degree; the aging mind and dementia.
- **Ritual:** science fairs and math contests; thesis defenses; the census; audits and annual reports; quarterly earnings calls; election-night analysis; after-action reviews and post-mortems; autopsies; film study and draft combines in sport; hackathons.
- **Institutions:** statistical agencies (the Census Bureau, BLS and BEA; Statistics Canada; INEGI, autonomous since 2008); legislative analysts (California's Legislative Analyst's Office, 1941; Canada's Parliamentary Budget Officer *check* year); policy evaluators (the Washington State Institute for Public Policy, 1983, known for cost-benefit analysis; Mexico's CONEVAL, 2004, folded into INEGI in 2025 *check*); the randomized evaluation of Mexico's Progresa (1997); think tanks (RAND, Santa Monica, 1948); evidence-based medicine (named at McMaster University in Ontario, 1991) and health technology assessment (Oregon's prioritized Medicaid list); audit offices (the GAO; the Auditor General of Canada; Mexico's Auditoría Superior de la Federación); universities and labs (Caltech and JPL, Stanford, Berkeley, the University of Washington, UBC); accounting standards.

- **Who it applies to:** experts and laypeople; children ("too young to understand"); women; elders; Indigenous knowledge-holders; institutions (agencies, firms); algorithms and AI (present-day attitudes: preferring human to algorithmic judgment, "algorithm aversion", Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey, 2015; surveys on AI in hiring and lending).
- **Silences:** what a culture doesn't count: the Urban Indian Health Institute's 2018 report on missing data about Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls in U.S. cities (71 cities *check*); Canada's National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (2019); unrecorded informal work; what overanalysis costs; what can't be measured (love, the sacred).
- **Change:** big data and AI analytics; trust in science split by politics after COVID; attacks on and defenses of statistical agencies; "evidence-based" as a slogan; self-tracking.

**Key questions:** Does the culture trust taking things apart and measuring them, or does it trust experience, intuition, faith or the whole picture more? Who is allowed to analyze, and whose analysis counts?

## 5. Discernment

**Working definition:** the ability to tell things apart and judge well: true from false, good from bad, fine from coarse, the trustworthy from the untrustworthy, what matters from what doesn't.

## 6. Perfectionism

## 7. Criticism
