# Avenues of investigation: 08 Scorpio (death and transformation)

**What this file is.** For each Scorpio concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Scorpio concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. Nothing here comes from astrology: the sign is a research classifier only.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; each sign's list is settled with the author just before its pass): death, rebirth, purging, betrayal, control, trust, revenge, secrecy, power, crisis. Trust was settled under Scorpio by the author (2026-11-02). Questions to settle with the list:
- **Secrecy:** proposed home Scorpio (it is also in Pisces's ten; Pisces keeps a cross-reference). **Privacy** (under seven signs, Tier 2) is researched here as a facet of secrecy, unless the author gives it its own entry.
- **Trust and loyalty:** loyalty (under ten signs, Tier 2) has no home yet; it is researched here under trust and betrayal until it gets one. The gullibility, naivety and credulity group is treated as trust's excess.
- **Control and power** overlap each other and Capricorn's authority, hierarchy and discipline. This file treats **control** as directing or restraining people, events and one's own life, and **power** as the capacity behind it: who holds it, how, and how it is judged. Self-control (in the discipline, self-discipline and self-control group) points to Capricorn. Power ways, Fischer's folkway, has its proposed home in Capricorn.
- **Rebirth** sits beside regeneration and transformation (both listed under Scorpio alone) and next to Aries's **beginning**. Beginning covers starting and fresh starts; rebirth covers renewal that comes through an ending.
- **Revenge** sits beside retaliation and in reach of the grudge, grievance and resentment group (grudge is under nine signs, Tier 2). Its opposite, **forgiveness** (under seven signs, Tier 2), has no home.
- **Death** takes in grief and mourning here (the grief, mourning and sadness group), unless the author wants them as their own entry.
- **Purging** is an unusual word for a cultural concept; the working definition below is a proposal.
- **Crisis** is under seven signs (Tier 2) and is in Scorpio's ten.

No Scorpio concept has its proposed home in another sign, so this file has no cross-reference entries.

## 0. The domain: death and transformation

**Traditional areas:** death and rebirth, sex, shared resources, inheritance, secrets. **The domain question:** how does the culture face death, power and what is hidden?

- **Death denied or death familiar.** Whether the culture keeps death out of sight or lives beside it: Gorer, "The Pornography of Death" (1955); Ariès, *Western Attitudes toward Death* (1974) and *The Hour of Our Death* (1981 in English); Mitford, *The American Way of Death* (1963); Becker, *The Denial of Death* (1973), and terror management theory (Greenberg, Pyszczynski and Solomon), whose experiments can be run in any population; Lomnitz, *Death and the Idea of Mexico* (2005); the contested claim (Paz, *El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950) that Mexicans are on familiar terms with death.
- **How people are believed to change.** By grace, by effort, by ordeal, by cycle, or not at all: conversion narratives; recovery programs; rites of passage built as a death and a rebirth (van Gennep, 1909; Turner's liminality); implicit theories of whether character is fixed or can change (Dweck), measurable in any sample.
- **The hidden and the shown.** What the culture keeps out of sight and what it drags into the open: taboo topics (money, sex, death, illness, family trouble); high- and low-context communication (Hall); self-disclosure studies (Jourard; Barnlund's 1975 comparison of Japanese and American self-disclosure); gaps between public face and private feeling (Japan's *honne* and *tatemae* as an example from elsewhere); Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966), on what a culture treats as polluting.
- **Depth against breadth.** Few deep ties or many light ones: the number of confidants (the GSS "important matters" item, and the dispute over McPherson, Smith-Lovin and Brashears, 2006); the Survey Center on American Life's friendship survey (2021 *check*); how long it takes to be let into a circle; what is shared only inside it.
- **Shared resources ("other people's money"):**
  - money in marriage: whether a couple pools everything or keeps "mine" and "yours"; joint and separate bank accounts; who in a couple controls the money (Pew *check*; INEGI's ENDIREH has items on economic control);
  - pooling outside the household: rotating savings clubs (*tandas*; Mexico's ENIF financial-inclusion survey asks about informal saving), credit unions (Vancity in Vancouver) and cooperatives (REI), Mexican American mutual-aid societies (*mutualistas*), remittances;
  - the commons (Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*, 1990): water (irrigation districts and farmers sharing a river; who gets water in a drought, and whether that feels fair); fisheries (tribal fisheries on the Columbia and Puget Sound, as the nations describe them; Baja California Sur's fishing cooperatives *check*); grazing on public land (BLM allotments in the Great Basin); Crown land, most of British Columbia, used for camping, hunting and gathering (*check*); the *ejido* and *tierras comunales*;
  - risk pooling and debt: life insurance (LIMRA's ownership studies *check*); household debt by county (the New York Fed's Consumer Credit Panel; the Urban Institute's debt maps); Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security; attitudes to taxes as a shared pot (Gallup and Pew on whether people pay their "fair share").
- **Inheritance:**
  - making a will, or not: the share of adults with a will (Gallup, 2016, and industry surveys *check*); Mexico's yearly *Septiembre, mes del testamento* campaign (*check*); why people put it off (cost, superstition, not wanting to talk about death);
  - who inherits: equal shares against one favored heir; daughters against sons; who keeps the family home; family farms, ranches, fishing boats and businesses;
  - what is inherited besides money: hereditary names, crests, songs and fishing places on the Northwest Coast, passed through houses and lineages as the nations describe them; heirlooms and recipes; feuds and debts;
  - the state's share as people feel it: whether taxing inheritances is seen as fair or as a "death tax" (Gallup and Pew items *check*);
  - quarrels: fights over heirlooms and the family home; siblings who stop speaking; elder financial abuse by kin;
  - measures: inheritances received (the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances; Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security).
- **Sex ways** (Fischer's folkway for Scorpio):
  - norms as people judge them: premarital, extramarital and same-sex relations, cohabitation, pornography and sex work (GSS items on premarital and extramarital sex and on homosexuality; Gallup's yearly moral-acceptability items; Pew); the National Survey of Family Growth (CDC); Laumann et al., *The Social Organization of Sexuality* (1994); Mexico's ENADID and its sexual and gender diversity survey, ENDISEG (2021 *check*); Statistics Canada's sexual-health items (*check*);
  - courtship and the double standard: *noviazgo*; the *casa chica* (a second household kept by a married man); *machismo* and *marianismo* (Stevens, 1973; both contested as descriptions); *albures* (Mexican sexual wordplay); dating apps; purity culture (True Love Waits, 1993);
  - sexual diversity: San Francisco's Castro, West Hollywood, Vancouver's Davie Village; Two-Spirit (a term adopted at a 1990 gathering in Winnipeg) and each nation's own terms, as published; the Zapotec *muxe* of Oaxaca as an example from elsewhere; Gallup's LGBT identification by state; Statistics Canada's 2021 census questions on gender;
  - sex education: what schools, churches and families teach about sex, and when; what parents want taught (polls *check*);
  - reproduction and its control: contraceptive use (NSFG; ENADID); teen births and births outside marriage (CDC's National Vital Statistics System by county; INEGI's birth statistics);
  - sexual violence and consent: the National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (CDC); ENDIREH; Statistics Canada's Survey of Safety in Public and Private Spaces; #MeToo (2017);
  - history: the gold rush's few women; trafficked Chinese women in San Francisco; the Inquisition's records on sexual conduct in New Spain; Kinsey; the Summer of Love (1967); the AIDS crisis;
  - Fischer's own method: each founding culture's sex ways read from church and town records (prenuptial pregnancy, births outside marriage).
- **Death ways** (Fischer's folkway for Scorpio; what death means is in section 1):
  - the body: burial, cremation, green burial, natural organic reduction (human composting, offered in Washington from 2019, Oregon from 2021, California later *check*), alkaline hydrolysis; embalming; open or closed caskets; sending bodies home to Mexico (*traslado de restos*, with consular help *check*); cremation rates by state and province (Cremation Association of North America; National Funeral Directors Association);
  - funerals and wakes: the *velorio*, the rosary, the *novenario*; the wake at home or at a funeral home; the "celebration of life" in place of a funeral; music at funerals (mariachi, *banda*); funeral food;
  - graves and cemeteries: gravestone styles as evidence (Dethlefsen and Deetz's seriation of New England stones, 1966; Fischer on Puritan death's heads); Colma, California, the "city of the dead", where San Francisco moved most of its cemeteries (*check*); Chinese cemeteries in Victoria and San Francisco; Mexican *panteones*; roadside *descansos*; mortuary and memorial poles and grave houses as the nations share them; pet cemeteries;
  - mourning: black dress and *luto*; how long mourning lasts; the first-anniversary mass (*cabo de año* *check*); bereavement leave at work (how many days employers give, and for which kin *check*); mourning online;
  - the cost: funeral prices (the NFDA's cost survey); burial insurance; Mexico's prepaid funeral plans (*previsión funeraria*); crowdfunded funerals;
  - the dead kept close: Día de Muertos *ofrendas*; tending graves; household altars in Chinese, Japanese, Vietnamese and Filipino American homes; the Filipino *Undas* (November 1); memorial feasts and headstone-raising feasts as the nations share them;
  - the unburied and unidentified: migrant deaths at the border (the Pima County Office of the Medical Examiner; the Colibrí Center); Mexico's disappeared, its mass graves and the *madres buscadoras* (searching mothers' collectives) (*check* for counts).
- **Power ways** (runner-up; home sign Capricorn): who rules, and how power is shown and checked; power as a concept is section 9 here.

## 1. Death

**Working definition:** the end of a life, and how a culture understands dying, the dead and what death means. Grief and mourning are researched here; funeral practice is in death ways (section 0).
**Facets:** what death is (biological, social, spiritual); the good death and the bad death; dying and end-of-life care; chosen death (assisted dying, suicide); violent death (homicide, war); the deaths of children; mass death (epidemics, disasters); the dead as still present (ancestors, ghosts, saints); grief and mourning; social death (being treated as dead).
**Neighbors and opposites:** mortality, dying, loss, grief, mourning, the afterlife; life, survival, immortality. **Excess:** morbidity, a cult of death, glamorized violent death. **Deficiency:** denial of death.

- **Words:** "pass away", "passed", "lost", "gone", "the late", "kick the bucket", "six feet under", "end of life", "death with dignity", "celebration of life"; euphemism as evidence (Allan and Burridge, *Euphemism and Dysphemism*, 1991 *check*); Spanish *muerte*, *fallecer*, *difunto*, *finado*, *el más allá*, *descanse en paz* (Q.E.P.D.); Mexican nicknames for death (*la Pelona*, *la Flaca*, *la Huesuda*, *la Calaca*, *la Catrina*) and *se lo llevó la Pelona*; *angelitos* (dead children); *Mictlán*, the Mexica land of the dead; Chinuk Wawa *memalust*, "die, dead", in the Grand Ronde Tribes' dictionary (*check*), and Memaloose Island on the Columbia.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is death an enemy, a passage, a return, or simply the end? Belief in heaven, hell, purgatory, reincarnation and ghosts; the good death (at home, prepared, reconciled, after the last rites); Catholic teaching on purgatory and prayer for the dead; the Latter-day Saints' spirit world and baptism for the dead; Buddhist teaching in Japanese, Chinese and Vietnamese American communities; secular and humanist views; whether suicide is a sin, an illness or a person's own choice; Santa Muerte devotion (Chesnut, *Devoted to Death*, 2012); contacting the dead (Spiritualism, mediums: see magic ways, Pisces); Indigenous teachings on death and the dead as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** fear of death (Templer's Death Anxiety Scale, 1970; the Collett-Lester Fear of Death Scale); whether family touch, wash, dress and sit with the body; who sees a corpse, and when; where people die (home, hospital, nursing home, hospice); pain and its relief at the end; how grief is shown (wailing, silence, composure) and for how long.
- **Everyday practice:** talking about death at home, or not; wills, advance directives and funeral planning; life insurance; death cafés (begun in London, 2011); visiting graves; roadside memorials; dark jokes; keeping ashes at home; putting pets down; hunting, fishing and slaughter as contact with death.
- **Life course:** how children learn about death (pets, grandparents); whether children go to wakes and funerals; the last years of the old; hospice; who cares for the dying; planning one's own funeral; the life judged at its close in obituaries, eulogies and epitaphs (what a community chooses to praise).
- **Ritual:** funerals and wakes (section 0); Día de Muertos (November 1-2) and its *ofrendas*; All Saints and All Souls; Halloween; Memorial Day and Remembrance Day; Obon in Japanese American communities; Qingming in Chinese American communities; the Jewish shiva; neopagan Samhain observances; annual mourning ceremonies of California and Yuman nations and memorial feasts on the Northwest Coast, as the nations share them; vigils after violent deaths.
- **Institutions:** death certificates and the coroner or medical examiner; hospitals, and brain death as doctors and families understand it; medical aid in dying where doctors offer it (Oregon, Washington, California, Canada's MAID), and the doctors, hospitals and religious health systems that take part or refuse (*check*); hospice and palliative care; organ donation; funeral homes and the funeral trade (the National Funeral Directors Association); cemeteries kept by churches, towns and families; hospital and hospice chaplains; grief support groups; museums and universities returning ancestors' remains to the nations, as the nations describe it; churches' practice on funerals and on burying suicides.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** "speak no ill of the dead"; shame on kin who skip a funeral; the old Catholic refusal of church burial to suicides (eased in 1983 *check*); posthumous honors and disgrace; desecrating a grave as an insult to the dead and their kin.
- **Expression:** *calaveras literarias* (mock epitaph verses); José Guadalupe Posada's prints and La Catrina; Mexican sayings about death; *corridos* that tell of deaths; *narco* tombs (Culiacán's Jardines del Humaya *check*); Pixar's *Coco* (2017); memoirs of grief (Didion, *The Year of Magical Thinking*, 2005); Kübler-Ross, *On Death and Dying* (1969); obituaries and epitaphs; mortuary art; songs ("Knockin' on Heaven's Door").
- **Exemplars:** the good death held up (saints and martyrs; Brittany Maynard, who moved to Oregon in 2014 for medical aid in dying); those who died for others (firefighters, soldiers); death's own figures (La Catrina; Santa Muerte; Jesús Malverde, the "narco saint" of Sinaloa); hospice founders (Cicely Saunders); contested figures.
- **History:** epidemics among Native nations (smallpox on the Northwest Coast in the 1770s and 1862); deaths in the Alta California missions; the California genocide (Madley, *An American Genocide*, 2016); the 1918 influenza; the AIDS epidemic in San Francisco; the drug-overdose crisis (British Columbia's public health emergency, 2016); COVID-19 and excess deaths in Mexico; the rise of cremation; the revival and spread of Día de Muertos.
- **Measures:** deaths by cause and place, by county (CDC WONDER; the National Vital Statistics System), by municipality (INEGI's registered-death statistics) and by province (Statistics Canada); cremation rates (CANA); assisted-dying reports (the Oregon Health Authority; Washington's Department of Health; California's Department of Public Health; Health Canada's annual MAID reports); belief in an afterlife (the GSS item POSTLIFE; Pew's 2021 survey on the afterlife *check*; WVS items on heaven, hell and life after death; the ISSP religion modules); ghost belief (Pew, 2009; Chapman University's Survey of American Fears); Mexico's ENCREER survey of beliefs and practices (2016 *check*); end-of-life care by region (the Dartmouth Atlas); advance-directive completion (*check*); the moral acceptability of doctor-assisted suicide (Gallup); organ-donor registration by state (*check*).
- **Variation:** religion; ethnicity and immigrant generation (for example, whether the family or the patient should be told a terminal diagnosis: Blackhall et al., 1995 *check*); class (what a funeral costs); rural and urban; generation; each nation's own practice; families marked by violent death.
- **Relations:** rebirth (its pair); crisis; inheritance (section 0); fear (Pisces); faith and meaning (Sagittarius); care (Cancer); beginning (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** animals (pet cemeteries, euthanizing pets, hunting); the dead as persons with interests (wills, respect for graves, the return of ancestors' remains); companies, languages and towns that "die"; social death (the shunned and the forgotten); machines "dying" or retired; present-day attitudes to griefbots and digital replicas of the dead.
- **Silences:** dying itself; the corpse; suicide (counted by county, seldom discussed); deaths at the border; Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (Canada's National Inquiry, 2019); deaths of unhoused people; miscarriage and stillbirth.
- **Change:** cremation overtaking burial; green burial and human composting; more people choosing MAID in Canada (Health Canada's reports *check*); the "death positive" movement (the Order of the Good Death, Los Angeles); celebrations of life in place of funerals; Día de Muertos spreading in the U.S.; remote funerals since COVID; online memorials.

**Key questions:** Is death an enemy to fight, a passage, or a homecoming? Are the dead gone, or still present and owed something?

## 2. Rebirth

**Working definition:** a new life that comes through an ending: the old self, place or way dies, and something renewed takes its place. It covers conversion, recovery and redemption, renewal after disaster, cultural revival, and beliefs that the dead return (resurrection, reincarnation).
**Facets:** religious conversion and being born again; recovery from addiction; redemption after crime; reinvention after a crisis or a brush with death; leaving a faith, a gang or a cult; rites of passage built as a death and a rebirth; land regrowing after fire and towns rebuilt after disaster; seasonal and world renewal; the revival of languages and ceremonies; resurrection and reincarnation.
**Neighbors and opposites:** transformation, regeneration, renewal, redemption, conversion, revival; stagnation, decline, a fixed nature. **Excess:** endless reinvention with no core; the convert's zeal. **Deficiency:** being stuck; the belief that people never change.

- **Words:** "born again", "saved", "a new man", "turn your life around", "hit rock bottom", "in recovery", "clean and sober", "redemption", "second chance", "comeback", "rise from the ashes"; Spanish *renacer*, *volví a nacer* (said after surviving a brush with death), *nueva vida*, *rehabilitarse*, *regenerarse*, *convertirse*, *cristiano* (in Mexico often an evangelical convert rather than any Christian *check*), *anexo* (an informal Mexican recovery house); *Regeneración*, the Flores Magón brothers' newspaper (1900), and the Movimiento Regeneración Nacional (Morena) today; the phoenix on San Francisco's flag; revival words in the nations' own languages, as published.
- **Beliefs and standing:** can people really change, and by what: grace, will, treatment, suffering or time? Christian rebirth (John 3; baptism); sudden evangelical conversion against gradual Catholic renewal through the sacraments; the spiritual awakening of the Twelve Steps; Buddhist rebirth and reincarnation beliefs; the American redemption story (McAdams, *The Redemptive Self*, 2006) against F. Scott Fitzgerald's "no second acts"; implicit theories of whether character is fixed (Dweck); Indigenous teachings on renewal as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** near-death experiences; the convert's tears and joy; withdrawal and sobriety; baptism by full immersion; bodily makeovers (weight loss; gender transition as people themselves describe it; tattoo removal after leaving a gang); the *temazcal* and sweat lodges as their keepers share them.
- **Everyday practice:** testimonies in church; Twelve-Step meetings and sobriety chips; changing careers or names; leaving a criminal past and finding work; leaving gangs (Homeboy Industries, Los Angeles); leaving a faith ("exvangelicals") or a cult; adult baptism; makeover culture; rebuilding after a fire.
- **Life course:** confirmation and adult conversion; rites of passage as death and rebirth (van Gennep; Turner); military boot camp as remaking; midlife reinvention; retirement as a new life; deathbed conversions.
- **Ritual:** baptism; Easter, Holy Week (*Semana Santa*) and Pentecost; revivals and crusades (Billy Graham's Los Angeles crusade, 1949); sobriety anniversaries; graduations from recovery programs; spring and new-year renewal; the World Renewal ceremonies of the Karuk, Hupa and Yurok and first-salmon ceremonies, as the nations share them (*check*); the burning of the Burning Man effigy; the reopening of a rebuilt town.
- **Institutions:** churches and their rules for conversion and readmission; recovery programs (Alcoholics Anonymous, 1935; treatment centers; Mexico's *anexos* and its Centros de Integración Juvenil); reentry programs run by charities and churches (Homeboy Industries; see beginning, Aries); restorative circles in schools and communities; boot camps and wilderness programs for troubled teenagers; makeover and life-coaching businesses; language revitalization (British Columbia's First Peoples' Cultural Council; the Master-Apprentice program of Advocates for Indigenous California Language Survival); Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) and its calls to action.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** the returning sinner celebrated (the prodigal son, Luke 15); the reformed addict as a speaker; suspicion of jailhouse conversion; the stigma of relapse and "backsliding"; sanctions on those who leave a faith; employers who won't hire someone with a past.
- **Expression:** conversion narratives (Augustine; *The Autobiography of Malcolm X*, 1965); "Amazing Grace" and gospel music; redemption films; comeback stories in sport; Johnny Cash's *At Folsom Prison* (1968); *testimonios*; phoenix emblems; murals of resurgence; recovery memoirs.
- **Exemplars:** the converted (Augustine; Malcolm X); the redeemed (the former gang members of Homeboy Industries); the recovered; towns rebuilt (San Francisco after 1906; Paradise, California, after the Camp Fire, 2018; Lytton, British Columbia, after the 2021 fire); revivals led by nations (the Makah's return to whaling, 1999, as the Makah describe it; language-revival leaders); prophets of renewal, as their own communities tell it (Wovoka of the Northern Paiute and the Ghost Dance, 1889-1890; Smohalla of the Wanapum and the Washat; John Slocum and the Indian Shaker Church, founded 1882 near Puget Sound).
- **History:** the Great Awakenings (the "burned-over district" of upstate New York, a Yankee seedbed); camp meetings; the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles (1906), the root of modern Pentecostalism; the Jesus Movement and Calvary Chapel (Costa Mesa, late 1960s); the human-potential movement (Esalen, Big Sur, 1962); the spread of the Twelve Steps; evangelical growth in Mexico; boarding and residential schools as a rebirth forced on Native children, and the resurgence since the 1960s and 1970s, told in the nations' own voices.
- **Measures:** born-again identity (the GSS item REBORN *check*; Gallup; Pew); religious switching (Pew's Religious Landscape Study, 2014 and 2023-2024); reincarnation belief (Pew, 2009 *check*); religion by municipality (INEGI's census) and by province (Statistics Canada, 2021); people in recovery (SAMHSA's National Survey on Drug Use and Health *check*); speakers of Indigenous languages over time (census language data; the First Peoples' Cultural Council's reports on the status of B.C. First Nations languages); how many households rebuild after a disaster (*check*).
- **Variation:** religious tradition (sudden conversion against slow growth); class (who can afford rehab or a fresh start); gender; generation; converts and non-converts in immigrant families; each nation's revival on its own terms.
- **Relations:** death (its pair); purging (cleansing before renewal); crisis (the turning point); beginning (Aries); faith (Sagittarius); hope (Aquarius); forgiveness (no home yet).
- **Who it applies to:** people; places (the phoenix city; urban renewal); companies (turnarounds and comebacks); nations and languages (revival); land (regrowth after fire; cultural burning as the nations practice it); species (present-day attitudes to de-extinction); machines (refurbishing; present-day attitudes to "bringing back" the dead as chatbots).
- **Silences:** relapse; conversions that didn't take; forced conversion (missions, boarding and residential schools); the pain of those a convert leaves behind.
- **Change:** "spiritual but not religious" and "healing journey" language; psychedelic therapy and retreats sold as rebirth (Oregon's psilocybin service centers *check*); deconversion movements; transformation stories online.

**Key questions:** Can a person truly become new, and who decides whether they have? Is rebirth given (grace), earned (the work of recovery) or cyclical (seasons, reincarnation)?

## 3. Purging

**Working definition** (proposed): getting rid of what is felt to be polluted, poisonous or corrupt, in a body, a mind, a group, an institution or a place, so the whole can be clean or healed again.
**Facets:** bodily cleansing (fasting, sweating, purgatives, "detox"); spiritual cleansing (confession, *limpias*, smudging as the nations share it); emotional release (catharsis, venting); clearing out (decluttering); cleaning up institutions (anti-corruption drives, firings, purges of officials); cleansing groups (excommunication, shunning, disenrollment, "cancel culture", purity tests); political purges and loyalty tests; cleaning up land and water; exposure as a purge (whistleblowing); scapegoating; at the extreme, mass expulsion and ethnic cleansing.
**Neighbors and opposites:** cleansing, purification, catharsis, elimination, detox, reform; pollution, contamination, retention, tolerance, cover-up. **Excess:** witch hunts, purity spirals, scapegoating, persecution. **Deficiency:** letting rot spread; hushing things up.

- **Words:** "purge", "cleanse", "detox", "clean house", "clear the air", "get it off your chest", "drain the swamp", "bad apples", "witch hunt", "cancel", "toxic" (Oxford's word of the year, 2018); Spanish *limpia* (a folk cleansing with herbs or an egg), *barrida* (*check*), *depurar* and *depuración* (used of voter rolls and of staff purges), *purga* (a purgative, and a purge), *desahogarse* (to unburden oneself), *sacar los trapos sucios* (to air dirty laundry), *la ropa sucia se lava en casa* (dirty laundry is washed at home), *mal de ojo*, *empacho*, *susto*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** what counts as pollution, and why (Douglas, *Purity and Danger*, 1966); sanctity and degradation as a moral foundation (Haidt's Moral Foundations); folk theories of toxins, and of illness from envy, fright or a blocked stomach (*curanderismo*: Trotter and Chavira, *Curanderismo*, 1981); the catharsis theory of emotion and the research against venting (Bushman, 2002); confession and penance; revivalist cleansing of sin; purification in Indigenous practice as the nations state it; whether exposing wrongdoing heals a group or shames it.
- **Feeling and the body:** disgust (the Disgust Scale of Haidt, McCauley and Rozin); fasting, juice cleanses, colonics; sweat baths (the *temazcal*; sweat lodges as the nations share them; saunas); crying as release; bathing and handwashing customs; purgatives given to children in folk practice (*check*); purging in eating disorders (a silence).
- **Everyday practice:** spring cleaning; decluttering (Marie Kondo); detox diets; fasting in Lent and at other times; confessing to a priest or venting to a friend; therapy; unfriending and cutting off kin; firing; neighborhood cleanups and graffiti removal; clearing homeless encampments; content moderation online.
- **Life course:** confession before First Communion; purity pledges for teenagers; detox at the start of recovery; clearing a house after a death (Swedish "death cleaning" as an example from elsewhere).
- **Ritual:** Lent and Ash Wednesday; Yom Kippur; Ramadan; *limpias* and the *temazcal*; smudging and sweat lodges as the nations share them; burning the old year (Ecuador's *Año Viejo* effigies and Valencia's Fallas as examples from elsewhere; the Burning Man effigy); the burning of Judas on Holy Saturday (see betrayal); baptism.
- **Institutions:** church discipline (excommunication; shunning); professional bodies that expel members; schools that suspend and expel; workplaces that fire after a scandal; social media platforms' bans; loyalty oaths (the University of California oath fight, 1949-1951); the House Un-American Activities Committee and the Hollywood blacklist; anti-corruption campaigns by governments and newspapers; voter-roll maintenance; environmental cleanup (EPA's Superfund; the Hanford Site in Washington; Canada's Federal Contaminated Sites Inventory); quarantine; tribal disenrollment disputes in California (*check*; researched in the nations' own voices); encampment sweeps by cities; deportation.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the reformer who cleans house; the purger called a zealot; the whistleblower blamed rather than thanked; firing, exile and ostracism for the purged; whether there is a way back after being "canceled".
- **Expression:** Arthur Miller, *The Crucible* (1953); films about the blacklist; muckraking journalism; sayings such as *la ropa sucia se lava en casa*; songs of release; slogans ("drain the swamp"); *The Purge* films.

## 9. Power

**Working definition:** the capacity to make things happen and to affect others, with or against their will; and how a culture judges who holds it, how it is held and how it is used.
**Facets:** personal power (strength, charisma, influence); social power (status, money, connections); political power; economic power (corporations, monopoly); power over (domination), power to (capacity) and power with (collective power); hidden power (behind the scenes); spiritual power, as believers describe it; empowerment; powerlessness; abuse of power.
**Neighbors and opposites:** influence, clout, might, strength, dominance; authority (legitimate power: Capricorn); powerlessness, weakness, vulnerability. **Excess:** tyranny, corruption, abuse. **Deficiency:** powerlessness, learned helplessness.

- **Words:** "power", "powerful", "clout", "pull", "connections", "it's who you know", "power broker", "power play", "power couple", "the Man", "the establishment", "speak truth to power", "empowerment"; Spanish *poder*, *poderoso*, *influencias*, *palancas* and *tener palanca* (to have pull), *el que tiene más saliva traga más pinole* (whoever has more saliva swallows more corn flour: the better-placed get more), *cacique* and *caciquismo* (the local strongman), *el dedazo* (the outgoing president's choice of successor under the PRI), *el gran elector*, *charrismo sindical* (union bosses tied to the state), *el poder tras el trono*, *coyote* (a fixer for paperwork), *hueso* (a government job given as a favor), *mordida* (a bribe); the double sense of "power" in the dam-built Northwest (the Bonneville Power Administration).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is power good, dangerous, or simply necessary? Acton's "power tends to corrupt"; the Protestant and republican distrust of concentrated power; Catholic teaching on legitimate authority and the common good; Weber's three types of authority; French and Raven's bases of power (1959); whether people want a strong leader (WVS); patron and client (*patrón*); Indigenous ideas of power and leadership as the nations state them, including hereditary chiefs alongside elected band councils (the Wet'suwet'en dispute over the Coastal GasLink pipeline, 2020).
- **Feeling and the body:** size and strength; posture and voice (the "power pose" research, contested); fear and deference in the body; the stress of powerlessness (Britain's Whitehall studies as an example from elsewhere); the swagger of the powerful.
- **Everyday practice:** who decides at home; office politics; networking; favors and *palancas*; bribes; union organizing; voting, lobbying and boycotts; neighborhood associations; protest; who speaks at public meetings.
- **Life course:** children's power in families; playground pecking orders; adolescent power struggles; power in marriages; elders' power and its loss; inherited power (family firms, political dynasties, hereditary chiefs).
- **Ritual:** inaugurations and swearings-in; Mexico's presidential sash and the yearly *informe*; the September 16 military parade; feasts that confirm rank and names, as the nations describe them; marches and rallies as rituals of counter-power; state funerals.
- **Institutions:** political parties and political machines; unions; business lobbies and trade associations; the press as a check on the powerful; churches as power brokers; the recall and the ballot initiative as citizens' power (Gray Davis recalled, 2003; Gavin Newsom's survival, 2021; see initiative, Aries); Mexico's creed of *no reelección* (*Sufragio efectivo, no reelección*); the *ejido* assembly; the nations' own governments, hereditary and elected, as the nations describe them; big employers in company towns.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** deference and access for the powerful; abuse punished by impeachment, recall, scandal and #MeToo; the powerless stigmatized or romanticized; resentment of *caciques*; envy of the powerful.
- **Expression:** Machiavelli; *The Godfather*; Frank Norris, *The Octopus* (1901, the railroad's grip on California); *Chinatown*; *There Will Be Blood* (California oil); Mexican political novels (Martín Luis Guzmán, *La sombra del caudillo*, 1929; Carlos Fuentes, *La muerte de Artemio Cruz*, 1962); the film *La ley de Herodes* (1999); *corridos* of powerful men; murals of power and resistance (Diego Rivera; Chicano murals); the slogans of Black Power, Brown Power and Red Power.
- **Exemplars:** the Big Four of the Central Pacific (Leland Stanford and his partners); William Randolph Hearst; Porfirio Díaz; Plutarco Elías Calles of Sonora, the *Jefe Máximo* who ruled from behind three presidents (the *Maximato*, 1928-1934); César Chávez (the power of the organized poor); Billy Frank Jr. (leader of the fish-ins); tech founders; the local *cacique*; contested figures.

## 10. Crisis

**Working definition:** a turning point of acute danger or breakdown, when things cannot go on as they were and must change, for a person, a family, a community or a country.
**Facets:** personal crises (illness, divorce, job loss, a mental-health crisis, the "midlife crisis"); family crises; economic crises; natural disasters; epidemics and public-health emergencies; political crises; slow or chronic troubles named as crises (housing, overdoses, homelessness); crisis competence (the people who keep their heads); crises manufactured or exploited ("never let a crisis go to waste"); crisis as test, punishment, chance or turning point.
**Neighbors and opposites:** emergency, disaster, catastrophe, breakdown, turning point, upheaval; stability, routine, normalcy, calm. **Excess:** crisis mentality, alarmism, manufactured drama, panic. **Deficiency:** complacency, denial, failure to prepare.

- **Words:** "crisis", "emergency", "disaster", "breakdown", "meltdown", "rock bottom", "midlife crisis", "crisis mode", "hold it together", "hunker down", "the Big One" (the expected great earthquake), "prepper", "polycrisis", "never let a crisis go to waste"; Spanish *crisis*, *emergencia*, *desastre*, *catástrofe*, *damnificados* (disaster victims), *tronar* (to go bust), *aguantar vara* (to take the strain), *echarle ganas*, *el error de diciembre* (the 1994 peso crisis) and *la crisis del 82* (crises named by year), *ataque de nervios* (a crisis of the nerves described among Caribbean Latinos, as an example from elsewhere), *susto*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is a crisis a disaster to survive, a test of character or faith, a punishment, a chance, or a needed break that lets change come? The popular myth that the Chinese word for crisis means "danger plus opportunity" (debunked by Victor Mair), as evidence of the wish to find opportunity in crisis; end-times belief (Pew on expectations of Christ's return, 2010 *check*); resilience as an ideal; fatalism against preparation; composure as a virtue ("keep calm"); trauma-informed views of crisis.
- **Feeling and the body:** fight, flight or freeze; panic and shock; calm in a crisis as a prized trait; the body after disaster (heart attacks after earthquakes; post-traumatic stress); grief; exhaustion; who may break down and who must hold together.
- **Everyday practice:** emergency kits and "go bags"; earthquake and wildfire drills; evacuation; prepping; neighbors checking on neighbors; mutual aid; crisis lines (988 in the U.S. since 2022); families closing ranks; borrowing from kin; crowdfunding medical and disaster costs; hoarding (toilet paper in 2020).
- **Life course:** children's earthquake and lockdown drills; teenage crises; the "midlife crisis" (Elliott Jaques, 1965; doubted as a universal stage by later research); crises of retirement and old age; how a family meets a crisis in one member (interventions; sitting up with the sick).
- **Ritual:** yearly drills (the Great ShakeOut each October; Mexico's national drill on September 19, the anniversary of the 1985 and 2017 earthquakes); commemorations of disasters (San Francisco's gathering at Lotta's Fountain at 5:12 a.m. each April 18); vigils and prayer services; days of remembrance; processions and masses after disasters.
- **Institutions:** emergency management (FEMA; California's Office of Emergency Services; British Columbia's emergency-management ministry *check* for its current name; Mexico's civil-protection system, created after the 1985 earthquake); the Mexican army's disaster plan (Plan DN-III-E); Mexico's disaster fund FONDEN, abolished in 2021 (*check*); earthquake early warning (Mexico's SASMEX, 1991; ShakeAlert on the U.S. West Coast); crisis lines and mobile crisis teams (CAHOOTS in Eugene, Oregon, since 1989, which sends medics and counselors); disaster declarations; earthquake insurance (the California Earthquake Authority); relief by the Red Cross, churches and Cáritas; central banks' crisis tools.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** honor for rescuers and first responders; leaders judged by their response, and voted out for it; looters and price-gougers scorned; blame for those who caused the crisis or ignored warnings; the one who warned, often ignored or blamed.
- **Expression:** disaster films; Steinbeck, *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939); *The Big Short*; Elena Poniatowska's chronicle of the 1985 earthquake (*Nada, nadie*, 1988); Kathryn Schulz, "The Really Big One" (*The New Yorker*, 2015), on the Cascadia earthquake; oral histories of the 1700 Cascadia earthquake and tsunami as the nations have shared them (gathered in Ludwin et al., 2005 *check*); disaster memes and jokes.
- **Exemplars:** first responders; the *topos* of Tlatelolco, volunteer rescuers who first organized in the 1985 earthquake; leaders praised for their crisis response; ordinary neighbors who organized; counter-exemplars: those who fled, panicked or profited.
- **History:** the 1700 Cascadia earthquake and tsunami (dated through Japanese tsunami records); the 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire; the Dust Bowl and the Great Depression; the 1964 tsunami at Crescent City; the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens; the 1985 Mexico City earthquake (often told as the birth of Mexican civil society *check*); the 1982 and 1994-1995 Mexican economic crises; the dot-com bust (2000-2001); California's electricity crisis (2000-2001), worsened by traders' manipulation (Enron); Hurricane Odile in Baja California Sur (2014); the 2008 financial crisis; wildfires (the Camp Fire, 2018; Lytton, 2021); British Columbia's heat dome and atmospheric river (2021); the overdose emergency; COVID-19; the housing crisis.
- **Measures:** disaster declarations by county (OpenFEMA); household preparedness (FEMA's National Household Survey; Statistics Canada's Survey of Emergency Preparedness and Resilience *check*); Mexico's disaster records (CENAPRED); hardship during crises (the Census Bureau's Household Pulse Survey, 2020-2024); 988 calls by state; overdose deaths (CDC; the BC Coroners Service); unemployment claims; consumer sentiment (the University of Michigan); the WorldRiskIndex (country); medical and disaster crowdfunding by county (*check*).
- **Variation:** rich and poor face crises differently; region (which hazards); rural and urban; immigrants (fear of asking for help); Native nations and reserves (distance from emergency services); generation; gender (who breaks down, who copes, who cares for others).
- **Relations:** death and rebirth; control (crisis as loss of control); power (crises gather power to leaders); trust (crises test institutions); purging (crisis as the moment of clearing out); courage (Aries); fear (Pisces); stability and security (Taurus); hope (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** persons, families, firms, towns, countries; ecosystems and the climate ("climate crisis"); groups others name as a "crisis" (the "border crisis", the "homeless crisis": who gets framed that way, and by whom); machines (outages and system failures; present-day attitudes to AI as a crisis, or as a tool in crisis response).
- **Silences:** men's mental-health crises; family crises kept hidden; slow harms that never get the name of crisis (Rob Nixon, *Slow Violence*, 2011); long-term drinking-water advisories on First Nations reserves.
- **Change:** talk of permanent crisis and "polycrisis"; more frequent climate disasters; preparedness culture; crisis lines and crisis teams of medics and counselors spreading; crisis politics.

**Key questions:** Is a crisis a disaster to survive, a test of character, or a chance to change? Who is expected to step up in a crisis: the person, the family, the neighbors, the church or the state?
