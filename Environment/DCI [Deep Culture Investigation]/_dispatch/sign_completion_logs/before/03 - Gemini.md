# Avenues of investigation: 03 Gemini (communication and learning)

**What this file is.** For each Gemini concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Gemini concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; it is settled with the author just before Gemini's pass): communication, information, learning, curiosity, connection, duality, siblings, mediation, gossip. Travel is also in Gemini's ten, but its proposed home is Sagittarius, so it is a cross-reference here (end of file); Gemini's traditional area of short journeys is covered in section 0. Questions to settle:
- **Learning and education.** Learning is here; education is in Sagittarius's ten. This file assumes learning covers how people come to know and do things (at home, by watching and pitching in, in early school, at work, all life long), and education covers schooling as an institution and higher learning. Schools sit on the line.
- **Gossip and rumor.** Rumor (from Gemini's own concept table) is researched under gossip here. Gossip is also in four other signs' tables (Aries, Virgo, Scorpio, Pisces).
- **Mediation and diplomacy.** Mediation is in six signs' tables (a Tier 2 concept); diplomacy is in Libra's ten. This file assumes mediation is the third party between two sides (and the go-between in general), and diplomacy is tact and negotiation between the parties themselves, states included.
- **Duality.** In Gemini's material the word means holding two ideas, moods or directions at once. As a concept of human life it needs a working definition; the one in section 6 (living with two-ness: double identities, paired worldviews, contradiction, two faces) is proposed. It touches the near-synonym group deception / lying / duplicity / pretense.
- **Siblings** is a relationship more than an idea. It is researched as how a culture understands the sibling bond, with twins and sibling rivalry inside it.
- **Near neighbors kept apart:** communication and conversation; information and knowledge (and truth, in Sagittarius); connection and friendship, community (Aquarius) and belonging (Cancer).

## 0. The domain: communication and learning

**Traditional areas:** communication, siblings and neighbors, short journeys, early learning. **The domain question:** how do people talk, learn and connect close to home?

- **The culture's style of talk as a whole.** High- and low-context communication (Hall, *Beyond Culture*, 1976); directness against indirectness; politeness systems (Brown and Levinson, *Politeness*, 1987); "communication" itself as a cultural term in American speech (Katriel and Philipsen, 1981; Carbaugh, *Talking American*, 1988); the ethnography of speaking (Hymes; Bauman and Sherzer, eds., *Explorations in the Ethnography of Speaking*, 1974), with local studies as models (Philipsen's "Teamsterville", 1975; Basso on Western Apache silence, "To Give Up on Words", 1970); business-culture guides that place countries on scales of directness and feedback (Meyer, *The Culture Map*, 2014: practitioner, country level only).
- **The place's languages.** Which languages people speak at home, and how well they speak the dominant one (ACS, by county and tract; Statistics Canada's mother tongue, home language and knowledge of official languages; INEGI's speakers of Indigenous languages, by municipality); English varieties (the *Atlas of North American English*, Labov, Ash and Boberg, 2006; the California vowel shift; Canadian raising); northern Mexican Spanish; Chinuk Wawa, the contact language of the Northwest, now taught by the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde (their dictionary, 2012); Indigenous languages and their revival, as the nations report it (the First Peoples' Cultural Council's reports on the status of B.C. First Nations languages; tribal language programs; Mexico's INALI); sign languages (ASL; Lengua de Señas Mexicana); immigrant languages (Chinese languages, Tagalog, Vietnamese, Korean; Mixtec, Zapotec and Triqui among Indigenous Mexican migrants in California and Oregon).
- **Language in public life.** The English-only movement and those who oppose it (U.S. English, founded 1983 *check*); bilingual-education and dual-language programs, and the public fights over them; Canada's image of itself as a bilingual country; Native-language teaching certificates in Washington and Oregon (*check*); government services in more than one language (forms, ballots, signs, phone lines); hospital and clinic interpreters.
- **Bilingualism and code-switching.** Spanglish and the fights over it (Zentella, *Growing Up Bilingual*, 1997, a New York study, as a model); Chicano English (Fought, *Chicano English in Context*, 2003); attitudes to accents (matched-guise experiments; perceptual dialectology, after Preston: where people believe "correct" or "pleasant" English is spoken); how many generations a language survives (Rumbaut, Massey and Bean, "Linguistic Life Expectancies", 2006, on Southern California); French immersion in British Columbia; dual-language schools (see duality).
- **Neighbors and neighborliness** (an area the concepts don't cover). Knowing neighbors' names; borrowing and favors; block parties and National Night Out; neighborhood watch; Nextdoor and neighborhood Facebook groups; homeowners' associations as neighborhood governments; the *vecindad* (housing around a shared courtyard) and the *colonia*; neighbors made into *compadres*; "the Seattle Freeze" (also in Aries). Measures: Pew on how many neighbors people know, by urban, suburban and rural (2018 *check*); the Current Population Survey's Civic Engagement and Volunteering supplement (talking with neighbors, doing favors for them), by state and metro; the Vancouver Foundation's Connections and Engagement surveys (2012, 2017 *check*); Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on social identity (knowing and trusting neighbors *check*); INEGI's victimization survey (ENVIPE) on neighbors organizing for safety (*check*).
- **Short journeys and local travel** (the part of travel that stays here). Commuting mode, time and distance (ACS journey to work, by county and tract; Statistics Canada's commuting data; INEGI's origin-destination surveys for large metros *check*); walking and cycling (Portland's bicycle culture; Vancouver's seawall); transit (SkyTrain, BART, light rail; Mexico's *combis*, *peseros* and *colectivos*); car culture (Los Angeles freeways; lowriders as mobile art); ferries as everyday travel on the Salish Sea (BC Ferries; Washington State Ferries); daily border crossing (*transfronterizos* who live in Tijuana and study or work in San Diego; the Bureau of Transportation Statistics' monthly crossings by port); errands and the walkable radius (Walk Score); the Sunday drive and the day trip; travel time in the American Time Use Survey; how far children may go alone (studies of children's independent mobility; Hillman, *One False Move*, 1990, in England, as a model).
- **Media habits.** Newspapers, radio, television, public broadcasting (OPB, KQED, KUOW, CBC), social media and messaging apps: see information (section 2).
- **Speech ways** (Fischer's folkway for Gemini):
  - accent and dialect, from the founders' speech on (Fischer traces each founding culture's speech: the Yankee, the Southern, the Quaker plain style, the backcountry); the Spanish of the Californios and *norteño* speech; Yankee and Midland speech carried to Oregon; regional dictionaries (*Dictionary of American Regional English*; *Dictionary of Canadianisms on Historical Principles*; *Diccionario del español de México*);
  - regional words: "hella" (Northern California); "spendy" (Pacific Northwest *check*); "the 5" and "the 101" (Southern California); Chinuk Wawa words in local English, such as "skookum" (strong, good), "saltchuck" (the sea) and "tyee" (chief; a big salmon);
  - forms of address: first names in the West; *tú* and *usted*, and who uses which with whom; *don* and *doña*; titles as address (*licenciado*, *ingeniero*, *maestro*); "sir" and "ma'am"; "auntie" and "uncle" for elders in many Native communities (*check*);
  - politeness formulas: *mande* (the Mexican answer when one is called), *con permiso*, *provecho*; "please", "thank you" and Canadian "sorry";
  - oratory and public speaking: speakers at Northwest Coast feasts, as the nations describe them; sermons; union and movement speeches; public comment at city council meetings;
  - swearing and taboo words; *albures* (Mexican double-meaning wordplay); slang (*güey*, *neta*, *chido*, *órale*; Chicano *caló*; "dude");
  - silence and pace: who may stay silent, how long a pause lasts, whether overlapping talk is rude or warm;
  - storytelling as a speech genre: stories as the nations share them; *cuentos*; tall tales; the performed joke.
- **Learning ways** (Fischer's folkway for Gemini; runner-up Sagittarius):
  - literacy, past and present (signatures against marks on old marriage records as a historical measure; adult skills in PIAAC, country level, with U.S. county estimates *check*; INEGI's literacy rate by municipality);
  - who teaches the young: parents, older siblings, grandparents, elders, clergy, teachers, peers, screens;
  - schooling's founders: Yankee common schools carried to Oregon and Washington; mission schools; Mexico's Secretaría de Educación Pública (1921), its rural schools and cultural missions; boarding and residential schools (Chemawa Indian School in Salem; Sherman Institute in Riverside; Kamloops and the other B.C. schools), told in the nations' own voices first (the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, 2015; the U.S. Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative reports, 2022 and 2024);
  - books and libraries in daily life: public library use (the IMLS Public Libraries Survey, by library system); bookstores (Powell's in Portland); books in the home;
  - how knowledge is held and passed on: oral tradition, as the nations share it; family recipes and trades; apprenticeship; the internet;
  - the full set of avenues is under learning (section 3).
- **Social ways** (runner-up; home sign Aquarius): visiting, hospitality and clubs as the settings where talk happens; see `11 - Aquarius.md`.

## 1. Communication

**Working definition:** the exchange of meaning between people, by speech, writing, gesture, silence and media; and what a culture believes good communication is and what it is for.
**Facets:** face-to-face talk and conversation; small talk; directness and indirectness; listening and silence; writing and letters; public speaking and oratory; the body's signals; communicating at a distance (mail, telephone, messaging); persuasion; communicating across languages (interpreting, translation, children as language brokers); "communication" as an ideal of relationships ("we need to talk").
**Neighbors and opposites:** conversation, dialogue, talk, expression, rapport; silence, withdrawal, miscommunication, the silent treatment. **Excess:** oversharing, talking at people, talk that says nothing. **Deficiency:** stonewalling, not speaking up. Where a culture prizes silence, silence is not a deficiency; that is itself a finding to look for.

- **Words:** "communication", "open communication", "we need to talk", "share" ("thanks for sharing"), "reach out", "touch base", "circle back" (the corporate register), "small talk", "straight talk", "beat around the bush", "passive-aggressive", "a man of few words"; Spanish *comunicación*, *platicar* and *la plática* (Mexican for a chat, and for a talk given to a group), *hablar claro*, *con rodeos*, *echar indirectas* (to drop pointed hints), *albur*, *cantinflear* (to talk at length and say nothing, after the comedian Cantinflas; in the Real Academia's dictionary), *dar el avión* (to pretend to listen *check*), *quedar bien* (to say what keeps the peace); *tú*, *usted*, *ustedes* and *vosotros* (the last absent in Mexico); Chinuk Wawa *wawa*, "talk, speak", in the language's own name; metaphors of talk as a pipe that carries meaning ("getting it across"; Lakoff and Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By*, 1980).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is talking things out the way to solve problems, or does talk make them worse? Talk as action or as cheap ("talk is cheap"; *del dicho al hecho hay mucho trecho*); the plain style (Puritan and Quaker plain speech); therapy culture's belief that "communication" is the key to marriage and health (Cameron, *Good to Talk?*, 2000); free speech as a founding value and a point of pride, and where people draw its limits (insult, offense, hate speech); religious teaching on the tongue (the Epistle of James; the Catholic sins of speech); prayer as speech with God; Indigenous teachings on careful speech and on listening, as the nations state them; philosophy (Habermas on communicative action).
- **Feeling and the body:** eye contact (whether a direct gaze reads as honesty or as disrespect toward elders: a question to research community by community, not a finding); talking with the hands; personal space and touch (Hall's proxemics; contact and noncontact cultures); voice volume, pitch and pace; uptalk and vocal fry; how much people smile at strangers; laughter; the fear of public speaking (Chapman University's Survey of American Fears *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** greetings and small talk with strangers and cashiers ("how are you?" as a greeting, not a question); who talks at family meals; calling against texting; voicemail and email manners; long Mexican goodbyes against leaving without a word; interrupting and overlapping; how meetings run and who speaks in them; giving feedback at work; talking with doctors (interpreters; *promotoras de salud*); children as language brokers for immigrant parents; WhatsApp as the family channel across borders; the languages of signs, menus and forms.
- **Life course:** whether adults talk to babies, and how (Ochs and Schieffelin, 1984, on language socialization); first words; "please" and "thank you"; "use your words"; children "seen and not heard"; speech therapy; show-and-tell, speech and debate; thank-you notes; job interviews; elders' storytelling; telling the dying their diagnosis (see autonomy in Aries); obituaries that praise "a great communicator" or "a man of few words".
- **Ritual:** speeches at weddings, graduations and funerals (*brindis*; eulogies); the sermon; the State of the Union; Mexico's *Grito de Independencia* (the night of September 15), a ritual utterance; the town hall; the potlatch and feast as public witnessing, where guests are paid to witness and remember, as the nations describe it; language days (International Mother Language Day, February 21; the International Decade of Indigenous Languages, 2022-2032).
- **Institutions:** postal services (USPS, Canada Post, Correos de México); public broadcasters; government interpreting and translation services (forms and phone lines in many languages); the interpreting professions (medical and sign-language interpreters); Deaf schools and communities (the California School for the Deaf); school speech and debate programs; Toastmasters International (founded 1924 in Santa Ana, California); churches as schools of public speech (the sermon; the testimony); communication training at work; couples therapy.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for being "articulate", "a good listener", "a great communicator" (and "articulate" as a backhanded compliment to Black speakers, a researched complaint); sanctions on rudeness, swearing and accents (accent bias in hiring and at work); the silent treatment, "ghosting" and blocking as sanctions; tone policing; "mansplaining".
- **Expression:** proverbs (*en boca cerrada no entran moscas*; *el que no habla, Dios no lo oye*; "the squeaky wheel gets the grease"; "silence is golden"); the comedy of talk (Cantinflas; *albures*; stand-up); Richard Rodriguez, *Hunger of Memory* (1982), on trading Spanish at home for public English in Sacramento; films about miscommunication; songs about calls and letters; stories as the nations share them.
- **Exemplars:** orators and "great communicators" (Ronald Reagan's nickname); Chief Seattle's speech, a contested text known through Henry Smith's 1887 version and a 1970s screenplay rewrite; organizers who spoke for others (César Chávez, Dolores Huerta); interpreters (La Malinche, contested; Sacagawea, Lemhi Shoshone); teachers and broadcasters; anti-exemplars: the blowhard, the mumbler, the liar.
- **History:** the founders' speech (see speech ways); Chinuk Wawa as the trade language of the Northwest from the fur trade into the 1900s; the missions' language policy; the Pony Express and the transcontinental telegraph (1860-1861); television's invention in San Francisco (Farnsworth, 1927); the first ARPANET message, from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute (1969); the suppression of Native languages in boarding and residential schools; Spanish-language media (*La Opinión*, Los Angeles, 1926); smartphones and social media from the Bay Area.
- **Measures:** ACS language spoken at home and English-speaking ability (county and tract); Statistics Canada's home language; INEGI's Indigenous-language speakers; Pew's surveys of Latinos on Spanish use; the American Time Use Survey's "socializing and communicating" and "telephone calls, mail and email" (state, with pooled years); Pew's mobile and texting surveys; INEGI's ENDUTIH and Statistics Canada's Canadian Internet Use Survey; cross-language studies of turn-taking and pause length (Stivers et al., 2009, ten languages); the frequency of "communication" in corpora (Google Books Ngram; the Corpus of Contemporary American English).
- **Variation:** gender (Tannen's "genderlect" thesis and its critics, such as Cameron, *The Myth of Mars and Venus*, 2007); class (Bernstein's restricted and elaborated codes; Heath, *Ways with Words*, 1983, as a model study of three communities); generation; Deaf and hard-of-hearing people; neurodivergent people; region ("the Seattle Freeze"); each Indigenous nation's own norms; immigrant generation.
- **Relations:** gossip, mediation and connection (this sign); self-assertion (Aries); self-expression (Leo); diplomacy (Libra); secrecy (Scorpio); honesty and truth (Sagittarius); empathy (Pisces).
- **Who it applies to:** animals (talking to pets; the distinct call dialects of the Southern Resident orcas of the Salish Sea); plants; ancestors and the dead (prayer; Día de Muertos altars); companies ("brand voice"; corporate speak); machines (present-day attitudes to voice assistants and chatbots; Pew surveys on AI use *check*).
- **Silences:** what families don't talk about (money, sex, death, mental illness, immigration status, the residential school years); people shut out of public talk by language or deafness; sexuality left unspoken in families and at work.
- **Change:** texting replacing calls; video calls and remote work; emoji; AI-written messages; the decline of letters; polarization ("we can't talk anymore"; depolarizing workshops such as Braver Angels); Indigenous-language revival.

**Key questions:** Is talking things out the cure or part of the problem? Is it better to say exactly what you mean, or to say it so no one loses face? Whose speech counts in public?

## 2. Information

**Working definition:** what is known or reported about the world, and how a culture gathers, passes on, trusts, withholds and uses it: news, facts, data, records.
**Facets:** news and the press; facts and data; official information (statistics, public records); access (libraries, the internet, broadband); trust in sources; misinformation and disinformation; privacy and personal data; censorship; information overload; expert knowledge against local knowledge; who owns knowledge (Indigenous data sovereignty); the right to know.
**Neighbors and opposites:** news, facts, data, knowledge, intelligence; ignorance, misinformation, secrecy. **Excess:** overload, surveillance, "too much information". **Deficiency:** ignorance, being cut off, news deserts.

- **Words:** "information", "info", "data", "the news", "fake news", "misinformation", "disinformation", "alternative facts", "do your own research", "FYI", "TMI", "need to know", "in the loop", "infodemic" (WHO, 2020); Spanish *información*, *pedir informes* (to ask at a counter *check*), *noticias*, *datos*, *enterarse*, *estar al tanto*, *la nota roja* (Mexican crime journalism), *desinformación*, *noticias falsas*, *transparencia*, *chayote* and *chayotero* (Mexican slang for a bribe paid to a journalist, and the journalist who takes it).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is information a public good, a property or a danger? The right to know; "knowledge is power"; trust in science and experts against "doing your own research"; conspiracy thinking; revealed against gathered knowledge in religious teaching; "information wants to be free" (Stewart Brand, at the first Hackers Conference, Marin County, 1984); tech-libertarian ideals (the Electronic Frontier Foundation, now in San Francisco); Indigenous rules that some knowledge is owned and restricted, as the nations state them, and Indigenous data governance (the First Nations principles of OCAP: ownership, control, access and possession; the CARE principles, 2019); Mexico's culture of *transparencia* after 2002.
- **Feeling and the body:** the pleasure of being in the know; the shame of not knowing; information anxiety, "doomscrolling", the fear of missing out; news avoidance (measured by the Reuters Institute); screen time and sleep.
- **Everyday practice:** where people get news (TV, radio, newspapers, social media, WhatsApp groups, family, church, work); Spanish-language TV and radio (Univision, Telemundo); word of mouth among farmworkers; community radio for Indigenous Mexican migrants in California (Radio Bilingüe, Fresno *check*; Radio Indígena, Oxnard *check*); bulletin boards and Nextdoor; looking up symptoms online; Wikipedia; checking weather, tides, and fire and smoke maps; reading labels and reviews.
- **Life course:** teaching children where to find things out; library cards for children; media-literacy teaching in schools (Washington's and California's curricula *check*); online-safety rules; elders targeted by scams; elders as keepers of knowledge (Indigenous knowledge keepers; Elders in residence at schools and universities).
- **Ritual:** the State of the Union, the throne speeches of Canada and the provinces, and Mexico's annual *Informe de Gobierno* (September 1); the Mexican president's daily morning press conference, the *mañanera* (from 2018); election-night returns; the evening news and the Sunday paper; Sunshine Week (March, U.S.) and the International Day for Universal Access to Information (September 28).
- **Institutions:** public and national libraries (Library and Archives Canada; the Biblioteca Vasconcelos); archives (national, state, provincial and tribal); statistical agencies and trust in them (the Census Bureau, Statistics Canada, INEGI; Canada's 2010 decision to make the long-form census voluntary, reversed for 2016); public records offices and open-data portals (Mexico's transparency institute, INAI, dissolved in 2025 *check*); the press and public broadcasters; journalism schools and press associations; tech platforms (Google, Meta, X, all from the Bay Area); fact-checkers (Snopes; PolitiFact); the First Nations Information Governance Centre; 211 helplines.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** being well informed as status; "know-it-all" as a sanction; whistleblowers honored and condemned (Daniel Ellsberg, who copied the Pentagon Papers at RAND in Santa Monica; Frances Haugen on Facebook, 2021); doxxing; platform penalties for misinformation; violence against journalists (Mexico's toll in the records of the Committee to Protect Journalists and Article 19).
- **Expression:** the *corrido* as a news ballad (Paredes, *With His Pistol in His Hand*, 1958, on the border *corrido*); the *nota roja* and the magazine *¡Alarma!*; newspaper mottos; investigative-journalism films; satire (*The Onion*); spy and hacker fiction; the Whole Earth Catalog's "access to tools" (Menlo Park, 1968).
- **Exemplars:** journalists (Rubén Salazar, killed by a sheriff's deputy's tear-gas projectile during the 1970 Chicano Moratorium in Los Angeles); librarians; whistleblowers; Indigenous knowledge keepers and language archivists; anti-exemplars: propagandists, hoaxers, the gossip (section 9).
- **History:** gold-rush news by letter and newspaper; Hearst and yellow journalism (the *San Francisco Examiner*); the Spanish-language press after 1848 (*El Clamor Público*, Los Angeles, 1855 *check*); the Mexican press under the PRI and the *chayote*; the silence around the 1968 Tlatelolco massacre; Silicon Valley and the internet; the fight over the records of residential schools (the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation); the COVID-19 "infodemic".
- **Measures:** Gallup's trust in mass media (annual, national); Pew's news-platform and trust surveys; the Reuters Institute Digital News Report (country, for the U.S., Canada and Mexico: trust, news avoidance, news by WhatsApp); the World Values Survey's confidence in the press and television, and its items on where people get information (*check*); Northwestern's Medill "State of Local News" (news deserts, by county); ACS computer and internet subscription (county and tract) and the FCC's broadband maps; INEGI's ENDUTIH (state); Statistics Canada's Canadian Internet Use Survey; public-library use (IMLS); the Wellcome Global Monitor on trust in science (country); the World Press Freedom Index and Freedom House's Freedom on the Net (country); census self-response rates by tract as a sign of trust in official data collection.
- **Variation:** age (news sources); partisanship (trust in media); language (studies of Spanish-language misinformation *check*); rural broadband gaps; each nation's data sovereignty; immigrants' fear of giving information to government (census undercounts; the 2019 fight over a citizenship question).
- **Relations:** communication, curiosity, learning, gossip (this sign); truth, honesty and freedom (Sagittarius); secrecy and trust (Scorpio); analysis (Virgo).
- **Who it applies to:** who may know what about whom: citizens about governments, governments about citizens, companies about customers, parents about children, nations about data on their own members; animals tagged and tracked (salmon, wolves); machines (present-day attitudes to AI search and chatbot answers, and how far people trust them *check*).
- **Silences:** what is not recorded (the data gap on missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls: the Urban Indian Health Institute's 2018 report, Seattle; Canada's National Inquiry, 2019); people missing from the data (the undocumented); classified records; deaths not counted.
- **Change:** algorithmic feeds; AI-generated content; the collapse of local newspapers; news moving into private group chats; growing worry over privacy and personal data; the Indigenous data sovereignty movement; the end of Mexico's transparency institute.

**Key questions:** Is information a public good, a property or a danger? Whom do people trust to tell them what is true, and who has the right to know what?

## 3. Learning

**Working definition:** how people come to know and to do: in the family, by watching and pitching in, through play, in early school, at work and across a life. (Schooling as an institution and higher learning: education, Sagittarius, proposed.)
**Facets:** early learning (speech, walking, the first years); learning by observing and pitching in; schooling; learning a trade; self-teaching and lifelong learning; learning a second language; learning from elders, stories and land; learning from mistakes; learning manners (*educación*).
**Neighbors and opposites:** education, schooling, study, training, apprenticeship, teaching; ignorance, forgetting, unlearning. **Excess:** credentialism; the endless student. **Deficiency:** incuriosity; being set in one's ways; anti-intellectualism.

- **Words:** "learn", "study", "smart", "book smart" against "street smart", "learn the hard way", "the school of hard knocks", "self-taught", "lifelong learner", "growth mindset", "nerd", "teacher's pet", "egghead"; Spanish *aprender*, *estudiar*, *educación* (in Mexican usage as much upbringing and manners as schooling: *bien educado*, *mal educado*), *maestro* and *maestra* (also an honorific for a master tradesman), *burro* (dunce), *matado* (Mexican slang for a grind *check*), *cerebrito*, *nadie nace sabiendo* (no one is born knowing), *la letra con sangre entra* (letters go in with blood: an old defense of harsh teaching), *acomedido* (alert to help without being asked; used in research on learning by pitching in *check*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** where ability comes from, talent or effort (Dweck's mindsets; Stevenson and Stigler, *The Learning Gap*, 1992, comparing U.S., Japanese and Chinese beliefs); what learning is for: getting ahead, being a good person (*educación*: Reese, Balzano, Gallimore and Goldenberg, 1995 *check*), serving God, carrying on a people; anti-intellectualism (Hofstadter, 1963); "learning by doing" (Dewey); Montessori, Waldorf and unschooling, strong in the West; Protestant Bible reading as a reason for literacy; the Latter-day Saint teaching that "the glory of God is intelligence"; Indigenous teachings on learning from elders and land, as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** learning with the hands (crafts, trades, cooking, farm work); the pleasure of mastery; test anxiety; the shame of not knowing; sitting still against learning on the move; diagnoses of ADHD and learning disabilities by state (CDC's National Survey of Children's Health).
- **Everyday practice:** homework and who helps with it; tutoring and test prep; libraries; YouTube tutorials and how-to culture; apprenticeships and on-the-job training; adult classes (community colleges; extension services such as OSU Extension and UC Cooperative Extension; 4-H); English classes for immigrants; children learning by joining adult work (the farm, the family business, the market stall); language nests and immersion for Indigenous languages, as the nations run them.
- **Life course:** talking and reading to young children (the National Survey of Children's Health: the share read to every day); preschool (ACS enrollment; California's transitional kindergarten for all four-year-olds; Mexico's push to put every child in preschool, from 2002 *check*; B.C.'s $10-a-day child care *check*); the first day of school; kindergarten readiness; dropping out and the GED; apprenticeship; adult learners; elders as learners and as teachers.
- **Ritual:** the first day of school and *el regreso a clases*; graduations at every level, from kindergarten up (how common *check*); spelling bees; Teacher Appreciation Week; Mexico's Día del Maestro (May 15) and Día del Niño (April 30); culture and language camps, as the nations share them; catechism and first-communion classes.
- **Institutions:** school districts and elected school boards (local control); school funding and the gaps between rich and poor districts; charter, private and religious schools; homeschooling networks and co-ops; bilingual and dual-language programs; Mexico's SEP, its free textbooks (since 1959) and their controversies, *telesecundaria* (secondary school by television, since 1968) and CONAFE's community instructors for small villages; Indigenous-controlled schools (the First Nations Education Steering Committee in B.C.; tribal schools; Mexico's intercultural universities); teachers' unions (CTA, BCTF; SNTE and CNTE in Mexico); libraries and museums; 4-H and Scouts; union apprenticeship programs; online learning platforms.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** grades, honor rolls, scholarships, valedictorians; the "nerd" stigma; the "acting white" accusation and the research on it (Fordham and Ogbu, 1986; Fryer, 2006); education as repayment of immigrant parents' sacrifice; school discipline (suspensions; corporal punishment, and when schools gave it up *check*); the shame of illiteracy.
- **Expression:** proverbs (*más sabe el diablo por viejo que por diablo*; "you learn something new every day"); *Stand and Deliver* (1988), on Jaime Escalante's calculus class at Garfield High in East Los Angeles; school films; children's television (*Sesame Street* and *Plaza Sésamo* *check*; *Bill Nye the Science Guy*, made in Seattle); Cantinflas as a village teacher in *El profe* (1971 *check*); teaching stories, as the nations share them.
- **Exemplars:** teachers (Escalante; Sal Castro, the teacher behind the 1968 East Los Angeles walkouts; José Vasconcelos; rural teachers of the 1920s and 1930s; fluent elders who teach their languages); the self-taught; the dropout who succeeded (a Silicon Valley story to test, not assume); anti-exemplars: the know-nothing, the cruel teacher.

**Key questions:** Is curiosity a virtue, a vice or a trespass? About what, and about whom, may one ask?

## 5. Connection

## 6. Duality

**Working definition** (proposed; see the header): how a culture lives with two-ness: holding two identities, languages, loyalties or views at once; dividing the world into pairs; and how it treats people who show two faces.
**Facets:** double identity and biculturalism (*ni de aquí ni de allá*); double consciousness (Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903); paired worldviews (body and soul, good and evil, sacred and profane, nature and culture), and whether the pairs are opposed or complementary; tolerating contradiction and mixed feelings; double lives and two faces (hypocrisy; *doble moral*); dual roles (the work self and the home self); dual loyalty and belonging to two countries; the border as a pair (twin cities); twins (see siblings).
**Neighbors and opposites:** ambivalence, contradiction, paradox, hybridity, *mestizaje*, two-facedness, hypocrisy; unity, wholeness, integrity (in its root sense of being one), consistency. **Excess:** two-facedness, duplicity, a split self. **Deficiency:** rigidity; black-and-white thinking; being unable to hold two views or two belongings.

- **Words:** "two-faced", "double life", "double standard", "both-and", "mixed feelings", "on the fence", "Jekyll and Hyde", "split personality" (a popular misuse), "hyphenated American", "code-switching", "talking out of both sides of one's mouth", "flip-flopper"; "Two-Spirit", a pan-Indigenous English term adopted in 1990 at a gathering in Winnipeg (whether its "two" means duality is for Two-Spirit people and each nation to say, not outsiders); Spanish *dualidad*, *doble cara*, *de dos caras*, *doble moral* (double standard; common in Mexican public debate), *hipócrita*, *ni de aquí ni de allá*, *mestizaje*, *nepantla* (Nahuatl for "in the middle", recorded by the friar Diego Durán in the 1500s and taken up by Anzaldúa), *la máscara* (Paz, "Máscaras mexicanas", in *El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950), *agridulce*, *cuates* (twins).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is two-ness a richness (biculturalism as an advantage; "two-eyed seeing"; Anzaldúa's *mestiza* consciousness) or a betrayal (*vendido*, *malinchista*; "no one can serve two masters", Matthew 6:24)? Opposed pairs (good against evil) against complementary pairs (Nahua thought on Ometeotl, the dual principle, as León-Portilla described it in *La filosofía náhuatl*, 1956; yin and yang in Chinese-American communities and in the Bay Area counterculture, as with Alan Watts in Sausalito); consistency as a virtue against tolerance of contradiction (Peng and Nisbett, 1999, on dialectical thinking); Christian body and soul; the mask as protection (Paz).
- **Feeling and the body:** mixed emotions, and whether people feel two at once (cross-cultural studies such as Miyamoto, Uchida and Ellsworth, 2010 *check*); bicultural stress; feeling "torn"; the face kept for others against the feeling behind it; dressing for two worlds (work clothes and regalia).
- **Everyday practice:** switching languages and registers between home and work; a work self and a home self; children living between two homes after divorce; dual citizens with two passports; daily border crossers; snowbirds who spend half the year in Baja California Sur (Los Cabos, La Paz, Todos Santos); second jobs and side hustles; pen names, online alter egos and second accounts ("finstas"); the masked *luchador*'s public second self.
- **Life course:** bicultural childhoods; losing and reclaiming a heritage language; adolescent identity splits; children of divorce; adults reconnecting with an Indigenous identity, as Sixties Scoop survivors and adoptees describe it; elders returning to the home country.
- **Ritual:** border gatherings (Friendship Park, *Parque de la Amistad*, at Tijuana and San Diego, where families met through the fence); Día de Muertos (the living and the dead side by side); carnival as the world turned upside down (Ensenada; La Paz, *check* its age); weddings that join two families; Halloween costumes as a night's second self; masked dances, as their communities present them.
- **Institutions:** dual-language schools and California's Seal of Biliteracy (2011, the first state seal); multiracial reporting in censuses (the U.S. since 2000; INEGI's Afro-Mexican self-identification, 2015 and 2020); hometown associations and consulates that keep migrants tied to two countries (see connection); cross-border art, music and community projects in the twin cities of the border; the Border Industrialization Program (1965) and its "twin plants"; lucha libre and the mask as a guarded second identity; Two-Spirit societies and gatherings, as their members describe them; workplaces that ask for one self at work and allow another at home.

- **Expression:** Anzaldúa, *Borderlands/La Frontera* (1987); Paz on masks; John Okada, *No-No Boy* (1957, Seattle); Le Guin, *The Left Hand of Darkness* (1969); Los Tigres del Norte's "Jaula de oro" (1980s *check*), on a migrant caught between countries; the film *Ni de aquí ni de allá* (La India María, 1988 *check*); Zorro (Johnston McCulley, 1919), the Californio gentleman with a masked second self; *Twin Peaks* (1990, set in Washington), full of doubles; lucha libre's masks; border art; Two-Spirit writing, as its authors share it.

**Key questions:** Is holding two identities or two views a richness, a weakness or a betrayal? Does the culture see the world in opposed pairs or in complementary ones?

## 7. Siblings

**Key questions:** What do brothers and sisters owe each other, and do birth order and gender set the debt? Who besides blood kin is called brother or sister?

## 8. Mediation

**Working definition:** a third party helping two or more sides settle a conflict or reach an agreement without imposing a decision; and, more broadly, the go-between who stands between parties (brokers, interpreters, intercessors).
**Facets:** formal mediation (labor, family, community, commerce); informal go-betweens (elders, clergy, *compadres*, friends, a sibling); intercession (saints, the Virgin, clergy between people and God); peacemaking and restorative circles; cultural brokers and interpreters; paid brokers and fixers; mediation between nations; online dispute resolution; children caught in the middle.
**Neighbors and opposites:** arbitration, conciliation, negotiation, reconciliation, intercession, brokering, peacemaking, diplomacy (Libra); feud, taking sides, escalation, imposed judgment. **Excess:** meddling; "triangulation" (a family-therapy term); brokers who profit from both sides; collusion. **Deficiency:** no one to go between; conflicts that escalate or are buried.

- **Words:** "mediate", "mediator", "go-between", "middleman", "honest broker", "peacemaker", "referee", "intervene", "stay out of it", "caught in the middle", "not my circus"; Spanish *mediación*, *mediador*, *conciliación*, *conciliador*, *intermediario*, *intercesión*, *amigable componedor* (a "friendly arbiter" whom both sides trust), *meterse* and *no te metas* (don't get involved), *hacer las paces*, *gestor* and *coyote* (in Mexico, a fixer who moves paperwork through offices for a fee; *coyote* is also a smuggler of migrants), *celestina* (a go-between in love, after *La Celestina*, 1499), *padrino* (sponsor and protector).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is a third party welcome or meddling? "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9); Catholic intercession by the saints and the Virgin of Guadalupe, against the Protestant teaching of one mediator between God and people (1 Timothy 2:5): a contrast between founding faiths worth tracing; Quaker and Mennonite peacemaking (the American Friends Service Committee; the Mennonite Central Committee); Indigenous peacemaking and circle processes, as the nations describe them; neutrality as a virtue against solidarity ("there are no neutrals"); harmony against open confrontation as ideals.
- **Feeling and the body:** a calm presence and voice; seating (round tables; circles); the strain of being in the middle (children between divorcing parents); mediators' emotional labor; the handshake and the *abrazo* that seal a settlement.
- **Everyday practice:** who steps in when neighbors quarrel; parents settling children's fights; older siblings and grandparents as go-betweens; friends patching up couples; HR at work; landlord-tenant mediation; community mediation centers; priests, pastors and elders in family disputes; go-betweens in courtship (the *pedida de mano*, where a respected person asks for the bride: *check* how regional); interpreters in clinics; children brokering between parents and institutions; labor contractors between farmworkers and growers.
- **Life course:** school peer-mediation and conflict-resolution programs (San Francisco's Community Boards and its school "conflict managers" *check*); "work it out yourselves" against "tell a teacher"; who helps divorcing parents agree about the children (family mediators, counselors, clergy, kin); elder mediation; family disputes over wills.
- **Ritual:** the Catholic sacrament of reconciliation (the priest between the penitent and God); ceremonies that seal a peace or a settlement; truth-and-reconciliation events; peacemaking and healing circles, as the nations share them; feasts where settlements are witnessed, as the Northwest Coast nations describe them; the public handshake.
- **Institutions:** the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service (1947) and the National Mediation Board (1934), which send mediators into labor disputes; Mexico's federal labor conciliation centers (the Centro Federal de Conciliación y Registro Laboral); community mediation centers (San Francisco Community Boards, 1976); ombudspersons; restorative practices in schools and communities; peacemaking and circle practices kept by the nations, as they describe them; churches and clergy as go-betweens, and the Catholic Church's mediation in Chiapas (Bishop Samuel Ruiz and the CONAI, 1994-1998); HR offices and employee-assistance programs at work; marriage and family counselors; Canada's self-image as an "honest broker" abroad; professional bodies (Mediate BC; the Association for Conflict Resolution).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** the Nobel Peace Prize (Lester Pearson, 1957, for the Suez peacekeeping force); praise for peacemakers; mediators blamed by both sides; "meddler" and *metiche*; "snitch" (when the go-between carries word to authority); distrust of middlemen (*coyotes*; labor contractors); La Malinche, condemned and reclaimed as the go-between.
- **Expression:** proverbs (*el que se mete a redentor sale crucificado*: whoever plays redeemer ends up crucified; *entre marido y mujer nadie se debe meter*; "it takes two to tango"); *La Celestina* and Cyrano as go-betweens in love; television shows where a stern arbiter settles quarrels (*Judge Judy*, made in Los Angeles; *Caso cerrado*, in Spanish), as a contrast with true mediation; negotiator films; stories of peacemakers, as the nations share them.
- **Exemplars:** peacemakers (Pearson; Bishop Samuel Ruiz); the makers of the 1992 Watts gang truce; interpreters at the old treaty councils; *comadres* and elders known for settling quarrels; anti-exemplars: the meddler, the double-dealing middleman.
- **History:** treaty councils as mediated events, and the dispute over what was understood when the 1854-1855 Stevens treaties were explained in Chinuk Wawa, a trade language of a few hundred words; fur-trade marriages as alliances between companies and nations; labor conflict and its settlement (the 1934 West Coast waterfront strike, ended by arbitration; the Delano grape strike, 1965-1970); the Japanese American Citizens League as a go-between during internment (contested); the 1994 Zapatista uprising and its mediation.
- **Measures:** FMCS case counts (*check* for regional detail); Mexico's labor conciliation statistics (CFCRL *check*); community mediation centers' caseloads (the National Association for Community Mediation *check*); studies of conflict styles by culture (Ting-Toomey's face-negotiation research; the Thomas-Kilmann instrument); Hofstede's power distance (whether the go-between must outrank the parties).
- **Variation:** religion (Catholic intercession against Protestant directness); class (who has a respected go-between to call on, and who must pay a professional); gender (women as kin-keepers and peacemakers; whether mediation disadvantages women); each Indigenous nation's own practice; immigrant children as brokers; rural and urban (priests and elders against professionals).
- **Relations:** communication, connection, siblings (a sibling as go-between) and duality (standing between two) (this sign); diplomacy, justice, balance and peace at any price (Libra); trust (Scorpio); authority (Capricorn: imposing against facilitating).
- **Who it applies to:** between people and God or the spirit world (saints, priests, and spiritual leaders as each tradition describes them); between nations and states (peace envoys; third countries as go-betweens); between companies and customers (ombudspersons; marketplaces' resolution centers); between generations (grandparents between parents and children); machines as mediators (present-day attitudes to algorithmic dispute resolution and AI-assisted negotiation *check*).
- **Silences:** power imbalances hidden by "neutral" mediation (the stronger side setting the terms); domestic violence and mediation; disputes families never let outsiders into; *no te metas*.
- **Change:** online dispute resolution; mediation spreading into schools, workplaces and families; restorative practices; AI tools for mediation.

**Key questions:** When two sides clash, does the culture send for a third party, an authority who decides, or no one? Must the go-between be neutral, or a respected person with ties to both sides?

## 9. Gossip

**Working definition:** talk about absent people, especially their private lives and conduct; rumor (unconfirmed reports passed from person to person) is researched here too.
**Facets:** everyday gossip (bonding, entertainment, news of the community); gossip as social control (enforcing norms); malicious gossip, slander and character assassination; rumor and urban legend; rumor in crises (war, riots, disasters); celebrity gossip and tabloids; workplace gossip; online gossip (call-outs, anonymous apps, review sites); "whisper networks" that warn of dangerous people; *el qué dirán*.
**Neighbors and opposites:** rumor, hearsay, scuttlebutt, chatter, scandal, slander, word of mouth, "tea"; discretion, confidentiality, minding one's own business. **Excess:** slander, harassment, mobbing, ruined reputations. **Deficiency:** being cut off from what everyone knows; a silence that protects wrongdoers.

- **Words:** "gossip" (from Old English *godsibb*, a godparent, then a woman's friends at a birth, then idle talk), "rumor", "hearsay", "scuttlebutt", "the grapevine", "dish", "spill the tea" and "tea" (from Black drag culture), "the rumor mill", "busybody", "gossipmonger", "talk of the town", "word on the street", "shoot the breeze" (how men may name their own gossip); Spanish *chisme*, *chismoso*, *chismear*, *comadrear* and *comadreo* (to gossip, from *comadre*: the same path from co-parent to gossip as in English), *mitote* and *mitotero* (Mexican, from a Nahuatl word for a dance: a ruckus, gossip), *argüende* and *argüendero* (Mexican: fuss and gossip), *radio pasillo* (the office grapevine *check*), *soltar un borrego* (Mexican: to start a false rumor *check*), *corre la bola* (word is going around *check*), *el qué dirán* (what people will say: fear of gossip as a social force), *lengua larga*, *lengua de víbora*, *dimes y diretes*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is gossip a sin (the Catholic sins of detraction and calumny; Jewish teaching on *lashon hara*; Proverbs on the whisperer who separates friends), a weakness (the stereotype that women gossip, to be tested, not adopted), the weapon of the weak (Scott, *Weapons of the Weak*, 1985, a Malaysian study, as a model), or the glue and police of community life (Gluckman, "Gossip and Scandal", 1963; Dunbar, *Grooming, Gossip and the Evolution of Language*, 1996)? "If you can't say anything nice..."; *el qué dirán* as a force that keeps people in line; teachings on speaking of others, as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** the pleasure of a secret shared; lowered voices, leaning in, looking around; the shame and anxiety of being talked about; "my ears are burning"; blushing; the cold shoulder that follows a rumor.
- **Everyday practice:** talk at the hair salon, barbershop, laundromat, church steps, market (*tianguis*), office kitchen, and the shared washing place of the *vecindad*; group chats; Nextdoor and neighborhood Facebook groups; anonymous apps (Yik Yak) and city-by-city "Are We Dating the Same Guy?" groups (*check*); review sites (Yelp, San Francisco, 2004); celebrity media (TMZ, Los Angeles, 2005; Mexico's *TVNotas*); gossip television (*Ventaneando*, TV Azteca, since 1996; *El Gordo y la Flaca*, Univision *check*); Hollywood's gossip columns.
- **Life course:** teaching children not to tattle, and not to gossip; "snitches get stitches"; adolescent gossip and relational aggression; cyberbullying; workplace gossip; gossip in old age (senior centers; retirement communities); "speak no ill of the dead" at funerals.
- **Ritual:** red carpets and award shows as gossip spectacles; the gathering after Sunday mass; the *velorio* and the *novenario* (the wake and the nine nights of prayer, where talk happens *check*); baby and bridal showers; the *tamalada* (making tamales together); roasts (licensed public mockery); Northwest Coast feasts where claims are made and witnessed in public, as the nations describe them (the opposite of gossip: public validation).
- **Institutions:** the gossip press and celebrity media (supermarket tabloids; *TVNotas*; TMZ); entertainment television's gossip shows; school anti-bullying programs and counselors; workplace "no gossip" policies and HR complaint channels; churches' preaching against gossip and slander; platform moderation and community guidelines, and the volunteer moderators of neighborhood groups; review and rating systems (Yelp; Google reviews).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** being "in the know" as status; the good storyteller as entertainer; "gossip" as a label that shuns; reputations ruined by rumor; call-outs and "cancellation"; reputational sanctions that fall harder on women (slut-shaming); the "no snitching" code of the street and of gangs; *el qué dirán* as the sanction on anyone who strays.
- **Expression:** proverbs (*pueblo chico, infierno grande*: small town, big hell; *el pez por la boca muere*; "where there's smoke, there's fire"; "loose lips sink ships", a wartime poster); Juan Rulfo, *Pedro Páramo* (1955), a town told through the murmurs of its dead; García Márquez, *Crónica de una muerte anunciada* (1981); *Peyton Place*; *Twin Peaks*; *telenovela* gossips; "I Heard It Through the Grapevine" (1967); the nosy neighbor of sitcoms (Gladys Kravitz in *Bewitched*); memes; warnings against loose talk in the stories the nations share.
- **Exemplars:** gossip columnists (Hedda Hopper and Louella Parsons in Hollywood); TV gossip hosts (Pati Chapoy of *Ventaneando*); whistleblowers set against gossips; victims of rumor (Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle, 1921, contested); anti-exemplars: the town busybody, the *chismosa*, the liar who starts false rumors.
- **History:** rumor as a cause of harm (wartime rumors of sabotage by Japanese Americans that fed the internment; press rumors before the 1943 Zoot Suit Riots in Los Angeles); rumor research (Allport and Postman, *The Psychology of Rumor*, 1947; Tamotsu Shibutani, *Improvised News*, 1966, which drew partly on his own internment *check*); the Hollywood studio system's gossip machine; Mexico's *nota roja* and *¡Alarma!* (from 1963); small-town gossip in mining and logging towns; rumor in disasters (the 1906 earthquake; wildfires; COVID-19).
- **Measures:** how much people gossip in daily life (a 2020 UC Riverside study that recorded everyday talk, Robbins and colleagues *check*); gossip and cooperation in experiments (Feinberg, Willer and Schultz, 2014, "Gossip and Ostracism Promote Cooperation in Groups"); cyberbullying (CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey, by state; INEGI's MOCIBA module on cyber-harassment, by state; Statistics Canada's surveys *check*); celebrity-media audiences (Nielsen); Google Trends for celebrity news by state; tightness and looseness by state (Harrington and Gelfand, 2014), as a measure of how hard norms are enforced; Pew on how often people see false information online.
- **Variation:** gender (whether women gossip more, or only differently: a classic claim to test); age; class; small towns against cities (*pueblo chico*); tight immigrant communities; workplaces; religious communities; each Indigenous community; online and offline.
- **Relations:** communication, information, curiosity and connection (this sign: gossip bonds as much as it harms); secrecy, betrayal and trust (Scorpio); reputation and honor (Leo, Capricorn); humiliation (Leo); justice (Libra: gossip as informal justice); crime (Pisces: slander and harassment as wrongs); privacy.
- **Who it applies to:** celebrities, politicians and public figures; businesses (reviews); institutions and governments (rumor and conspiracy theory); the dead ("speak no ill of the dead"); animals (gossip as a stand-in for grooming, in Dunbar's theory); machines (present-day attitudes to AI that states false things about real people *check*).
- **Silences:** who may not be gossiped about; topics too dangerous to talk about (where fear of cartels or officials silences talk, and journalists are killed); the gossip about harassers that never reaches a complaint; men's gossip, called something else.
- **Change:** group chats and social media; anonymous apps; review culture; "cancel culture"; AI-generated rumors and deepfakes; whisper networks after #MeToo; the decline of print tabloids; TikTok "storytime".

**Key questions:** Is gossip a sin, the weapon of the weak, or the glue and police of community life? Who fears *el qué dirán*, and who is free of it?
