# Avenues of investigation: 05 Leo (creativity and self-expression)

**What this file is.** For each Leo concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Leo concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** (*check*): a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. Indigenous-language terms are not supplied here. They come from the nations' own dictionaries and language programs (FirstVoices, run by the First Peoples' Cultural Council in British Columbia; the *Lushootseed Dictionary*, 1994; the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde's Chinuk Wawa dictionary, 2012). The exceptions are a few Chinuk Wawa words long since taken into regional English.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; it is settled with the author just before Leo's pass): creativity, self-expression, pride, recognition, being seen, dignity, humiliation, generosity, leadership, play, and the author's two named examples, honor and joy (placed under Leo by the author, 2026-11-02). Twelve entries.

**Questions to settle with the list:**
- **Recognition** is also in Capricorn's ten; its proposed home is Leo, and Capricorn keeps a cross-reference. It heads a near-synonym group: approval, praise, validation, acknowledgment, appreciation. This file researches all of them under recognition, together with the recognition of standing (titles, honors, credentials). The author may prefer to split esteem (praise for achievement) from standing (recognition of status).
- **Humiliation** is in a near-synonym group with ridicule, and the Leo notes folded shame into it. This file researches ridicule and embarrassment under humiliation, and shame only as the feeling humiliation produces. Shame has no home on any sign's list yet.
- **Pride, dignity and honor** overlap: one field of worth seen three ways (the feeling and the stance; the worth every person has; the worth held in others' eyes). They are kept apart because cultures split them differently (Leung and Cohen's dignity, honor and face cultures, 2011). Face, the fourth corner, has no entry.
- **Recognition, being seen and self-expression** overlap: being seen is perception and witness, recognition is valuation, self-expression is the act of showing. Kept apart here; they can be merged if the author prefers.
- **Play and joy** overlap (an activity and an emotion). **Leadership** borders authority (Capricorn); this file keeps leadership to the following that people give without formal command.

## 0. The domain: creativity and self-expression

**Traditional areas:** creativity, play, pleasure, romance, children. **The domain question:** how do people shine, play and take pleasure?

- **How much shining is wanted.** Whether the culture rewards standing out or blending in: survival against self-expression values (Inglehart and Welzel; the World Values Survey's cultural map); indulgence against restraint (Hofstede); tightness and looseness by state (Harrington and Gelfand, 2014); ideal affect, the excited or the calm good feeling a culture most wants (Tsai, 2007); "let your light so shine" (Matthew 5:16) against "the nail that sticks out"; the tall poppy (Aries).
- **How the culture groups the domain.** Whether its words split or fuse the Leo concepts: English *play* covers games, music and theater, where Spanish splits *jugar*, *tocar* and *actuar*; Spanish separates *honor* (standing) from *honra* (virtue and good name); Mexican *pena* spans shame, embarrassment and shyness; *orgullo* is praise and blame at once. Which of the twelve the culture treats as central, which as marginal, and which it has no word for.
- **Pleasure.** Whether pleasure is a good, a reward for work, or a danger: Puritan suspicion of festivity; the fiesta as release (Paz, *El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950, the chapter on the fiesta, a classic claim to test); Sunday rest and Sunday closing; how people see cannabis and psychedelics, as pleasure, medicine or vice (Gallup's polling on marijuana use *check*); vice tourism (Prohibition-era Tijuana and its Agua Caliente resort; Reno and Las Vegas; spring break in Cabo San Lucas); vacation, how much of it people take, and whether leaving days unused is a badge of devotion (*check* surveys); the World Values Survey item on how important leisure is; leisure time in the American Time Use Survey (ATUS). Food pleasure belongs to Taurus, sex to Scorpio, escape to Pisces.
- **Romance.** Courtship and dating scripts; who asks whom and who pays; romantic love as the reason to marry (Jankowiak and Fischer, 1992, found romantic love in 147 of 166 cultures sampled); the *serenata* and *llevar gallo* (*check*); *noviazgo* and *pedir la mano*; Valentine's Day and Mexico's *Día del Amor y la Amistad*, which includes friends; love songs (the *bolero*, the *ranchera*, the power ballad); romance novels and *telenovelas*; dating apps (Tinder, founded in Los Angeles, 2012) and Pew's surveys of online dating (*check*). Marriage belongs to Libra, sex to Scorpio.
- **Children as joy and display.** Whether children are a blessing, a cost, a project or a status display: the shift from the useful to the "priceless" child (Zelizer, *Pricing the Priceless Child*, 1985); baby showers and gender-reveal parties (one started California's El Dorado Fire, 2020); children's birthday parties and *piñatas*; "proud parent of an honor student" bumper stickers; whether children are welcome in restaurants, churches and workplaces; childlessness by choice (Pew, 2024 *check*); birth rates (CDC's National Vital Statistics System; Statistics Canada; INEGI). Child-rearing ways are Cancer's (runner-up here).
- **Fame and celebrity.** Hollywood and the influencer; what children say they want to be (a 2019 Harris Poll for LEGO found more U.S. and U.K. children wanting to be YouTubers than astronauts *check*); fame rising to the top value in tween television by 2007 (Uhls and Greenfield, 2011); fan cultures; local celebrity (the high-school star, the *charro* champion, the radio host); the Hollywood Walk of Fame.
- **Celebration and display.** Turning moments into occasions: what weddings, *quinceañeras* and graduations cost (The Knot's annual wedding survey *check*); sponsors (*padrinos*) and *mayordomías* who pay for fiestas; fireworks; house decorations for Christmas and Halloween; luxury and conspicuous display (Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899); lowriders and the car as display.

## 1. Creativity

**Working definition:** making something new that others find valuable or fitting, in the arts, in crafts, in solving problems and in everyday life.
**Facets:** artistic creation; craft and skill; invention and innovation; everyday improvising (making do); creativity in speech (wit, wordplay); variation within a shared tradition against individual originality; who owns what is made.
**Neighbors and opposites:** originality, imagination, ingenuity, invention, craftsmanship; imitation, copying, convention, routine. **Excess:** novelty for its own sake, "disruption", eccentricity without craft. **Deficiency:** rigidity, conformity, sterility.

- **Words:** "creative", "artsy", "original", "genius", "gifted", "innovative", "disruptive", "maker", "tinkerer", "hack", "think outside the box", "creatives" (as a noun), "sellout", "derivative"; Spanish *creatividad*, *ingenio* and *el ingenio mexicano* (a popular phrase for clever improvised fixes), *hechizo* (Mexican: homemade, improvised), *chispa*, *inventiva*, *maña*, *artesanía* and *artesano*, *oficio*, *arte popular*, *rasquachismo* (the Chicano aesthetic of making do with scraps, named by Tomás Ybarra-Frausto, 1989); whether each Indigenous language has a separate word for "art" at all, or only words for making, a question for its own dictionary.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is creativity a gift (from God, the muse, the ancestors), an inborn trait, or a skill won by practice? Novelty against faithful variation within a tradition (Lubart, 1999, on Western and Eastern ideas of creativity); the Romantic genius and the tortured artist; whether art is self-expression, service to the community, prayer, or a house's inherited right (crests and designs owned by houses on the Northwest Coast, as the nations describe them); craft against fine art; whether art is real work ("get a real job"); Silicon Valley's creed of disruption.
- **Feeling and the body:** inspiration and flow (Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow*, 1990, and *Creativity*, 1996); the maker's hands; creative block; the "mad genius" belief and the research on creativity and mental illness; creativity and drugs (the psychedelic Bay Area of the 1960s).
- **Everyday practice:** hobbies and crafts (quilting, woodworking, knitting, sewing); cooking as invention; home decoration and gardens; customizing cars (lowriders, hot rods); costumes; do-it-yourself and improvised fixes (*hechizo*); music at home; community theater; open mics; posting made content online; garage startups.
- **Life course:** art and music in school, and whether they are the first classes cut; children's drawings on the refrigerator; arts camps; science fairs; whether parents push toward or away from creative careers; late-life creativity; elders as culture bearers and master artists, as the nations name them.
- **Ritual:** arts festivals and fairs (Burning Man; Maker Faire, begun in San Mateo, 2006; Seattle's Bumbershoot; the Vancouver Mural Festival); craft markets; *ofrendas* for Día de Muertos and their contests; Rose Parade floats; the raising of totem poles and the dedication of new carved works, as the nations share them.
- **Institutions:** arts councils (the National Endowment for the Arts; the California Arts Council; the Canada Council for the Arts; the BC Arts Council; Mexico's Secretaría de Cultura and its grants for creators, formerly FONCA); art schools (CalArts; Emily Carr University); museums and galleries; the film, music and game industries (Hollywood; game studios around Seattle and Vancouver); writers' and actors' guilds (the Writers Guild of America; SAG-AFTRA); maker spaces and community art centers; the nations' own protocols on designs and crests, and their cultural centers; the disputes between Indigenous weavers in Mexico and fashion brands over copied textile designs.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** prizes (the Oscars, the Grammys and Latin Grammys, Mexico's Ariel awards and national arts prizes); grants and fellowships (the MacArthur "genius" grants); sales and fame; sanctions on copying (plagiarism scandals; charges of cultural appropriation); ridicule of the pretentious artist; books pulled from school and library shelves; self-censorship by publishers and studios.
- **Expression:** novels, films and songs about artists and inventors; murals as public creation (the Chicano mural movement; Chicano Park, San Diego, 1970; the Great Wall of Los Angeles, begun by Judith Baca in 1976); Northwest Coast formline art, in the artists' own accounts and in outsiders' analyses (Bill Holm, 1965); Seri ironwood carving and basketry; Paipai pottery; Kumeyaay and Pomo basketry.
- **Exemplars:** Bill Reid and Robert Davidson; Emily Carr; Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera as national icons; Judith Baca; Juan Quezada and the potters of Mata Ortiz; Walt Disney; Steve Jobs and the garage founders; anti-exemplars: the plagiarist, the hack, the sellout.
- **History:** Mexican muralism after the Revolution; Hollywood from the 1910s; the 1960s San Francisco counterculture; the Chicano art movement; Silicon Valley; Seattle grunge; the suppression of the potlatch (1884-1951), which also struck at art made for feasts, and the revival of Northwest Coast art from the 1960s.
- **Measures:** the Arts and Cultural Production Satellite Account (BEA and NEA; by state); the NEA's Survey of Public Participation in the Arts (with the Census Bureau; state estimates *check*); artists in the workforce (ACS occupation data; NEA reports); Statistics Canada's Culture Satellite Account and census counts of artists (Hill Strategies' analyses by province and city); INEGI's satellite account for culture and its module on cultural events (MODECULT *check*); patents per capita, as a rough count of invention (USPTO by county); creative-class shares by metro (Florida, *The Rise of the Creative Class*, 2002, and later); trends in creative-thinking test scores (Kim, 2011 *check*); openness to experience by state (Rentfrow et al., 2013).
- **Variation:** class (fine art against folk art and craft); urban arts districts and rural crafts; gender (who is called brilliant: Leslie et al., 2015, on beliefs about brilliance across fields); ethnicity and the gatekeepers of art worlds; immigrant artists; each nation's own art traditions.
- **Relations:** self-expression (creativity's outlet); play (its practice ground); recognition (credit and authorship); work and craft (Virgo); ownership of works (Taurus); curiosity (Gemini); rebirth (Scorpio).
- **Who it applies to:** children ("every child is an artist"); animals (painting elephants, bowerbirds); communities and nations as collective makers; corporations ("innovation"); machines: present-day attitudes to AI-made art and authorship (whether people count a machine as an author; the 2023 Hollywood writers' and actors' strikes, which bargained over AI; polls on AI art *check*).
- **Silences:** the assistants, ghostwriters and craftspeople behind famous names; art taken from nations and held in museums, and its return (the U'mista Cultural Centre's return of confiscated potlatch regalia); creative failure and poverty.
- **Change:** generative AI; the creator economy; arts funding cut and restored; art and regalia returned to the nations; growing markets for Indigenous art; film production moving between Los Angeles, Vancouver and elsewhere.
- **The senses:** the sounds of making (the loom, the chisel and adze, the potter's wheel, the radio in the workshop); the smells of studios and shops (oil paint, sawdust, kiln smoke, cedar shavings, hot solder); the hand and touch: handmade against machine-made, and what people say they feel in a handmade thing (clay, wool, carved wood); taste as a field of invention: new dishes and the cooks who make them (California cuisine; the Caesar salad, credited to a Tijuana restaurant of the 1920s *check*; Baja's fusion cooking); improvisation in music, where making and hearing happen at once; the senses a craft trains (the weaver's fingers, the carver's eye, the cook's tongue); the colors a place makes things in (Mexican pinks and blues; Northwest Coast red and black, as the artists describe their palette).
- **Space, place and things:** studios, workshops, garages and sheds (the Palo Alto garage where Hewlett-Packard began in 1939, and the founding garage as a legend); artists' lofts and arts districts, and the rents that drive artists out of them; public art and murals, and where a city allows them; museums and galleries, and who walks in; craft markets and tourist art; maker spaces; the kitchen as a studio; the refrigerator door as a family gallery; tools and their care, and tools handed down; sketchbooks and notebooks; the materials a place offers (cedar, clay, ironwood, wool, shell), as the makers share them; walls set aside for graffiti, and the rest of the city's surfaces; the "creative city" that planners and boosters try to build.
- **Time and rhythm:** the seasons of making (carving and weaving in winter; floats, costumes and *ofrendas* begun months before a festival); deadlines and the all-nighter; the long apprenticeship, and the popular "10,000 hours" claim (from Ericsson's research, spread by Gladwell, *Outliers*, 2008); child prodigies against late bloomers, and research on when in life creative work peaks (Simonton); the slowness of handwork against fast fashion and mass production; openings, premieres and launch days; old crafts revived after a gap of decades.
- **The economy of it:** whether art pays: the "starving artist", day jobs and second jobs; grants, commissions and patrons; the market for handmade goods and tourist art, and the price gap between "art" and "craft"; auction prices; gig work for illustrators, musicians and designers; work offered for "exposure" instead of pay; crowdfunding and fan subscriptions; who earns from a people's designs, and the nations' own answers (see Taurus C37, owning the intangible); the "creative class" and the housing costs it is blamed for; AI tools and what they do to the pay of illustrators, writers and voice actors.
- **Conflict and crisis:** quarrels over copying and credit, settled by reputation, apology and ridicule (plagiarism scandals in music and publishing); fights over murals (Siqueiros's *América Tropical*, painted over soon after it was finished in Los Angeles in 1932 and restored decades later); purists against innovators within a tradition (in mariachi, in Northwest Coast art, in regional cooking); studios, archives and collections lost to fire and flood; art made in crisis (protest art; art made in the pandemic lockdowns; songs written after disasters); artists who fled war and repression and remade their work in exile.
- **Margins and extremes:** the "mad genius" and the tortured artist, as belief and as research topic; self-taught and visionary builders (Simon Rodia's Watts Towers, built by one man in Los Angeles between 1921 and 1954); makers who cannot stop (endless projects, garages full of half-built things); graffiti writers and street artists, admired by some and painted over by others; performance art meant to shock; founders who burn out chasing the next invention; art made in captivity and confinement (the crafts of Japanese American internment camps); at the other edge, people who say they "can't draw a straight line" and never make anything.

## 2. Self-expression

**Working definition:** showing one's inner self (thoughts, feelings, tastes, beliefs, identity) outwardly, in speech, appearance, art and action.
**Facets:** speaking one's mind; showing emotion; dress and body as expression; expression through art and things owned; expressing identity (gender, ethnicity, faith); political expression; restraint and masks.
**Neighbors and opposites:** authenticity, candor, voice, individuality, freedom of expression; reserve, restraint, conformity, self-censorship, the mask. **Excess:** oversharing, exhibitionism, self-indulgence. **Deficiency:** repression, a false self, silence.

- **Words:** "be yourself", "express yourself", "speak your truth", "authentic", "let it all hang out", "let your freak flag fly", "Keep Portland Weird" (after "Keep Austin Weird"), "TMI", "oversharing", "repressed", "uptight", "buttoned-up"; Spanish *expresarse*, *ser uno mismo*, *desahogarse* (to unburden oneself), *sin pelos en la lengua*, *decir las cosas como son*, *el qué dirán* (what people will say, a brake on expression), *aguantarse* (holding it in), *rajarse* (Paz's word for opening up, read as weakness; see Aries), *ser muy expresivo*, *ser reservado*, *las apariencias*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is expressing the self a need, a right, a duty or an indulgence? The "true self" of humanistic psychology (Rogers; Maslow's self-actualization) and therapy culture; the self kept behind a mask (Paz, "Máscaras mexicanas", 1950, a classic claim to test, not adopt); religious teaching on modesty, humility and the guarded tongue; whether restraint is read as dignity or as repression; whether talking helps thinking (Kim, 2002; Kim and Sherman, 2007, comparing East Asian American and European American students).
- **Feeling and the body:** how freely emotion is shown (Rychlowska et al., 2015, linked expressiveness to how many source countries a nation's population came from); display rules (Matsumoto); dress, hair, tattoos, piercings and makeup as expression; gender expression; hair rules at school and work, and the fights over natural Black hairstyles.
- **Everyday practice:** clothing and personal style; decorating rooms, cars and lockers; bumper stickers and yard signs; posting online; journaling; singing and dancing in public; karaoke; protest; how much people tell strangers (the "Seattle Freeze" as a local claim to test); venting at work and at home.
- **Life course:** teenage style and rebellion; dress codes and uniforms (uniforms in Mexican public schools; dress-code fights in U.S. schools); "use your words"; whether children may show anger, sadness or pride; coming out; the restraint expected of elders.
- **Ritual:** Pride parades (San Francisco, Seattle, Vancouver); Halloween and Día de Muertos costumes (*catrinas*); Carnival (La Paz, Ensenada); Burning Man's principle of "radical self-expression" (one of its Ten Principles, 2004); poetry slams and open mics.
- **Institutions:** Berkeley's Free Speech Movement (1964) and later fights over speech on campus; student newspapers, drama clubs and art programs; therapy and counseling, the talking cure as a profession; churches' teaching on modest dress and speech; dress and grooming codes at work; tattoo rules in the military and other uniformed jobs; school uniform policies; talk radio and call-in shows; social-media platforms and their moderation rules.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for authenticity and courage; sanctions for too much (the show-off, the drama queen, "attention-seeking") and for too little ("fake", "robotic"); the *qué dirán* and family shame; firing over posts; online pile-ons.
- **Expression:** confessional songwriting (the Laurel Canyon singer-songwriters); punk, grunge and hip hop; *corridos* that speak for their singers; Chicano poetry; memoir and the personal essay; zines; the selfie.
- **Exemplars:** self-expressive icons (Frida Kahlo; Kurt Cobain; Harvey Milk; Juan Gabriel, whose reply "lo que se ve no se pregunta" (what is seen is not asked) is itself a case of expression and silence *check*); counter-exemplars (the poseur, the conformist).
- **History:** the Beats and the hippies of San Francisco; the Chicano movement's self-naming; the *pachucos* and the zoot suit, punished in the 1943 Zoot Suit Riots; Native dress, hair and ceremony suppressed in boarding and residential schools and in the campaign against the potlatch, and their revival.
- **Measures:** self-expression values (Inglehart and Welzel; World Values Survey; country, some regions); indulgence (Hofstede); openness and extraversion by state (Rentfrow et al., 2013); tattoos (Pew, 2023 *check*); Google Trends for "authentic" and "be yourself"; attitudes to free expression (Pew; the Knight Foundation and Gallup; FIRE's campus surveys); Pride attendance (*check* sources).
- **Variation:** gender (men's emotional expression); generation; class (taste as expression: Bourdieu, *Distinction*, 1979); ethnicity and code-switching; religion; urban and rural; LGBTQ people.
- **Relations:** being seen (expression needs a witness); creativity; pride; identity (Aries); communication (Gemini); freedom (Sagittarius); nonconformity (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** children, employees, soldiers, the clergy; corporations (a brand's "voice"; companies taking public stands); animals ("expressive" pets); machines: whether people think AI "expresses" anything, or only imitates expression (present-day attitudes and debates).
- **Silences:** what families leave unsaid (*el qué dirán*); men's feelings; sexuality in some communities; politics at work and at family tables.
- **Change:** social media; "authenticity" as a marketing word; debates over gender expression; backlash against oversharing.
- **The senses:** loudness as expression: who may be loud, and where (the *grito*; music played loud from cars and porches; cheering against keeping quiet); color as a statement (bright clothes and painted houses; the drab and the plain chosen on purpose); scent as a signature (perfume, cologne, the smell of a person's home); taste in music as a declaration of self; food as self-statement (the signature dish; the hot sauce carried everywhere; the diet that announces who one is, with identity itself under Aries); touch as expressiveness (hugging and kissing in greeting against the handshake or the nod); singing and dancing in public, karaoke and the shower; silence and the flat voice as the sound of reserve.

- **Conflict and crisis:** quarrels over dress and grooming at school and work (hair, piercings, sagging pants, head coverings); clashes over flags, yard signs and murals; families split by a coming out or a change of faith; expression in a crisis: vigils, shrines, protest signs and murals after killings and disasters; disputes over drag shows and Pride events at libraries and schools; feuds that start with a post.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):

  - *Online:* selfies; profiles and bios; personal blogs and vlogs; posts about feelings; anonymous confession pages; comment sections; avatars and their customizing; hashtag campaigns; personality-quiz results shared as self-description.

**Key questions:** Is showing the inner self a need, a right, or an indulgence? Is restraint a mask that hides the self, or a form of dignity?

## 3. Pride

**Working definition:** feeling and showing that oneself, one's work or one's own people have worth, from healthy self-respect to arrogance.
**Facets:** pride in achievement (authentic pride); arrogance (hubristic pride); pride in work and craft; pride in family and children; group pride (ethnic, national, regional, school, team); pride as a movement (LGBTQ Pride; Black, Chicano and Native pride); wounded pride.
**Neighbors and opposites:** self-respect, self-esteem, confidence, dignity, honor; humility, modesty, shame. **Excess:** arrogance, vanity, hubris. **Deficiency:** shame, self-abasement, no self-respect.

- **Words:** "proud", "too proud to beg", "pride goes before a fall", "swallow your pride", "full of himself", "cocky", "stuck-up", "humblebrag", "humble", "Pride Month"; Spanish *orgullo* (praise and blame), *soberbia* (the sin), *amor propio*, *creído*, *presumido* and *presumir* (to show off), *mamón* and *sangrón* (Mexican: arrogant, stuck-up), *se cree muy salsa*, *orgullo herido*, *orgullosamente* (as in "orgullosamente sonorense"), *humilde* (which in Mexico also means poor).
- **Beliefs and standing:** pride as the root sin (Augustine; Proverbs 16:18) against Aristotle's greatness of soul; Benjamin Franklin on pride as the passion hardest to subdue (*Autobiography*); authentic against hubristic pride (Tracy and Robins, 2007); collective pride as a remedy for stigma (Pride; Chicano and Native pride); whether a person may be proud of themselves, or only of family and people; Samuel Ramos's claim of a Mexican inferiority complex (*El perfil del hombre y la cultura en México*, 1934) and the replies to it, as a debate to read.
- **Feeling and the body:** the pride display (expanded chest, head tilted back, arms raised), shown even by congenitally blind athletes (Tracy and Matsumoto, 2008); swagger and standing tall; dressing up; pride in a strong, working body.
- **Everyday practice:** bragging norms; trophies, diplomas and children's report cards on display; flags on houses and trucks; regional and team merchandise; pride in the house, the garden, the clean truck; craftsmen's marks; telling or hiding one's achievements.
- **Life course:** praising children (praise for effort against praise for ability: Mueller and Dweck, 1998); school spirit; graduation sashes and *sarape* stoles; the first paycheck; pride in grandchildren.
- **Ritual:** Pride parades and Pride Month; heritage months; national days (September 16, July 1, July 4); homecoming; *honores a la bandera*; hometown fiestas.
- **Institutions:** state and national symbols and the care given them (flag etiquette taught in schools and Scouts; the Monday flag ceremony in Mexican schools); fights over flying Pride flags at city halls and schools; school spirit programs and alumni associations; heritage associations and ethnic clubs (*check* pilot examples); corporate Pride branding.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** pride admired when "earned"; arrogance cut down (the tall poppy; *mamón*); humbling by elders, coaches, peers; ridicule of "excessive" pride; groups punished for collective pride (the Zoot Suit Riots); backlash against Pride events.
- **Expression:** pride anthems ("Say It Loud - I'm Black and I'm Proud", 1968; "México lindo y querido"); *corridos* praising towns and states; Chicano art; hubris stories (Icarus; *The Great Gatsby*); boasting songs in hip hop and *corridos*.
- **Exemplars:** figures of proud bearing (boxers; *charros*; Muhammad Ali); proud craftsmen; Pride's founders (Harvey Milk; José Sarria in San Francisco); anti-exemplars: the arrogant who fall.
- **History:** San Francisco's and Vancouver's early Pride marches; "brown pride" in the Chicano movement; Red Power; "Black is beautiful"; Mexican national pride after the Revolution (*indigenismo*, muralism); Canadian pride defined against the United States (a claim to test).
- **Measures:** national pride (World Values Survey; ISSP National Identity modules *check* years; Gallup's annual question on pride in being American; Environics Focus Canada); state pride (Gallup's 50-state poll; see Aries); self-esteem across 53 nations (Schmitt and Allik, 2005); narcissism trends among the young (Twenge and Campbell, 2009) and the rebuttals (Trzesniewski, Donnellan and Robins, 2008).
- **Variation:** gender; generation; immigrants' pride in origin against pride in the new country; minority pride movements; class (working-class pride); region.
- **Relations:** dignity and honor (overlap to settle); humiliation (pride's wound); recognition; self-worth (Taurus); identity (Aries); humility and modesty (a near-synonym pair with no home sign yet); shame.
- **Who it applies to:** individuals, families, teams, towns, nations; companies (pride in the firm); animals (pride in show animals and working dogs); machines: pride in one's tools, car or code, and whether people credit robots with pride (present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** pride that refuses help; pride in contested heritage (pioneers against dispossession); shame hidden behind pride.
- **Change:** Pride's corporate sponsorship and backlash; falling national pride in U.S. surveys (Gallup *check*); bragging online.
- **The senses:** the sounds of pride: anthems, school fight songs, chants of "we're number one", horns honked through town after a win, the *grito* on September 15; dishes served with pride as a place's own (salmon, *carne asada*, a grandmother's mole), and the boast that "ours is better"; the taste of one's own produce, catch or recipe; the feel of the uniform, the letter jacket, the medal, the new boots; colors worn with pride (team colors, the flag's colors, the rainbow); the swagger and the walk, as others hear and see them.
- **Space, place and things:** flags on houses and trucks; trophy cases, walls of diplomas and framed photographs of achievements; town-entrance signs that boast a title; murals and statues of local heroes; parade routes; the car washed and polished on Sunday; lawns, gardens and houses kept as a family's pride; the hometown landmark everyone shows to visitors; places proud of being odd or rough ("Keep Portland Weird"); craftsmen's marks and the name painted on a work truck; the family land and the farm sign giving the year it was founded.
- **Time and rhythm:** national days, heritage months, homecoming weekends, graduations and Pride Month; anniversaries of victories and of founding; pride across generations ("five generations on this land"; a family name on the same business for a century); the sports season and the playoff run; the moment of achievement against the long memory of it; how quickly pride is wounded, and how long it takes to heal.
- **The economy of it:** pride for sale: team and regional merchandise, flags, "made in" and "buy local" labels; place branding and tourism built on local pride; companies' Pride sponsorships; pride in work as part of the pay (the trades, farming, fishing), and the wounded pride of men who lose such work; what families spend so their children can do them proud (lessons, uniforms, tutors); spending on weddings and *quinceañeras* so a family can hold its head up.
- **Conflict and crisis:** wounded pride and the fights it starts (bar fights over an insult to a town or a team; riots after championships); rival prides between towns, schools and peoples; quarrels over flying Pride flags and national flags; pride in defeat and in lost causes, and the arguments over it; disasters that turn into sources of pride ("we rebuilt"); national pride in wartime, and pride in having refused a war.
- **Margins and extremes:** arrogance at its limit, and narcissistic personality disorder as its medical framing; supremacist movements built on group pride; pride that refuses all help, even to the point of harm ("too proud to beg"); the braggart nobody believes; chauvinism and jingoism; at the other edge, people with no pride left: self-abasement, chronic shame, and men who say they have "lost their pride" after losing work or family.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* the words for pride and arrogance, and whether one word covers both; boasting songs and praise songs; victory songs and dances; proverbs on pride and the fall that follows; tales of the proud punished and the humble raised; how a hunter's, fisher's or warrior's success is announced, as each people shares it; postures and gestures of pride (chest out, chin up) and of humility; a group's emblems (crests, banners, colors), as each people shares them; songs about one's town, valley or people; craftsmen's marks; how a family's achievements are shown at gatherings; how children are praised, and for what.
  - *Writing:* scriptures and sermons on pride as a sin and on humility; moral philosophy on self-respect and greatness of soul; memoirs of achievement; local and family histories written with pride; poems of national and regional pride; self-help on self-esteem; parenting books on praise.
  - *Public records:* national, state and provincial symbols (flags, anthems, mottoes, seals, official birds, flowers and songs); heritage months and national days; flag ceremonies and pledges in schools; the national-pride items in surveys; official tourism slogans; plaques and monuments; town signs that boast ("home of...").
  - *Mass media:* broadcasts of national teams; patriotic advertising; local features on hometown heroes; coverage of Pride; sports talk and trash talk; anthems of pride on the radio; "made here" campaigns.
  - *Online:* brags and humblebrags; "proud parent" posts; flag emojis and regional memes; bragging-rights threads; Pride profile frames; local-pride groups; posts calling out arrogance.
  - *Commerce:* regional and team merchandise; flags and decals; trophies, plaques and diploma frames; "made in" and "buy local" labels (*hecho en México*); class rings and letter jackets; graduation stoles and sashes; Pride Month products and sponsorships; vanity plates on cars.
  - *Organizations:* heritage and ethnic associations; alumni associations and booster clubs; veterans' groups; Pride committees; hometown clubs; unions and craft guilds (pride in a trade); supporters' clubs; service clubs and their "citizen of the year" awards; historical societies; cultural centers, as the nations share them.
  - *The people who handle it:* coaches; teachers; drill instructors; therapists (self-esteem; narcissism); clergy, on pride as a sin; trophy engravers; tour guides who tell what a place is proud of; parade organizers.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' remarks on a people's pride or arrogance; national stereotypes recorded as outsiders' claims, never adopted; guides warning newcomers what not to criticize; foreign journalists at national celebrations.
  - *Scholarship and data:* research on authentic and hubristic pride; studies of self-esteem and narcissism; national-identity and national-pride surveys; studies of self-enhancement across cultures; social-identity research (Tajfel and Turner); histories of nationalism and of pride movements; studies of collective narcissism (Golec de Zavala and colleagues).

**Key questions:** Is pride a sin, a virtue, or a right of the stigmatized? May a person be proud of themselves, or only of their family and people?

## 4. Recognition

**Working definition:** others' acknowledgment of a person's or group's worth, achievements or standing, from a word of credit to a formal honor or title.
**Facets:** credit and praise for achievement (esteem); recognition of equal standing or identity (the politics of recognition); formal recognition (titles, honors, diplomas and credentials); acknowledgment of wrongs (apologies, land acknowledgments); recognition withheld.
**Neighbors and opposites:** acknowledgment, credit, praise, approval, validation, appreciation, esteem; being ignored, neglect, misrecognition, erasure. **Excess:** craving approval, grabbing credit, flattery. **Deficiency:** ingratitude; unsung and thankless work.

- **Words:** "credit where credit is due", "shout-out", "props", "kudos", "pat on the back", "unsung hero", "thankless job", "glory hog", "steal the credit", "validation", "acknowledge", "honorable mention", "participation trophy"; titles of address (Dr., "sir" and "ma'am"); Spanish *reconocimiento* (in Mexico also the certificate or plaque itself), *mérito*, *palmadita en la espalda*, *lambiscón* and *barbero* (Mexican: flatterer), *hacer la barba*, *quedar bien*, *protagonismo*, professional titles as address in Mexico (*licenciado*, *ingeniero*, *maestro*, *doctor*), *don* and *doña*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is seeking recognition healthy or vain? Taylor's "The Politics of Recognition" (1992) and Honneth's three spheres of love, rights and esteem (*The Struggle for Recognition*, 1992); achieved against ascribed status (Trompenaars; Capricorn); good deeds done in secret ("let not thy left hand know", Matthew 6:3); whether credit goes to the individual, the team or the family.
- **Feeling and the body:** the glow of praise; the sting of being passed over; applause and standing ovations; whether public praise pleases or embarrasses (Mexican *pena* when singled out *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** thanking and crediting; employee recognition programs; awards banquets; likes and follower counts; reviews and ratings; thank-you notes; who is thanked for the meal at home.
- **Life course:** praise in child-rearing; stickers and gold stars; honor rolls; the Mexican *escolta*, the flag guard chosen from the best students; graduations; retirement parties; obituaries as the last recognition.
- **Ritual:** awards ceremonies (the Oscars; the Latin Grammys; investitures into the Order of Canada); halls of fame; commencement; land acknowledgments; state apologies (Canada, 2008, for residential schools; California's 2019 apology to Native peoples; Mexico's 2021 apologies to the Yaqui people and to the Chinese community for the Torreón massacre of 1911).
- **Institutions:** honors systems (the Presidential Medal of Freedom and the Congressional Gold Medal; the Order of Canada, 1967, and the Order of British Columbia, 1989; Mexico's Belisario Domínguez Medal and national prizes for the arts and sciences; the California Hall of Fame, 2006); awards bodies and halls of fame in the arts, sports and trades; schools' honor rolls and awards nights; workplaces' recognition programs and service pins; professional bodies and how they treat foreign credentials; churches' honors for lay service; newspapers' profiles of local people, and the obituary page.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** recognition is itself the reward; withholding it is a sanction; credit theft and the resentment it breeds; sanctions on self-promotion and on flatterers (*lambiscón*).
- **Expression:** acceptance speeches and dedications; liner notes and film credits; *corridos* that name a hero; murals honoring local figures; plaques, statues and street names.
- **Exemplars:** the long-unsung (the Nisei soldiers whose Medals of Honor were upgraded in 2000; Chinese railroad workers, honored by the U.S. Department of Labor in 2014 *check*; Dolores Huerta, long in Chávez's shadow); anti-exemplars: the credit thief, the flatterer.
- **History:** the Chicano and Red Power movements as struggles to be seen and named; Canada's 2006 apology to Chinese Canadians for the head tax; the Japanese American and Japanese Canadian redress movements and the apologies of 1988; California's 2005 apology for the 1930s Mexican Repatriation.
- **Measures:** Gallup's workplace item on recognition or praise in the last seven days (Q12; by country and industry *check* finer levels); honors per capita (Order of Canada by province *check*); perceived respect and appreciation at work (*check* surveys); state apologies and land acknowledgments counted.
- **Variation:** gender (women's work credited less: Rossiter's "Matilda effect", 1993); race; immigrants and their credentials; class (whose work counts as skilled); seniority; individual against team-centered workplaces.
- **Relations:** being seen (recognition's precondition); honor and pride; humiliation and erasure; merit and hierarchy (Capricorn, which also lists recognition); approval and praise (near-synonyms to settle); belonging (Cancer); gratitude.
- **Who it applies to:** individuals, groups, nations, states; animals (medals for military and rescue dogs); companies (rankings, "best places to work"); places renamed to recognize first peoples (Haida Gwaii, 2010; the Salish Sea, 2009-2010); machines: whether AI should get credit (journals' rules against listing AI as an author; present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** housework and care; farm and immigrant labor; women behind famous men; Native contributions left out of local histories.
- **Change:** likes and follower counts; land acknowledgments spreading and criticized; renaming of mountains, schools and streets; credit for work made with AI.
- **The senses:** applause, cheers and the standing ovation; one's name called aloud (at graduation, over a loudspeaker, from a stage); the handshake, the pat on the back, the hug from the coach; the medal hung around the neck; the cake and the meal in an honoree's name; stage lights and the spotlight; the silence of being passed over, as people describe it.
- **Space, place and things:** plaques, donor walls and "employee of the month" frames; trophy cases; the stage, the podium and the head table; buildings, streets, benches and bricks named for people; halls of fame as buildings people visit; certificates on office and kitchen walls; the *reconocimiento* framed in a Mexican office or home; memorial benches and trees; credits on screen and on album sleeves.
- **Time and rhythm:** award seasons (film awards in winter and spring; school awards nights in June); service anniversaries (five, ten, twenty-five years); retirement parties; yearly honors lists; recognition that comes too late or after death; recognition delayed for decades and then given (the Nisei soldiers' upgraded medals); the speed of online praise, and how fast it fades.
- **The economy of it:** the recognition trade (trophies, plaques, programs, banquets); whether praise is used in place of pay; "award-winning" as a selling point (prize stickers on books and wines); awards people pay to receive; tips as recognition of service; attention and likes as a kind of currency; the career value of credits and bylines; who gets the credit, and the money, for team work.
- **Conflict and crisis:** fights over credit (who wrote the song, who had the idea, whose name goes first); quitting after being passed over; boycotts of award shows over who is left out (#OscarsSoWhite, 2015); quarrels over renaming places and removing names and monuments; groups protesting to be recognized; arguments over land acknowledgments; crises that bring sudden recognition (nurses and grocery workers in 2020; firefighters after wildfires) and how long it lasts.
- **Margins and extremes:** the craving for approval as a problem people seek help for; credit thieves; collectors of awards and buyers of vanity awards; people who claim honors they never received; at the other edge, those who refuse honors (people who turn down medals and orders, and their stated reasons), anonymous donors and artists who will not sign their work.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* forms of thanks and praise in speech; titles and forms of address; names given to honor a deed (a new name, a praise name), as each people gives them; gifts that mark an achievement; seats of honor and the order in which people are served; songs made to honor a person, as the nations share them; witnesses called to recognize an event, as the nations describe it; public announcements at gatherings (who is named and thanked, and in what order); naming children after benefactors; proverbs on gratitude and ingratitude; tales of the unrecognized hero; the toast.
  - *Writing:* acknowledgments in books and theses; dedications; letters of recommendation; testimonials; eulogies; etiquette books on thank-you notes; biographies that restore forgotten figures; scriptures on good deeds done in secret and on giving honor where it is due; histories of "hidden figures".
  - *Public records:* honors lists and award citations; lists of hall-of-fame inductees; school honor rolls and award nights; place, street and school names, and the records of their renaming; registers of monuments and plaques; official apologies and land acknowledgments as published; service medals and long-service awards; proclamations of days in someone's honor.
  - *Mass media:* awards shows; "person of the year" lists; features on local heroes; obituaries; film credits and liner notes; thank-you notices in local papers; most-valuable-player awards; "40 under 40" lists.
  - *Online:* likes, shares and followers; endorsements on professional networks; ratings of people (drivers, sellers, hosts); shout-outs and tags; viral thank-you posts; appreciation threads; crediting, and failing to credit, the people who made a work ("credit the artist").
  - *Commerce:* trophy, plaque and medal makers; employee-recognition programs and the firms that sell them; service-anniversary gifts (the gold watch); certificates and frames; banquet venues for award nights; paid "vanity" awards; greeting cards of congratulation and thanks.
  - *Organizations:* awards committees and academies (film, music, sport); halls of fame; professional associations and their honors; service clubs' awards; churches' honors for lay service; alumni associations' distinguished-alumni awards; unions' honors for long membership; veterans' groups; cultural centers that honor elders, as the nations share them; youth organizations' badges and ranks.
  - *The people who handle it:* managers and human-resources staff who run recognition programs; teachers handing out praise; coaches; award juries; ceremony planners; obituary writers; engravers; speechwriters; historians who recover forgotten figures.
  - *Outsiders:* guides for newcomers on giving and receiving praise and thanks; business-culture guides on public against private praise; expatriates' accounts of being praised, or ignored, at work.
  - *Scholarship and data:* the philosophy of recognition (Hegel; Taylor; Honneth; Fraser); organizational research on employee recognition; surveys of feeling appreciated at work; studies of gratitude; studies of credit and authorship (the "Matthew effect", Merton, 1968); studies of honors systems; studies of commemoration and naming.

**Key questions:** Who deserves recognition, and for what: achievement, existence or rank? Is seeking it a right or a vanity?

## 5. Being seen

**Working definition:** being noticed and perceived as one really is; the need to be witnessed, and the rules for who may be visible, when and to whom.
**Facets:** attention from others; being witnessed at life events and ceremonies; groups' visibility in public life and the media (representation); being known as oneself (intimacy, "feeling seen"); being watched (surveillance, the gaze, the evil eye); invisibility and erasure; anonymity chosen.
**Neighbors and opposites:** attention, visibility, witness, representation, the spotlight; invisibility, being ignored, anonymity, privacy. **Excess:** attention-seeking, exhibitionism, overexposure, surveillance. **Deficiency:** invisibility, neglect, erasure.

- **Words:** "I see you", "feel seen", "seen and not heard", "spotlight", "limelight", "attention-seeking", "pick me", "wallflower", "invisible", "visibility", "representation matters", "main character", "pics or it didn't happen"; Spanish *ningunear* (to treat someone as nobody; Paz on *ninguneo*), *invisibilizar* and *invisibilización*, *no me pela* (Mexican: pays me no attention), *hacerse notar*, *lucirse*, *apantallar* (to dazzle), *llamar la atención* (which in Mexico also means to scold, a telling double sense), *dar el avión* (Mexican: to humor someone without listening *check*), *protagonismo*, *mal de ojo*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is wanting to be seen natural or vain? "Children should be seen and not heard"; humility and anonymity (anonymous giving; Alcoholics Anonymous); the God who sees (Genesis 16:13); witnessing as a duty (the calling of witnesses at Coast Salish and other Northwest Coast gatherings, as the nations describe it *check*); whether visibility is power or danger (the closet; families who teach their children to keep their heads down).
- **Feeling and the body:** eye-contact norms (a direct gaze as respect or as challenge; lowered eyes as respect toward elders in some families and nations *check*); staring and "civil inattention" (Goffman, *Behavior in Public Places*, 1963); the evil eye and the *ojo de venado* amulet worn by children; stage fright and blushing; modesty and how much of the body may be seen.
- **Everyday practice:** posting online; dressing to be noticed or not; greeting everyone in a room on arrival (*check* as a Mexican norm); being acknowledged by shop staff; the invisibility of waiters, cleaners and drivers; nodding to strangers on trails; the "Seattle Freeze"; who is looked at in meetings.
- **Life course:** showing off babies; recitals and the parent in the audience; teenagers' "imaginary audience" (Elkind, 1967); being overlooked in old age; obituaries.
- **Ritual:** ceremonies with witnesses (marriage witnesses, *testigos*; witnesses at feasts, as the nations describe them); a child's *presentación* at church; the *quinceañera*'s entrance and the debutante ball; walking at graduation; vigils.
- **Institutions:** the census (undercounts of Hispanic people and of Native people on reservations in 2020 *check*); race and ethnicity categories (the 2024 revision of the federal standard, adding a Middle Eastern or North African category); missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls (Canada's National Inquiry, 2019; BC's "Highway of Tears"); media representation studies (UCLA's Hollywood Diversity Report; USC Annenberg's Inclusion Initiative; GLAAD); newsrooms and whom they cover; museums, school textbooks and heritage months that include or leave out groups.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** attention as reward (fame, likes, the spotlight); ignoring as sanction (shunning, the silent treatment, *ningunear*); public exposure as punishment (see humiliation); "attention-seeker" as an insult.
- **Expression:** Ralph Ellison's *Invisible Man* (1952); Matika Wilbur's Project 562, photographs of Native nations; murals that put a community on the wall; representation in film and television; Native-made film (*Smoke Signals*, 1998).
- **Exemplars:** those who made groups visible (Harvey Milk's call to come out); photographers (Edward S. Curtis, whose images of Native peoples are contested, and Native photographers' replies); influencers; anti-exemplars: the attention-seeker.
- **History:** Native peoples erased from maps, histories and the "vanishing" myth; Mexican American history left out of state histories; Asian Americans and Asian Canadians left out of local histories; coming out and "visibility" as a strategy of gay liberation; the MMIWG movement.
- **Measures:** census undercount estimates (the Post-Enumeration Survey); representation counts in media; social-media use (Pew, by group); loneliness surveys (*check* which); Gallup's "treated with respect" item; the items on being ignored in the Everyday Discrimination Scale (Williams et al., 1997).
- **Variation:** gender (who is looked at, and how); age; race (being overlooked or being watched); disability; sexuality; recent immigrants and their children; each Indigenous nation.
- **Relations:** recognition; self-expression; dignity; humiliation (unwanted visibility); secrecy and privacy (Scorpio); isolation and solitude (Tier 2); surveillance and control (Scorpio).
- **Who it applies to:** children; elders; the poor and the unhoused; the dead (remembered on Día de Muertos); animals; overlooked towns and regions; machines: whether people want machines to "see" them (cameras, facial recognition) and whether people feel seen by chatbots (present-day surveys *check*).
- **Silences:** the unhoused; domestic and farm workers; the disabled; the elderly in care homes.
- **Change:** social media and influencer culture; surveillance technology; representation campaigns; talk of a loneliness epidemic.
- **The senses:** the gaze: being looked at, stared at, looked through; the spotlight and the camera flash; hearing one's name, and being greeted by name in a shop, a church or a bar ("where everybody knows your name"); touch as acknowledgment (a hand on the shoulder; the *abrazo*); clothes that make a person visible or not (bright colors; the high-visibility vest that marks the worker nobody looks at); the mirror and the selfie screen.
- **Space, place and things:** stages, front rows and head tables; porches and front windows, from which people see and are seen; the plaza and the evening stroll where people go to see and be seen (*dar la vuelta*, with young men and women once circling the plaza in opposite directions in many Mexican towns *check*); the places of the unseen (back kitchens, service corridors, loading docks, night shifts); the gym mirror; red carpets; portraits in public buildings; maps of census tracts that are hard to count.
- **Time and rhythm:** the days when a person is the center (a birthday, a wedding, a *quinceañera*, a funeral); seasons of visibility (graduation, the big game, election season); "fifteen minutes of fame"; fading from view in old age; night-shift and early-morning workers, who are never seen by day; how long a post is seen before it scrolls away.
- **The economy of it:** the attention economy; paying to be seen (advertising, sponsored posts, paid badges of verification); influencers paid by views; the low pay of unseen work (cleaners, farmworkers, care aides); jobs in which being seen is the work (models, hosts, greeters, news anchors); the market for cameras and for privacy (hiding from view as a product).
- **Conflict and crisis:** being watched as a threat (being followed or stared at by strangers; neighbors' cameras); quarrels over surveillance cameras on streets and in schools; visibility as a danger in times of conflict (coming out; being known as a member of a hated group); crises in which the unseen die (heat waves, in which old people living alone die unnoticed: Klinenberg's *Heat Wave*, Chicago, 1995; British Columbia's heat dome of 2021, when many who died were alone at home *check*); people missing for days before anyone noticed.
- **Margins and extremes:** attention-seeking at its limit (hoaxes and dangerous stunts for views); recluses and the voluntarily invisible (Japan's *hikikomori*, as an example from elsewhere); people who vanish on purpose; the totally exposed (reality-show families; child influencers); people treated as invisible by everyone (the unhoused; the forgotten in institutions); fame that turns into being hunted by cameras.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* greeting customs (who must greet whom, and the slight of not being greeted); customs of eye contact, and of lowering the eyes; beliefs about the harmful gaze (the evil eye, envy's look) and the amulets against it; customs of presenting a newborn, a bride or a newcomer to the community; witnessing at ceremonies, as the nations describe it; names and nicknames that make a person known; dress that makes a person stand out or disappear (festival dress, mourning dress, veils); children's games of hiding and seeking; tales of the overlooked who are finally seen (the youngest son; the cinder girl) and tales of invisibility; proverbs about being seen and not heard; where people sit or stand at gatherings (the front, the back, the edge); praise given in public.
  - *Writing:* memoirs of feeling invisible (immigrants, servants, disabled people, the old); novels about invisibility and being noticed; etiquette books on staring, greeting and introductions; religious writing on the God who sees and on humble anonymity; teenagers' diaries.
  - *Public records:* census forms and their categories (who is counted, and who is missed); census-undercount estimates; school textbooks (who appears in them); museum exhibits and labels; heritage months; monuments and public portraits (whose faces are shown); the faces on stamps and money.
  - *Mass media:* casting in film and television; whom the news covers; the faces in advertising; talk shows; documentary portraits; local features on ordinary people; reality television.
  - *Online:* follower counts and views; read receipts and being "left on read"; selfies and stories; posts about "feeling seen"; representation hashtags; lurking and anonymous accounts; influencer culture; doorbell cameras and neighborhood apps (being watched by neighbors).
  - *Commerce:* portrait studios; fashion that stands out or blends in; the faces chosen for advertising; dolls and toys in many skin tones; casting and modeling agencies; security cameras sold for homes; celebrity endorsements.
  - *Organizations:* groups that press for media representation (GLAAD; the National Hispanic Media Coalition); disability and visibility groups; visiting programs for the old, the isolated and the bereaved; museums and historical societies that recover overlooked people; Pride organizations; community photography projects; mentoring programs; church visiting committees; veterans' groups.
  - *The people who handle it:* photographers; casting directors; teachers (whom they call on); nurses and care workers; social workers; census takers; journalists; museum curators; front-desk and other service workers, who greet or ignore; outreach workers with the unhoused.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' remarks on staring, greeting and eye contact; guides for newcomers on greetings and introductions; outsiders' photographs of a people, read critically; outside accounts of the evil eye, read critically.
  - *Scholarship and data:* studies of gaze and eye contact across cultures; research on ostracism (Williams's Cyberball experiments); studies of loneliness and of feeling invisible; research on "mattering" (Rosenberg and McCullough, 1981 *check*); content analyses of media representation; census-undercount research; folklore studies of the evil eye (Dundes, ed., *The Evil Eye*, 1981 *check*); studies of surveillance and of being watched.

**Key questions:** Is being seen a need everyone has, or something to earn or avoid? Who may be visible, and when is visibility a danger?

## 6. Dignity

**Working definition:** the worth a person has as a person, and the bearing and treatment that honor it; usually held to be inherent rather than earned.
**Facets:** inherent human dignity; dignity in bearing (composure, self-possession); dignity at work and in poverty; dignity in illness, old age and death; the "dignity of risk" (choosing for oneself); dignity in public treatment (courtesy, respect); the dignity of peoples.
**Neighbors and opposites:** respect, self-respect, decency, decorum, worth; degradation, indignity, humiliation, objectification. **Excess:** stiffness, pomposity, "standing on one's dignity". **Deficiency:** degradation, self-abasement.

- **Words:** "dignity", "with dignity", "self-respect", "respect", "decent", "decency", "dignity of labor", "death with dignity", "dehumanizing", "treat people like people"; Spanish *dignidad*, *digno* and *vida digna*, *trabajo digno*, *vivienda digna* (decent housing, a common phrase in Mexican public life), *trato digno* (a standard in Mexican public services *check*), *respeto*, *darle su lugar* (to give someone their due place), *faltar al respeto*, *gente decente* (a phrase with a class history), *bien educado*, *la digna rabia* (a Zapatista phrase).
- **Beliefs and standing:** inherent dignity (Kant; the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 1; Catholic social teaching from *Rerum Novarum*, 1891, to *Dignitas Infinita*, 2024); dignity cultures against honor and face cultures (Leung and Cohen, 2011); Peter Berger's "On the Obsolescence of the Concept of Honor" (1970), on dignity replacing honor; whether dignity can be lost or only violated; dignity through work (Lamont, *The Dignity of Working Men*, 2000; Hodson, *Dignity at Work*, 2001); respect for all beings in Indigenous teachings, as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** composure in suffering; posture; being clean and well dressed when poor ("Sunday best"; *andar bien arreglado*); bodily dignity in hospitals and care homes (nakedness, being fed and washed); dignity in dying (Chochinov's dignity therapy, Manitoba).
- **Everyday practice:** forms of address (*usted*, titles, first names); courtesy to service workers; how the poor are treated at food banks (the "client choice" model); waiting in lines and waiting rooms; searches and ID checks; how elders are spoken to.
- **Life course:** teaching respect (*respeto* in Mexican families: Valdés, *Con Respeto*, 1996); children's dignity and spanking (whether parents and teachers still hit children, and what people think of it *check*); dignity in old age and in care homes; death with dignity.
- **Ritual:** funerals as dignity for the dead; burial of the unclaimed; reburial of ancestors returned by museums in the U.S. and Canada; the dignified transfer of soldiers' remains; caps and gowns.
- **Institutions:** hospitals, hospices and palliative care (dignity at the end of life; assisted dying where it is offered, and the doctors and patients who choose it or refuse it); care homes and home care for the old and the disabled; disability services and the "dignity of risk" (Perske, 1972); unions and the language of dignity at work (the United Farm Workers); shelters, food banks and church charities; Dignity Village, Portland's self-run camp for unhoused people (2000).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** admiration for grace under pressure; contempt for those who degrade others; "undignified" behavior mocked; apology and amends after an indignity.
- **Expression:** César Chávez's speeches on the dignity of farmworkers; Dorothea Lange's *Migrant Mother* (Nipomo, California, 1936); Chief Joseph's surrender words as recorded (1877; the transcription is disputed); *corridos* of the poor; the Zapatistas' language of dignity.
- **Exemplars:** César Chávez and Dolores Huerta; Fred Korematsu (see Aries); residential- and boarding-school survivors who testified; elders; anti-exemplars: those who degrade the weak.
- **History:** the farmworker movement; Japanese American and Japanese Canadian internment; residential and boarding schools, as survivors tell it (Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, 2015); the Bracero Program; the Dust Bowl migrants' treatment ("Okies"); the 1930s Mexican Repatriation.
- **Measures:** Gallup World Poll, "treated with respect all day yesterday" (country); Mexico's discrimination survey (ENADIS, INEGI; regions); the Everyday Discrimination Scale in U.S. surveys; Statistics Canada's discrimination questions (General Social Survey on Social Identity; Canadian Social Survey); assisted-dying reports (the Oregon Health Authority's annual reports; Health Canada's annual MAID reports).
- **Variation:** class ("decency" as a class marker); occupation; ethnicity; disability; age; each Indigenous nation; recent immigrants.
- **Relations:** honor (dignity's counterpart; overlap to settle); pride; humiliation (its violation); recognition (Honneth); justice (Libra); compassion (Pisces); autonomy (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** all humans or only some; the dead; animals (whether people grant animals dignity, as in the care of old working animals); nature (rivers and mountains as relatives in Indigenous teachings, as the nations state them); machines: present-day reactions to robots being abused (hitchBOT, a Canadian hitchhiking robot destroyed in Philadelphia in 2015; videos of robots being kicked).
- **Silences:** undignified care of the old; the dying poor; the daily indignities of poverty and of migrant life.
- **Change:** assisted dying becoming more common (Canada); dignity in the disability movement; "dignity" as a word in public life ("dignified" wages, housing and vacations; *vida digna*).
- **The senses:** the smells that mark degradation or care (an unwashed ward against clean linen; a shelter's disinfectant); how a body is touched in care (gently, roughly, without a word); food and dignity: choosing what to eat, eating at a table, being fed by hand; being called by one's name and title rather than a number or "honey"; quiet and courtesy against shouting in hospitals, offices and lines; wearing one's own clothes in a hospital or care home; clean, pressed clothes as the poor man's dignity.
- **Space, place and things:** waiting rooms and lines, and where people are made to wait (outdoors, standing, in view of others); private rooms against open wards; restrooms and who may use them; how food banks are laid out (a counter that hands out boxes against a "grocery store" where people choose); shelters with and without privacy; graves for the poor and the unclaimed; how housing for the poor looks from the street; ramps, curb cuts and elevators, and what their absence says; the walker, the wheelchair and the hearing aid as things people hide or wear with pride.
- **Time and rhythm:** waiting as an indignity (lines, waiting lists, hold music); time to say goodbye at the end of life; breaks and mealtimes at work, and who gets them; dignity in old age, and the moment people say it was lost (being moved to a home; losing the car keys); how long the dead wait for burial, and what that says.
- **The economy of it:** earning one's own way against living on charity, and how each is felt; "a living wage" and "decent work" as claims of dignity; the cost of dignified care, and who can pay it; dignity sold as a premium (private rooms; priority lines); cash given freely against vouchers and food boxes, argued over as a matter of dignity; the industries that serve the poor and the old, and how they speak of their clients.
- **Conflict and crisis:** strikes fought in the name of dignity (the farmworkers; the Memphis sanitation workers' "I AM A MAN" signs, 1968); the dignity of the displaced after fires and floods (shelters in gyms; trailers; donated clothes); the treatment of the defeated and of captives in war; the COVID-19 pandemic in care homes, and the dying who could not be visited; disasters and the scramble for aid, and who keeps their composure in it.
- **Margins and extremes:** degradation at its far end (in camps, residential and boarding schools and other institutions, as survivors tell it); self-neglect, and the old who refuse help to keep their pride; people who keep their bearing in extreme poverty (Dorothea Lange's subjects, in their own later accounts); "undignified" pleasures and the people who scorn them (clowning, dancing badly, karaoke); debates over whether animals and the dead have dignity, and over dignity extended to machines.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* forms of respectful address (titles, *usted*, kin terms used for elders); customs of washing, dressing and burying the dead; how the sick and the old are fed, washed and clothed; customs that spare a guest or a poor neighbor from having to ask; how help is refused without shame; proverbs on keeping one's dignity in poverty; tales of the humble poor who keep their bearing; composure and bearing taught to children; what may never be done to a person (stripping, spitting, striking the face), as each people names it; mourning customs; respect for elders, as the nations describe it; "Sunday best"; teachings on respect for all beings, as the nations state them.
  - *Writing:* philosophical and religious writing on human dignity; memoirs of poverty, illness, disability and old age; workers' memoirs and union literature; survivors' testimony (residential and boarding schools; internment); essays on decency; etiquette books on courtesy to servants and service workers; nurses' and care workers' handbooks on dignity in care.
  - *Public records:* the published values statements of hospitals and care homes; government reports on poverty, care homes and the treatment of the old; truth-commission reports, in the survivors' words first; official apologies; public-health guidance on end-of-life care; the respect and discrimination items in government surveys; records of the burial of the unclaimed dead.
  - *Mass media:* documentaries on poverty, care homes and work; human-interest stories of dignity in hardship; exposés of degrading treatment in care homes, workplaces and shelters; advertising that shows, or exploits, the poor and the old; photojournalism of poverty; obituaries.
  - *Online:* viral videos of people treated with or without dignity; fundraising appeals, and what telling one's need costs the teller; forums of disabled, old and poor people; service workers' posts about how customers treat them; critiques of "poverty porn".
  - *Commerce:* funeral homes and their services; how incontinence products, mobility aids and hearing aids are marketed (dignity as a selling point); uniforms and workwear; brochures for care homes and assisted living; secondhand and thrift stores, and the shame or pride of shopping there.
  - *Organizations:* unions and worker centers; food banks and mutual-aid groups (the "client choice" model); self-advocacy groups of disabled people; elders' advocacy groups; hospice and palliative-care associations; church charities; groups led by unhoused people (self-run camps; street newspapers); veterans' groups; survivors' groups of residential and boarding schools, as they share their work; burial and funeral societies.
  - *The people who handle it:* nurses, aides and personal-support workers; hospice workers and chaplains; funeral directors; social workers; food-bank volunteers; shelter staff; teachers; union stewards; doctors who deliver bad news.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' remarks on how servants, the poor and the old are treated; guides for newcomers on forms of address and respect; foreign reporters on working conditions.
  - *Scholarship and data:* the philosophy and theology of dignity; research on dignity, honor and face cultures; medical research on dignity in care (Chochinov's dignity-conserving care); the sociology of work and dignity (Hodson; Lamont); ethnographies of poverty and of care homes; survey items on respect and discrimination; research on dehumanization (Haslam, 2006).

**Key questions:** Is dignity in every person by birth, or earned and kept by conduct? What may never be done to anyone, and who counts as "anyone"?

## 7. Humiliation

**Working definition:** being lowered in standing or worth against one's will, usually before others; also the act of lowering someone.
**Facets:** public shaming; ridicule and mockery; degradation (stripping, forced acts); defeat and how it is displayed; humiliation in love (being jilted or betrayed); collective humiliation of peoples and nations; lowering chosen (humility, penance); embarrassment, its mild form.
**Neighbors and opposites:** shame, embarrassment, ridicule, mortification, disgrace, degradation; honor, dignity, vindication, saving face. **Excess:** cruelty, bullying, a culture of public shaming. **Deficiency** (of the sense of shame): shamelessness (*sinvergüenza*).

- **Words:** "humiliate", "embarrass", "mortified", "put down", "roast", "dunk on", "own", "ratio", "walk of shame", "public shaming", "cancel", "loser", "eat crow", "lose face"; Spanish *humillar*, *vergüenza*, *pena* (Mexican: embarrassment, "me da pena"), *ridículo* ("hacer el ridículo"), *exhibir* (to expose publicly), *balconear* (Mexican: to expose someone's secrets), *quemar* (to ruin someone's name), *carrilla* (Mexican, especially northern: teasing, ribbing), the *albur* (a contest of double meanings with a loser), *chingar* and *el chingado* (Paz's reading of humiliation as violation, a classic claim), *ningunear*, *poner en su lugar*, *agachón*, *sinvergüenza*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is humiliating someone ever right (punishment, hazing that "builds character", humbling the proud)? Shame cultures against guilt cultures (Benedict, 1946, much criticized); Margalit, *The Decent Society* (1996): a decent society is one whose institutions don't humiliate; Christian humility as chosen lowering, and "turn the other cheek"; whether humiliation must be avenged (Scorpio) or forgiven; whether shaming is meant to cast a person out or to bring them back (shunning against forgiveness and return).
- **Feeling and the body:** blushing, lowered head, hidden face; "humiliated fury" (Helen Block Lewis, 1971); bodily humiliations (stripping, head-shaving, forced kneeling); the stress response to insult (Cohen et al., 1996).
- **Everyday practice:** teasing and *carrilla* among friends (where it is play and where it turns cruel); roasts; criticism at work, in public or in private; parents scolding in public; bullying at school and online; humiliation formats on reality television; online pile-ons; abuse of service workers.
- **Life course:** schoolyard bullying; hazing in fraternities, sports teams and the military; parents shaming children (including online); the humiliations of dependence in old age.
- **Ritual:** roasts; *lucha libre*'s bets of mask against mask and mask against hair, where the loser is unmasked or shaved; hazing rites; degradation ceremonies (Garfinkel, 1956), such as drumming out of a regiment and stripping of rank; Carnival's burning of "bad humor" (*quema del mal humor*, in Mexican carnivals *check* which); the charivari or "shivaree", the noisy mocking of a couple by their neighbors in rural North America.
- **Institutions:** schools and their anti-bullying programs; fraternities, sororities, sports teams and boot camps, where hazing lives; workplaces' rules on harassment and abusive bosses; social-media platforms and their rules on harassment and shared intimate images; reality television and the tabloid press as makers of humiliation; comedy clubs and the roast tradition (the Friars Club).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** humiliation is itself a sanction: who may use it (parents, teachers, coaches, drill sergeants, online crowds); sanctions on humiliators (punished bullies; "punching down" condemned); recovery after shaming (public apology; the comeback).
- **Expression:** *The Scarlet Letter* (1850); stand-up comedy and roast culture; "fail" videos and memes; *corridos* mocking rivals; insult duels in *albur* and in hip-hop battles; films of revenge for humiliation.
- **Exemplars:** those whose humiliation became a public cause (Amanda Todd of Port Coquitlam, BC; Olimpia Coral Melo of Puebla); comeback figures; the young men beaten and stripped in the Zoot Suit Riots; anti-exemplars: bullies, hazers.
- **History:** collective humiliations and how they are remembered: the 1846-1848 war and the loss of half of Mexico's territory, in Mexican schooling (*check* how it is taught); the Zoot Suit Riots (1943); internment; residential and boarding schools; the anti-Chinese riots and expulsions (Tacoma, 1885; Seattle, 1886; Vancouver, 1907); segregation signs aimed at Mexicans in the Southwest (*check* pilot cases); the suppression of the potlatch; contempt for the "Okies".
- **Measures:** bullying (CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey, by state; the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study in Canada; PISA's bullying measures for Mexico); INEGI's ENDIREH (emotional violence against women, including humiliation, by state) and its cyberbullying module (MOCIBA); the insult experiments (Cohen, Nisbett, Bowdle and Schwarz, 1996); workplace bullying surveys (*check* which); online harassment (Pew, 2021).
- **Variation:** gender (whether men's humiliation is tied to violence, women's to sexual reputation: questions to test); class; race; age; LGBTQ youth; where the line between joking and cruelty falls.
- **Relations:** honor and dignity (what it violates); pride (what it wounds); revenge and betrayal (Scorpio); anger (Aries); ridicule (near-synonym); shame (folded in here); being seen (unwanted visibility).

## 8. Generosity

**Working definition:** giving freely, beyond what is owed: money, goods, time, food, attention or praise.
**Facets:** charity to strangers; giving within family and kin (sharing, remittances); hospitality; gifts and their obligations; giving that makes standing (feasts, sponsorship, philanthropy); generosity of spirit (forgiving, praising, crediting); tipping.
**Neighbors and opposites:** charity, philanthropy, hospitality, magnanimity, sharing; stinginess, greed, selfishness. **Excess:** prodigality, giving to show off, gifts that bind or shame the receiver. **Deficiency:** stinginess, meanness.

## 9. Leadership

**Working definition:** guiding a group so that others follow willingly, by example, persuasion, vision or standing; distinct from the formal right to command (authority, Capricorn).
**Facets:** charismatic leadership; servant leadership; leading by example; consensus and consultative leadership; hereditary and ceremonial leadership; elected leadership; informal leaders (the respected elder, the team captain, the eldest sibling); leadership in crisis; women's leadership.

## 10. Play

**Working definition:** activity done for its own sake, set apart from ordinary life by rules or make-believe: games, sport as play, pretending, joking, toying.
**Facets:** children's free play; games of skill, chance, make-believe and vertigo (Caillois's four kinds, 1958); adult play (hobbies, games, sport, gambling); verbal play (joking, teasing, wordplay); play in music and theater; digital play; playfulness as a trait.
**Neighbors and opposites:** fun, games, recreation, leisure, sport, humor; work, seriousness, boredom, duty. **Excess:** frivolity, addiction (gambling, gaming), immaturity. **Deficiency:** joylessness, the over-scheduled childhood, "all work and no play".

- **Words:** "play", "fun", "game", "recess", "goof off", "horse around", "play hooky", "playdate", "free play", "work hard, play hard", "all work and no play makes Jack a dull boy", "kid at heart", "gamer", "spoilsport"; English *play* covers games, music and theater, where Spanish splits *jugar*, *tocar* and *actuar*; Spanish *juego*, *relajo* (playful disorder; Jorge Portilla, *Fenomenología del relajo*, 1966), *desmadre*, *cotorrear* and *cotorreo*, *echar relajo*, *pachanga*, *mitote* (from Nahuatl *check*), *recreo*, *kermés*, *lotería*, *matatena*, *canicas*, *trompo*, *balero*, *papalote*, *piñata*, the *albur*, *carrilla*.

## 11. Honor

### C20. Authenticity
- *Working definition:* being true to oneself, and things and people being "real" rather than fake: genuine feeling, genuine food, genuine art, the genuine person.
- *Why here:* the research notes give authenticity ("to be truly oneself, with worth not hanging on others' opinions"), pretense ("detects fake respect and fake compliments") and integrity; Self-expression lists authenticity among its neighbors and its Change line notes authenticity as a marketing word. *Also fits:* Aries (identity; C14, self-discovery), Sagittarius (honesty and truth), Gemini (C29, *honne* and *tatemae*).
- *What it would add:* the modern ideal of being oneself (Trilling, *Sincerity and Authenticity*, 1972; Taylor, *The Ethics of Authenticity*, 1991) and its everyday forms: "keep it real", "fake" as an insult, "sellout", authentic food and culture sold to tourists, and the suspicion of anyone too polished.
- *Avenues:* "fake", *falso*, *hipócrita* and "poser" as insults; "keeping it real" in hip hop and in politics; authenticity in advertising and tourism (MacCannell's staged authenticity, under Aries's Identity); the Authenticity Scale (Wood and colleagues, 2008 *check*); grunge's and punk's hatred of selling out; arguments over the "authentic" taco or the "real" mariachi; reviews that praise a place as "authentic"; celebrities praised for being "real".

### C21. Comedy and laughter
- *Working definition:* what a people finds funny, how it makes others laugh (jokes, clowning, satire, comedy as a performing art), and the standing of the comic.
- *Why here:* humor is a Tier 2 concept, under seven signs; the research notes give playfulness ("plays and clowns to steal the scene"); Play's Relations list humor, and Joy's senses list laughter. Gemini proposes wit, wordplay and humor as its C10, centered on quickness with words; this entry takes comedy as a performance and laughter as joy shown. *Also fits:* Gemini (C10, wit and humor; C27, teasing and banter), Aquarius (satire and nonconformity).
- *What it would add:* the comedian as a figure (loved, envied, feared), physical and clown comedy, satire of the powerful, the joke at someone's expense and its limits (where comedy meets humiliation), and laughter as the sound of a happy gathering.
- *Avenues:* comedy clubs, specials and their audiences; Cantinflas and Chespirito (under Play's Exemplars); the Mexican *carpa* (C11); regional and ethnic humor and where people say it crosses a line; satire magazines and cartoons (*El Chamuco*; editorial cartoons); clowns at children's parties and fiestas; laughter research (Provine, *Laughter*, 2000); the Humor Styles Questionnaire (Martin and colleagues, 2003); Bergson, *Laughter* (1900); what may not be joked about, and who decides.

### C22. Shame
- *Working definition:* the painful sense of being seen, by others or by oneself, as lesser, bad or exposed; and, in many languages, also the good sense of modesty that keeps a person from shameful acts.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group humiliation / ridicule; the file's opening notes that the research notes folded shame into humiliation and that "shame has no home on any sign's list yet"; the research notes list shame as borderline, named beside humiliation but never developed. The author writes for men, and men's shame (at failure, at weakness, at needing help) is one of the live subjects of the present, framed here as questions for each group. *Also fits:* Pisces (guilt and the hidden), Scorpio (secrecy), Capricorn (reputation), Aries (C19, which names Tagalog *hiya*).
- *What it would add:* shame as a feeling and a social force apart from the act of humiliation: shame against guilt (Benedict, under Humiliation's Beliefs; Tangney and Dearing, *Shame and Guilt*, 2002); Spanish *vergüenza* as a virtue (*tener vergüenza*, having a sense of decency) against *sinvergüenza*; *pena*; the shame of poverty, illness, unemployment and failure; and stigma (Goffman, *Stigma*, 1963).
- *Avenues:* *vergüenza* and *pena* in Mexican ethnography and in everyday talk; men's shame and help-seeking (Brené Brown's interviews with men *check*; men's groups); shame in sermons and confession; Scheff's sociology of shame; what families hide out of shame (illness, debt, a child in trouble); "no shame" and "shameless" as praise and as blame online; shame in songs (the *ranchera* of the man who lost everything).

### C23. Contempt and condescension
- *Working definition:* looking down on others as beneath consideration, and showing it: the sneer, the snub, the condescending tone.
- *Why here:* contempt is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs, with no home; the research notes give contempt ("for service, for thrift and for the audience it depends on"), and privilege and elitism. It is the opposite of respect (C17), and the stance of pride gone bad. *Also fits:* Capricorn (hierarchy), Taurus (C28, class), Scorpio (power).
- *What it would add:* contempt as a moral emotion (one of the three "moral emotions" of Rozin, Lowery, Imada and Haidt, 1999, beside anger and disgust) and as a social habit: snobbery, contempt for service workers, regional and class contempt (*naco*, "redneck", "white trash", quoted only briefly), contempt for the rich, and contempt between partners.
- *Avenues:* Rozin and colleagues (1999); contempt as a warning sign in marriages (Gottman's research, which Cancer's C18 cites for stonewalling); class insults and the groups they target (under Taurus C28); how customers treat waiters and clerks, in workers' accounts; snobbery (Epstein, *Snobbery*, 2002; Fussell, *Class*, 1983); eye-rolling and other gestures of contempt; contempt between city and country, coast and inland, north and south (*chilangos* and *norteños*, as each describes the other).

### C24. Vanity and narcissism
- *Working definition:* too much concern with one's looks, image or importance; and narcissism, the trait of needing admiration and feeling special, from everyday vanity to its far end.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group egocentricity / self-absorption / selfishness / narcissism; the research notes give vanity, arrogance ("takes its own arrogance and pomposity for self-confidence"), self-image ("will not give itself a bad review") and self-importance. Aries proposes self-centeredness and entitlement (C20) and names Leo for narcissism; this entry takes vanity of looks and image and the narcissism debates, and leaves entitlement there. *Also fits:* Aries (C20), Taurus (self-worth).
- *What it would add:* vanity as a sin, a joke and a market (mirrors, selfies, cosmetic surgery, men's grooming), and the public argument over whether the young are more narcissistic than before (Twenge and Campbell, and the rebuttals, under Pride's Measures).
- *Avenues:* the Narcissistic Personality Inventory (Raskin and Hall, 1979); cosmetic-procedure figures (the American Society of Plastic Surgeons' annual reports *check*); research on selfies and self-image; "vanity of vanities" (Ecclesiastes) and *vanidad* in sermons; the myth of Narcissus, as it is retold; the men's grooming and cosmetics market; jokes and sayings about vain men and vain women, and whether they differ; the *Catrina* as a figure of vanity before death (C9).

### C25. Reputation and good name
- *Working definition:* what others think and say of a person, a family, a business or a place, built over time and spread by talk.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group status / rank / reputation / public life; Gemini leaves reputation to "Capricorn or Leo, where Gossip's Relations place it"; Honor's facets include personal honor as good name, and Spanish *honra* names it (under Honor's Words). *Also fits:* Capricorn (reputation and status), Gemini (gossip).
- *What it would add:* the good name as something owned and guarded (a family's name in a small town, a tradesman's name, a business's reviews), and the ways it is made and lost: gossip, the press, reviews, *el qué dirán* (under Self-expression's Words).
- *Avenues:* "a good name is rather to be chosen than great riches" (Proverbs 22:1) in sermons and sayings; *el qué dirán*; reputations of families in small towns, in memoirs and local histories; online reviews and ratings of businesses and of people; research on gossip as reputation management (Emler; Dunbar); how a ruined reputation is rebuilt (apology; moving away; good works); the reputations of places ("the bad side of town"; town nicknames); brands and their "reputation" surveys.

**Concepts other languages name** (that English has no single word for)

### C26. Face (*miànzi* and *liǎn*)
- *Working definition:* the public worth a person claims and others grant in an encounter, which can be given, saved and lost; Chinese splits it into *miànzi* (prestige from success and standing) and *liǎn* (the moral standing a decent person must not lose), as Hu Hsien Chin described them ("The Chinese Concepts of 'Face'", 1944).
- *Why here:* the file's opening names face as "the fourth corner" of the field of worth (with pride, dignity and honor) and notes it has no entry; Leung and Cohen's dignity, honor and face cultures (2011) are cited under Dignity and Honor; Gemini's C29 (*honne* and *tatemae*) and Taurus's C44 (admitting mistakes) both name Leo for face. English has the phrases "lose face" and "save face", but no concept that holds face whole. *Also fits:* Gemini (C29), Taurus (C44), Capricorn (reputation), Libra (diplomacy).
- *What it would add:* worth as something negotiated in every meeting, and held jointly: giving others face, sparing them embarrassment, and the duty not to make anyone lose face in public. It also joins Recognition, Humiliation and Honor through one idea.
- *Avenues:* Goffman, "On Face-Work" (1955); politeness theory's positive and negative face (Brown and Levinson, 1987); Ting-Toomey's face-negotiation theory; Japanese *mentsu* and Korean *chemyeon*, as examples from elsewhere, and how the Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Filipino communities of the pilot speak of face; "lose face" in English, said to be a loan translation from Chinese *check*; Mexican Spanish *dar la cara*, *quedar bien* and *no me hagas quedar mal*; business-culture guides on face; how criticism is given at work (in private or in public).

### C27. *Sprezzatura* and cool (effortless grace)
- *Working definition:* the Italian *sprezzatura*, "a certain nonchalance" that hides all effort and makes the hard look easy (Castiglione, *The Book of the Courtier*, 1528); and its modern cousin, "cool": composure, style and detachment worn as a way of being.
- *Why here:* self-expression, display and being seen all assume effort shown; *sprezzatura* names the opposite ideal, display that must look unforced. "Cool" is English, but it came into wide use from Black American jazz musicians *check*, and Robert Farris Thompson traced an "aesthetic of the cool" to West African ideals of composure ("An Aesthetic of the Cool", 1973). *Also fits:* Aries (C2, appearance and self-presentation), Taurus (C24, beauty), Aquarius (nonconformity).
- *What it would add:* the prestige of seeming not to try, and the scorn for the "try-hard": the effortless athlete, the cool kid, the musician who never breaks a sweat, the man who does not show he cares; and the costs of keeping it up.
- *Avenues:* Castiglione; Thompson (1973); Pountain and Robins, *Cool Rules* (2000); Dinerstein, *The Origins of Cool in Postwar America* (2017); "cool" and its replacements in youth slang, by generation; *buena onda* and the Mexican youth culture of *la Onda* in the 1960s; the *pachuco*'s style (under Self-expression's History); "try-hard" as an insult; who is voted "coolest" in yearbooks; cool in advertising.

### C28. *Bella figura*
- *Working definition:* the Italian ideal of making a good impression in public (dress, manners, bearing, a well-laid table, a well-made gift), and the shame of a *brutta figura*.
- *Why here:* Self-expression's Words give *las apariencias* and *el qué dirán*; Honor's give *quedar bien* and *quedar mal*. Italian names the positive ideal behind them as one phrase, where English must say "keeping up appearances", which sounds like criticism. *Also fits:* Aries (C2, appearance and self-presentation), Taurus (C40, display of wealth), Gemini (C6, politeness and manners).
- *What it would add:* appearance as a social duty and a courtesy, not only vanity: dressing well out of respect for others, hosting so as not to shame the family, and the fear of looking poor, careless or rude in front of others.
- *Avenues:* Severgnini, *La Bella Figura* (2006), as an insider's account; Italian communities of the pilot (San Francisco's North Beach; Vancouver's Commercial Drive) and what they keep of the ideal; Mexican *aparentar*, *quedar bien* and *el qué dirán*; dressing up to go downtown, to church or to the airport, then and now; what is served to guests so as not to look poor; Sunday best (C9).

### C29. *Duende*
- *Working definition:* the Spanish word, made famous by Federico García Lorca's lecture "Juego y teoría del duende" (1933), for the dark, mysterious power that seizes a performer and moves an audience, born of struggle and closeness to death rather than of skill.
- *Why here:* Creativity's Feeling and the body names inspiration and flow, and Joy's names tears and the *grito*; *duende* names what neither holds: the shiver an audience feels when a singer or dancer goes beyond technique. It is in the pilot's language. *Also fits:* Pisces (the hidden; transcendence), Scorpio (death), Sagittarius (the sacred).
- *What it would add:* the performer's power over feeling as something a culture names, prizes and looks for: in flamenco, in the *ranchera* sung with *sentimiento*, in the blues and "soul", in the preacher's voice; and the belief that suffering makes the greater artist.
- *Avenues:* Lorca's lecture and its readers; flamenco and *cante jondo* as examples from Spain; *sentimiento* in talk of *ranchera* and *bolero* singers (Chavela Vargas; Lucha Reyes; José Alfredo Jiménez); "soul" and "feel" in music criticism; research on musical chills and frisson (*check* which studies); critics' and fans' words for a performance that "has it"; the tortured artist (under Creativity's Margins).

### C30. *Firgun*
- *Working definition:* the Israeli Hebrew word (from the Yiddish *farginen*, not to begrudge) for taking generous, unselfish pleasure in another's success and saying so; and its opposite, the German *Schadenfreude*, pleasure at another's misfortune.
- *Why here:* Generosity's facets include generosity of spirit (praising, crediting), Joy's include joy in others' good fortune, and Recognition holds praise; English has no single word for the generous joy *firgun* names, and borrows *Schadenfreude* for its opposite. *Also fits:* Taurus (C27, envy), Aquarius (friendship), Pisces (compassion).
- *What it would add:* how a culture answers another's success (congratulates, envies, cuts down, ignores), which ties Generosity, Pride and Recognition to the envy customs Taurus's C27 describes (the evil eye, the crab in the bucket, the tall poppy).
- *Avenues:* congratulations customs (cards, calls, posts, toasts) and who is expected to give them; research on answering others' good news (Gable and colleagues, 2004, on "capitalization"); Smith, *The Joy of Pain* (2013), on *Schadenfreude*; "crab in a bucket" and *mentalidad de cangrejo* (under Aries's Competition); whether friends say they are glad when a friend succeeds; sayings for envious people and for generous ones; the English word "compersion", coined in recent decades, as a sign of the gap.

### C31. *Philotimo*
- *Working definition:* the Greek "love of honor": a sense of duty, pride and generosity toward one's family, guests and community, felt as one's own honor; often said by Greeks to be untranslatable.
- *Why here:* Honor and Generosity meet in it, and Pride's facets include pride in family and one's people; no English word joins doing right, giving freely and personal honor in this way. *Also fits:* Capricorn (duty), Taurus (C21, hospitality), Cancer (family).
- *What it would add:* honor turned outward into generosity and duty, rather than inward into touchiness or outward into violence: a model of the field of worth (C52) in which honor is shown mainly by what one gives and does for others.
- *Avenues:* Triandis and Vassiliou's studies of *philotimo* in Greece (1972 *check*); Greek American and Greek Canadian communities of the pilot and their festivals (church Greek festivals in Portland and elsewhere; Vancouver's Greek Day *check*); comparable ideals in other traditions (Spanish *pundonor*, the point of honor; Arabic *sharaf*), as examples, not equivalents; how hosts and givers speak of their duty; stories of men who gave or helped because their honor demanded it.

### C32. *Amour-propre* (self-love that needs others' esteem)
- *Working definition:* Rousseau's French term for the self-love that depends on how one stands in others' eyes, set against *amour de soi*, the plain self-love that wants only one's own good (*Discourse on Inequality*, 1755).
- *Why here:* Recognition, Pride, Being seen and Honor all turn on the worth others grant; the research notes give approval ("without real self-worth it grows desperate for approval"), validation and self-worth. *Amour-propre* names the drive beneath them as one thing, which English splits among "vanity", "self-esteem" and "pride". *Also fits:* Aries (C22, *thymos*, spiritedness), Taurus (self-worth), Capricorn (status).
- *What it would add:* a way to ask whether a culture thinks the need for others' esteem is natural and good, a corruption, or both (Rousseau saw it as the root of inequality and of civility alike); and its everyday forms: comparison, keeping up with the Joneses, status anxiety.
- *Avenues:* Rousseau and his readers (Neuhouser, *Rousseau's Theodicy of Self-Love*, 2008); Adam Smith on the love of praise and of being praiseworthy (*The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, 1759); social-comparison research (Festinger, 1954); de Botton, *Status Anxiety* (2004); "keeping up with the Joneses" (the comic strip of 1913 and the phrase); comparison on social media and how people say it makes them feel; *el qué dirán* (under Self-expression's Words).

### C33. *Tarab* (enchantment through music)
- *Working definition:* the Arabic word for the ecstatic transport that music, above all a singer's voice, brings to listeners and performers together, with its shouts of response and its tears.
- *Why here:* Joy's facets include exuberance and shared joy, and its senses lead with music; Creativity and Self-expression hold the making of music, not the listener's rapture. Arabic names that shared rapture as one thing; English must say "being carried away". *Also fits:* Pisces (dissolution and ecstatic states), Sagittarius (worship).
- *What it would add:* the listener's side of art: whether a culture expects audiences to sit still or to cry out, weep and call back to the singer, and how it judges being carried away by music (holy, healthy, dangerous, vulgar).
- *Avenues:* Racy, *Making Music in the Arab World: The Culture and Artistry of Tarab* (2003), as an example from elsewhere; Umm Kulthum's concerts as its classic scene; the Lebanese Mexican and Arab American families of the pilot and the music they keep, as they describe it; the *grito* and the tears of a mariachi or *banda* audience; call and response in Black churches; concert audiences and their rules of silence (under C11); music and chills (C29).

### C34. *Relajo* (playful disorder)
- *Working definition:* the Mexican word for the collective joking and clowning that breaks up seriousness and suspends a value or a rule for a while, studied by Jorge Portilla (*Fenomenología del relajo*, 1966).
- *Why here:* Play's Words give *relajo*, *desmadre* and *echar relajo*, and Joy's give *echar desmadre*; Portilla treats *relajo* as a stance toward value and not only a game. It is in the pilot's language and has no English equal ("horsing around" misses the refusal of seriousness). *Also fits:* Gemini (C27, teasing and banter), Aquarius (nonconformity), Capricorn (order, which it mocks).
- *What it would add:* play as a way of refusing seriousness and authority, apart from games and fun: the class that dissolves into *relajo*, the joke at a solemn moment, the party that turns into *desmadre*, and whether a culture prizes or fears that release.
- *Avenues:* Portilla and his readers; Bartra, *La jaula de la melancolía* (1987), on the myths of Mexican national character, read critically; *relajo* in classrooms, offices, wakes and *velorios*, as people describe it; *cotorreo*, *vacilón* and the *albur* (under Humiliation's Words); comedy that mocks the solemn (Cantinflas; *carpa* humor); teachers' and bosses' views of *relajo*; whether the same thing is called "goofing off" or "team bonding" in English-speaking workplaces.

### C35. *Personalismo* (loyalty to persons)
- *Working definition:* the value, often described in Latin American and Latino life, of dealing through warm personal relationships rather than impersonal rules and roles: trusting and following a person more than an office.
- *Why here:* Leadership's Words give *caudillo*, *cacique* and *el que manda*, and its Beliefs name *caudillismo*; the research notes give leadership "that inspires rather than commands" and warmth. *Personalismo* names the following that leaders (and doctors, priests, bosses and shopkeepers) win by personal ties. *Also fits:* Capricorn (authority), Taurus (C32, connections as wealth, *palanca*), Gemini (C9, sociability).
- *What it would add:* the personal side of leadership and service: why people follow a person and not a party, choose a doctor or a mechanic they know, and expect warmth before business; and its dark side in politics, the strongman followed for himself.
- *Avenues:* studies of *personalismo* in Latino health care and counseling (Añez and colleagues, 2005 *check*); *personalismo* and *caudillismo* in the political history of Mexico; the small talk expected before business; customers loyal to a person rather than a store; politicians' personal brands and followers' attachment to them; whether newcomers find local dealings "too personal" or "too cold", in outsiders' guides.

**Splits and merges of the current concepts**

### C36. Art and the arts (split from creativity)
- *Working definition:* works made to be looked at, listened to, read or performed, and valued for beauty, meaning or skill; and "the arts" as a sphere of life with its own people, places and standing.
- *Why here:* Creativity now holds art beside craft, invention and everyday improvising; art is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs; the research notes give art ("drawn to the arts, theater and anything made to be admired") and taste; the investigation sheet's Leo angles begin with the arts. *Also fits:* Taurus (C24, beauty; C19, craftsmanship), Libra (harmony), Gemini (C11, storytelling).
- *What it would add:* the arts as an institution and a value: whether a people has a separate idea of "art" at all (Creativity's Words ask this of each Indigenous language); fine art against folk art and craft; what art is for (beauty, prayer, protest, decoration, status); who goes to museums and concerts; and whether art is a calling, a hobby or a luxury.
- *Avenues:* cultural-participation surveys (under Creativity's Measures); Shiner, *The Invention of Art* (2001), on the recent birth of "fine art" in Europe; Dissanayake, *Homo Aestheticus* (1992), on art as "making special"; Becker, *Art Worlds* (1982); arts education and how often it is cut; museum attendance and who is absent; the nations' arts as they present them, and their own words for them; "art for art's sake" against art with a purpose; what people hang on their walls at home.

### C37. Invention and innovation (split from creativity)
- *Working definition:* making new tools, methods, products and businesses, and the value a culture puts on the new and on the people who make it.
- *Why here:* Creativity's facets include invention and innovation and everyday improvising; its Beliefs name Silicon Valley's creed of disruption and its Words *el ingenio mexicano* and *hechizo*; the investigation sheet's space and things family names technology and its ideology. *Also fits:* Aries (C8, being first; C26, resourcefulness), Virgo (improvement), Gemini (curiosity).
- *What it would add:* the technical and business side of creativity, which the arts can crowd out: the garage founder as hero, the tinkerer and the inventor, *ingenio* as a national self-image, fear and love of new technology, and who is believed to invent (by gender and origin).
- *Avenues:* science fairs and inventors' clubs (under Creativity's source types); garage founders as legend (Creativity's Space line); Turner, *From Counterculture to Cyberculture* (2006), and "The Californian Ideology" (Barbrook and Cameron, 1995); Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962), on who adopts new things first; Pew's surveys on new technology; *el ingenio mexicano* in jokes and posts about clever fixes; inventors on stamps and in school textbooks; who is shown inventing in films and advertising.

### C38. Emotional expressiveness (split from self-expression)
- *Working definition:* how freely and how strongly feelings are shown (in the face, the voice, tears, touch, words), and who may show which feelings.
- *Why here:* Self-expression now holds speech, dress, art, identity and feeling together; its Feeling and the body cites Rychlowska and colleagues (2015) and Matsumoto's display rules, and its Silences name "men's feelings". The author writes for men, and whether men may show grief, fear, tenderness or joy is one of the live questions of the present, framed here as questions for each group. *Also fits:* Pisces (empathy), Cancer (C18, guardedness and withdrawal), Aries (anger).
- *What it would add:* feeling shown as a concept of its own: "boys don't cry" and *los hombres no lloran*; the songs and places where men may weep (the *ranchera* and the cantina; the stadium; the funeral); expressive and reserved peoples as a question, not a stereotype; and the costs of holding feelings in.
- *Avenues:* display-rule research (Matsumoto); crying across countries (Vingerhoets's International Study on Adult Crying *check*); "normative male alexithymia" (Levant, 1992); men's groups and men's sheds (under Cancer); the *ranchera* and *bolero* as men's music of feeling; fathers' tears at weddings and births, in memoirs; how mourners are expected to behave, by group; therapy use by men (under Cancer C18).

### C39. Persona and the public self (split from self-expression)
- *Working definition:* the self a person shows in public (at work, on stage, online, in office), kept apart from the private self, and the masks and characters people wear on purpose.
- *Why here:* Self-expression's facets end with "restraint and masks", and its Key questions ask whether restraint is a mask or a form of dignity; the research notes give persona ("a public self performed for the world and dropped only with a trusted kindred spirit"). *Also fits:* Gemini (C29, *honne* and *tatemae*), Aries (C2, appearance and self-presentation), Scorpio (secrecy).
- *What it would add:* the deliberate public self as a normal part of life rather than a failure of expression: stage names and characters, the work persona, the politician's image, the wrestler who never breaks character, the separate account for friends; and when a persona is admired, expected or condemned as fake.
- *Avenues:* Goffman's front stage and back stage (*The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959); Paz's "Máscaras mexicanas" (under Self-expression's Beliefs), read as a claim to test; stage names and ring names (*lucha libre* masks, under Humiliation's Ritual); politicians' public images and the moments they slip; "finstas" and second accounts; code-switching at work (under Self-expression's Variation); the customer-service voice; what people say they can be only with their closest friends.

### C40. Group pride (split from pride)
- *Working definition:* pride taken in one's group (a people, a nation, a region, a town, a school, a team, a trade) and shown together in public.
- *Why here:* Pride now holds personal and collective pride together; its facets list group pride and pride as a movement, and its History names "brown pride", Red Power and "Black is beautiful"; Cancer's C20 (homeland and love of country) names Leo for national pride; Aries's C36 splits group identity from personal identity. *Also fits:* Cancer (C20), Aries (C36, personal and group identity), Aquarius (the group).
- *What it would add:* the "we" side of pride, which works differently from personal pride: pride movements as answers to stigma, school and town spirit, national pride and its measures, rivalries between groups' prides, and collective narcissism at the far end.
- *Avenues:* Pride parades and heritage months (under Pride's Ritual); *orgullosamente sonorense* and other regional boasts (under Pride's Words); national-pride survey items (under Pride's Measures); collective narcissism research (under Pride's source types); school spirit and alumni pride; pride in a trade or a union; the flags and colors a group flies; quarrels when one group's pride is felt as an insult by another.

### C41. Status and standing (split from recognition)
- *Working definition:* a person's or family's rank in others' eyes (above, equal or below), and the titles, goods and manners that signal it.
- *Why here:* the file's opening suggests that the author "may prefer to split esteem (praise for achievement) from standing (recognition of status)"; Recognition's facets include formal recognition through titles and honors; the research notes give status ("being seen as high-ranking; spends and positions itself to signal it") and rank ("insists on titles and honorifics"); the near-synonym group status / rank / reputation / public life has no home. *Also fits:* Capricorn (hierarchy and rank), Taurus (C28, class; C40, display of wealth).
- *What it would add:* standing as a field of its own, apart from praise for deeds: status symbols (cars, watches, phones, brands, the right address), titles in speech (*licenciado*, *don*), seating and serving orders, status anxiety, and how people feel about their place on the ladder.
- *Avenues:* titles of address (under Recognition's Words); status symbols in advertising and in what people save for; the MacArthur ladder of subjective social status (Adler and colleagues, 2000); occupational prestige ratings; Ridgeway, *Status* (2019); Weber's status groups; *gente bien*, *fifí* and *naco* (under Taurus C28); who sits where at weddings and banquets; status online (verified badges; follower counts).

### C42. Praise and flattery (split from recognition)
- *Working definition:* words of approval spoken to a person (compliments, praise, encouragement), and their counterfeit, flattery, given to win favor.
