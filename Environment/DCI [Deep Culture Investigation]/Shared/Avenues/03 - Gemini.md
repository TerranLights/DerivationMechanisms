# Avenues of investigation: 03 Gemini (communication and learning)

**What this file is.** For each Gemini concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Gemini concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; it is settled with the author just before Gemini's pass): communication, information, learning, curiosity, connection, duality, siblings, mediation, gossip. Travel is also in Gemini's ten, but its proposed home is Sagittarius, so it is a cross-reference here (end of file); Gemini's traditional area of short journeys is covered in section 0. Questions to settle:
- **Learning and education.** Learning is here; education is in Sagittarius's ten. This file assumes learning covers how people come to know and do things (at home, by watching and pitching in, in early school, at work, all life long), and education covers schooling as an institution and higher learning. Schools sit on the line.
- **Gossip and rumor.** Rumor (from Gemini's own concept table) is researched under gossip here. Gossip is also in four other signs' tables (Aries, Virgo, Scorpio, Pisces).
- **Mediation and diplomacy.** Mediation is in six signs' tables (a Tier 2 concept); diplomacy is in Libra's ten. This file assumes mediation is the third party between two sides (and the go-between in general), and diplomacy is tact and negotiation between the parties themselves, states included.
- **Duality.** In Gemini's material the word means holding two ideas, moods or directions at once. As a concept of human life it needs a working definition; the one in section 6 (living with two-ness: double identities, paired worldviews, contradiction, two faces) is proposed. It touches the near-synonym group deception / lying / duplicity / pretense.
- **Siblings** is a relationship more than an idea. It is researched as how a culture understands the sibling bond, with twins and sibling rivalry inside it.
- **Near neighbors kept apart:** communication and conversation; information and knowledge (and truth, in Sagittarius); connection and friendship, community (Aquarius) and belonging (Cancer).

## 0. The domain: communication and learning

**Traditional areas:** communication, siblings and neighbors, short journeys, early learning. **The domain question:** how do people talk, learn and connect close to home?

- **The culture's style of talk as a whole.** High- and low-context communication (Hall, *Beyond Culture*, 1976); directness against indirectness; politeness systems (Brown and Levinson, *Politeness*, 1987); "communication" itself as a cultural term in American speech (Katriel and Philipsen, 1981; Carbaugh, *Talking American*, 1988); the ethnography of speaking (Hymes; Bauman and Sherzer, eds., *Explorations in the Ethnography of Speaking*, 1974), with local studies as models (Philipsen's "Teamsterville", 1975; Basso on Western Apache silence, "To Give Up on Words", 1970); business-culture guides that place countries on scales of directness and feedback (Meyer, *The Culture Map*, 2014: practitioner, country level only).
- **The place's languages.** Which languages people speak at home, and how well they speak the dominant one (ACS, by county and tract; Statistics Canada's mother tongue, home language and knowledge of official languages; INEGI's speakers of Indigenous languages, by municipality); English varieties (the *Atlas of North American English*, Labov, Ash and Boberg, 2006; the California vowel shift; Canadian raising); northern Mexican Spanish; Chinuk Wawa, the contact language of the Northwest, now taught by the Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde (their dictionary, 2012); Indigenous languages and their revival, as the nations report it (the First Peoples' Cultural Council's reports on the status of B.C. First Nations languages; tribal language programs; Mexico's INALI); sign languages (ASL; Lengua de Señas Mexicana); immigrant languages (Chinese languages, Tagalog, Vietnamese, Korean; Mixtec, Zapotec and Triqui among Indigenous Mexican migrants in California and Oregon).
- **Language in public life.** The English-only movement and those who oppose it (U.S. English, founded 1983 *check*); bilingual-education and dual-language programs, and the public fights over them; Canada's image of itself as a bilingual country; Native-language teaching certificates in Washington and Oregon (*check*); government services in more than one language (forms, ballots, signs, phone lines); hospital and clinic interpreters.
- **Bilingualism and code-switching.** Spanglish and the fights over it (Zentella, *Growing Up Bilingual*, 1997, a New York study, as a model); Chicano English (Fought, *Chicano English in Context*, 2003); attitudes to accents (matched-guise experiments; perceptual dialectology, after Preston: where people believe "correct" or "pleasant" English is spoken); how many generations a language survives (Rumbaut, Massey and Bean, "Linguistic Life Expectancies", 2006, on Southern California); French immersion in British Columbia; dual-language schools (see duality).
- **Neighbors and neighborliness** (an area the concepts don't cover). Knowing neighbors' names; borrowing and favors; block parties and National Night Out; neighborhood watch; Nextdoor and neighborhood Facebook groups; homeowners' associations as neighborhood governments; the *vecindad* (housing around a shared courtyard) and the *colonia*; neighbors made into *compadres*; "the Seattle Freeze" (also in Aries). Measures: Pew on how many neighbors people know, by urban, suburban and rural (2018 *check*); the Current Population Survey's Civic Engagement and Volunteering supplement (talking with neighbors, doing favors for them), by state and metro; the Vancouver Foundation's Connections and Engagement surveys (2012, 2017 *check*); Statistics Canada's General Social Survey on social identity (knowing and trusting neighbors *check*); INEGI's victimization survey (ENVIPE) on neighbors organizing for safety (*check*).
- **Short journeys and local travel** (the part of travel that stays here). Commuting mode, time and distance (ACS journey to work, by county and tract; Statistics Canada's commuting data; INEGI's origin-destination surveys for large metros *check*); walking and cycling (Portland's bicycle culture; Vancouver's seawall); transit (SkyTrain, BART, light rail; Mexico's *combis*, *peseros* and *colectivos*); car culture (Los Angeles freeways; lowriders as mobile art); ferries as everyday travel on the Salish Sea (BC Ferries; Washington State Ferries); daily border crossing (*transfronterizos* who live in Tijuana and study or work in San Diego; the Bureau of Transportation Statistics' monthly crossings by port); errands and the walkable radius (Walk Score); the Sunday drive and the day trip; travel time in the American Time Use Survey; how far children may go alone (studies of children's independent mobility; Hillman, *One False Move*, 1990, in England, as a model).
- **Media habits.** Newspapers, radio, television, public broadcasting (OPB, KQED, KUOW, CBC), social media and messaging apps: see information (section 2).
- **Speech ways** (Fischer's folkway for Gemini):
  - accent and dialect, from the founders' speech on (Fischer traces each founding culture's speech: the Yankee, the Southern, the Quaker plain style, the backcountry); the Spanish of the Californios and *norteño* speech; Yankee and Midland speech carried to Oregon; regional dictionaries (*Dictionary of American Regional English*; *Dictionary of Canadianisms on Historical Principles*; *Diccionario del español de México*);

  - books and libraries in daily life: public library use (the IMLS Public Libraries Survey, by library system); bookstores (Powell's in Portland); books in the home;

  - the full set of avenues is under learning (section 3).
- **Social ways** (runner-up; home sign Aquarius): visiting, hospitality and clubs as the settings where talk happens; see `11 - Aquarius.md`.

## 1. Communication

**Working definition:** the exchange of meaning between people, by speech, writing, gesture, silence and media; and what a culture believes good communication is and what it is for.
**Facets:** face-to-face talk and conversation; small talk; directness and indirectness; listening and silence; writing and letters; public speaking and oratory; the body's signals; communicating at a distance (mail, telephone, messaging); persuasion; communicating across languages (interpreting, translation, children as language brokers); "communication" as an ideal of relationships ("we need to talk").
**Neighbors and opposites:** conversation, dialogue, talk, expression, rapport; silence, withdrawal, miscommunication, the silent treatment. **Excess:** oversharing, talking at people, talk that says nothing. **Deficiency:** stonewalling, not speaking up. Where a culture prizes silence, silence is not a deficiency; that is itself a finding to look for.

- **Words:** "communication", "open communication", "we need to talk", "share" ("thanks for sharing"), "reach out", "touch base", "circle back" (the corporate register), "small talk", "straight talk", "beat around the bush", "passive-aggressive", "a man of few words"; Spanish *comunicación*, *platicar* and *la plática* (Mexican for a chat, and for a talk given to a group), *hablar claro*, *con rodeos*, *echar indirectas* (to drop pointed hints), *albur*, *cantinflear* (to talk at length and say nothing, after the comedian Cantinflas; in the Real Academia's dictionary), *dar el avión* (to pretend to listen *check*), *quedar bien* (to say what keeps the peace); *tú*, *usted*, *ustedes* and *vosotros* (the last absent in Mexico); Chinuk Wawa *wawa*, "talk, speak", in the language's own name; metaphors of talk as a pipe that carries meaning ("getting it across"; Lakoff and Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By*, 1980).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is talking things out the way to solve problems, or does talk make them worse? Talk as action or as cheap ("talk is cheap"; *del dicho al hecho hay mucho trecho*); the plain style (Puritan and Quaker plain speech); therapy culture's belief that "communication" is the key to marriage and health (Cameron, *Good to Talk?*, 2000); free speech as a founding value and a point of pride, and where people draw its limits (insult, offense, hate speech); religious teaching on the tongue (the Epistle of James; the Catholic sins of speech); prayer as speech with God; Indigenous teachings on careful speech and on listening, as the nations state them; philosophy (Habermas on communicative action).
- **Feeling and the body:** eye contact (whether a direct gaze reads as honesty or as disrespect toward elders: a question to research community by community, not a finding); talking with the hands; personal space and touch (Hall's proxemics; contact and noncontact cultures); voice volume, pitch and pace; uptalk and vocal fry; how much people smile at strangers; laughter; the fear of public speaking (Chapman University's Survey of American Fears *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** greetings and small talk with strangers and cashiers ("how are you?" as a greeting, not a question); who talks at family meals; calling against texting; voicemail and email manners; long Mexican goodbyes against leaving without a word; interrupting and overlapping; how meetings run and who speaks in them; giving feedback at work; talking with doctors (interpreters; *promotoras de salud*); children as language brokers for immigrant parents; WhatsApp as the family channel across borders; the languages of signs, menus and forms.
- **Life course:** whether adults talk to babies, and how (Ochs and Schieffelin, 1984, on language socialization); first words; "please" and "thank you"; "use your words"; children "seen and not heard"; speech therapy; show-and-tell, speech and debate; thank-you notes; job interviews; elders' storytelling; telling the dying their diagnosis (see autonomy in Aries); obituaries that praise "a great communicator" or "a man of few words".
- **Ritual:** speeches at weddings, graduations and funerals (*brindis*; eulogies); the sermon; the State of the Union; Mexico's *Grito de Independencia* (the night of September 15), a ritual utterance; the town hall; the potlatch and feast as public witnessing, where guests are paid to witness and remember, as the nations describe it; language days (International Mother Language Day, February 21; the International Decade of Indigenous Languages, 2022-2032).
- **Institutions:** postal services (USPS, Canada Post, Correos de México); public broadcasters; government interpreting and translation services (forms and phone lines in many languages); the interpreting professions (medical and sign-language interpreters); Deaf schools and communities (the California School for the Deaf); school speech and debate programs; Toastmasters International (founded 1924 in Santa Ana, California); churches as schools of public speech (the sermon; the testimony); communication training at work; couples therapy.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for being "articulate", "a good listener", "a great communicator" (and "articulate" as a backhanded compliment to Black speakers, a researched complaint); sanctions on rudeness, swearing and accents (accent bias in hiring and at work); the silent treatment, "ghosting" and blocking as sanctions; tone policing; "mansplaining".
- **Expression:** proverbs (*en boca cerrada no entran moscas*; *el que no habla, Dios no lo oye*; "the squeaky wheel gets the grease"; "silence is golden"); the comedy of talk (Cantinflas; *albures*; stand-up); Richard Rodriguez, *Hunger of Memory* (1982), on trading Spanish at home for public English in Sacramento; films about miscommunication; songs about calls and letters; stories as the nations share them.
- **Exemplars:** orators and "great communicators" (Ronald Reagan's nickname); Chief Seattle's speech, a contested text known through Henry Smith's 1887 version and a 1970s screenplay rewrite; organizers who spoke for others (César Chávez, Dolores Huerta); interpreters (La Malinche, contested; Sacagawea, Lemhi Shoshone); teachers and broadcasters; anti-exemplars: the blowhard, the mumbler, the liar.
- **History:** the founders' speech (see speech ways); Chinuk Wawa as the trade language of the Northwest from the fur trade into the 1900s; the missions' language policy; the Pony Express and the transcontinental telegraph (1860-1861); television's invention in San Francisco (Farnsworth, 1927); the first ARPANET message, from UCLA to the Stanford Research Institute (1969); the suppression of Native languages in boarding and residential schools; Spanish-language media (*La Opinión*, Los Angeles, 1926); smartphones and social media from the Bay Area.
- **Measures:** ACS language spoken at home and English-speaking ability (county and tract); Statistics Canada's home language; INEGI's Indigenous-language speakers; Pew's surveys of Latinos on Spanish use; the American Time Use Survey's "socializing and communicating" and "telephone calls, mail and email" (state, with pooled years); Pew's mobile and texting surveys; INEGI's ENDUTIH and Statistics Canada's Canadian Internet Use Survey; cross-language studies of turn-taking and pause length (Stivers et al., 2009, ten languages); the frequency of "communication" in corpora (Google Books Ngram; the Corpus of Contemporary American English).
- **Variation:** gender (Tannen's "genderlect" thesis and its critics, such as Cameron, *The Myth of Mars and Venus*, 2007); class (Bernstein's restricted and elaborated codes; Heath, *Ways with Words*, 1983, as a model study of three communities); generation; Deaf and hard-of-hearing people; neurodivergent people; region ("the Seattle Freeze"); each Indigenous nation's own norms; immigrant generation.
- **Relations:** gossip, mediation and connection (this sign); self-assertion (Aries); self-expression (Leo); diplomacy (Libra); secrecy (Scorpio); honesty and truth (Sagittarius); empathy (Pisces).
- **Who it applies to:** animals (talking to pets; the distinct call dialects of the Southern Resident orcas of the Salish Sea); plants; ancestors and the dead (prayer; Día de Muertos altars); companies ("brand voice"; corporate speak); machines (present-day attitudes to voice assistants and chatbots; Pew surveys on AI use *check*).
- **Silences:** what families don't talk about (money, sex, death, mental illness, immigration status, the residential school years); people shut out of public talk by language or deafness; sexuality left unspoken in families and at work.
- **Change:** texting replacing calls; video calls and remote work; emoji; AI-written messages; the decline of letters; polarization ("we can't talk anymore"; depolarizing workshops such as Braver Angels); Indigenous-language revival.
- **The senses:** the soundscape of talk: how loud conversation runs in public (restaurants, buses, phone calls on speaker), and which places call for hushed voices (libraries, churches, hospitals, the quiet car on trains); voices heard as warm, cold, educated, rural or foreign (accent, pitch, pace), and the "phone voice" and "customer-service voice" people put on; silence as part of talk (Basso, under section 0), and how long a silence may last before someone fills it; touch while talking (a hand on the arm; the cheek kiss in greeting; the handshake; or no touch at all), and who may touch whom; food and drink as the setting of talk (talking over coffee; the *sobremesa*, lingering at the table after a meal; the business lunch; "let's grab a beer"); the feel of handwriting and paper (a handwritten letter against a typed one; perfumed love letters); ringtones and notification sounds as the rhythm of a connected day; communication beyond hearing: sign languages, lip-reading, and the touch-based (protactile) communication of DeafBlind people, which grew up in Seattle's DeafBlind community *check*.
- **Space, place and things:** where talk happens: the kitchen table, the porch and the stoop, the plaza, the café, the bar, the barbershop, the church steps, the break room; the car, where people talk side by side without facing each other; seating and talk (round tables against rows; the head of the table; circles); conversational distance (Hall's proxemics, under Feeling and the body); the objects of communication and their histories: the letter, the postcard, the telephone on the kitchen wall with its long cord, the phone booth, the cell phone, the truckers' CB radio, the bulletin board, the mailbox at the end of a rural road; the post office as a public place, and what a small town loses when it closes; signs and the languages on them (the linguistic landscape: Landry and Bourhis, 1997), such as Chinese on street signs in Vancouver's Chinatown and Squamish and Musqueam names on Vancouver signs *check*; open-plan offices with their phone booths and quiet rooms; the pulpit, the lectern and the microphone; talking pieces passed around a circle, as the nations share them.
- **Time and rhythm:** when one may call (not too early or too late; not at mealtimes; not on Sunday morning), and when only a text is polite; how soon a message must be answered (a letter, an email, a text; being "left on read"); how long greetings and leave-takings last (the long goodbye at the door; leaving a party without a word), measured, not assumed; the length of pauses between turns (Stivers et al., under Measures); the weekly call home (the Sunday call to a mother), and the night and weekend rates that once set when people called long distance; holiday cards and the annual family letter; how long speeches and sermons may run, and the seasons of public speaking (graduations, weddings, elections); the pace of talk itself (fast and slow speakers, by region, as heard and as measured); time zones and calls across borders and oceans; the fading evening phone call and the rise of all-day messaging.
- **The economy of it:** what people pay to communicate (phone and data plans; the calling cards once sold for calls home; postage); the phone companies and their place in public life (Telmex and Telcel in Mexico; complaints about Canada's few large carriers and their prices *check*); call centers, including those in Mexico that answer U.S. customers in English *check*; the interpreting and translation trades and their pay; communication coaching, public-speaking courses and accent-reduction services; greeting cards and stationery; advertising as paid communication; jobs that are mostly talk (sales, customer service, teaching, therapy, radio) and how they are paid; the earnings gap for people who speak the dominant language poorly, as economists measure it *check*; free messaging apps, paid for by data and advertising; extra pay for bilingual workers (cross-reference: duality).
- **Conflict and crisis:** quarrels that start as misunderstanding (between partners, neighbors, coworkers), and the words for it ("you're not listening"; *un malentendido*); the shouting match, the slammed door and the silent treatment as ways a quarrel is carried on; talk across languages failing in emergencies (no interpreter in the emergency room; warnings issued in one language only during fires and floods *check*); communication when the systems fail: phone lines down after earthquakes (Loma Prieta, 1989; the El Mayor-Cucapah earthquake in Baja California, 2010 *check*), with battery radios and word of mouth in their place; wildfire evacuation alerts that came late (the 2018 Camp Fire in Paradise, as survivors tell it *check*); the COVID-19 pandemic: masks that muffled speech and hid the face (hard for deaf people who lip-read), clear-window masks, video calls in place of visits and funerals; families and friends who stopped speaking over politics, and the workshops set up to get them talking again (under Change).
- **Margins and extremes:** the limits of speech: people who cannot speak or hear, and whether a culture treats this as a lack or as a difference (Deaf people's view of themselves as a language community); stuttering, aphasia and selective mutism, and whether each is treated as illness, as nerves or as character; the compulsive talker, and the person who speaks to no one; vows and retreats of silence (monastic orders; silent meditation retreats; the shared silence of a Quaker meeting); speaking in tongues in Pentecostal worship, as the churches describe it (the Azusa Street revival, Los Angeles, 1906); the private jargon of high-control groups, and the new words they give members; children's secret languages (Pig Latin; the Spanish *jerigonza* *check*); words a community treats as beyond the pale (slurs; threats), and how it shuns those who use them; machine talk at the edge of human talk (present-day attitudes to people who would rather talk to chatbots and voice assistants than to people).

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):

## 2. Information

**Working definition:** what is known or reported about the world, and how a culture gathers, passes on, trusts, withholds and uses it: news, facts, data, records.

## 4. Curiosity

**Working definition:** the wish to know, to find out and to explore; and how far a culture encourages, channels, limits or punishes it.
**Facets:** curiosity about ideas and how things work; curiosity about other people (interest, and nosiness); curiosity about places (exploring); scientific inquiry; children's questions; morbid curiosity (rubbernecking, true crime); curiosity about other peoples (anthropology, tourism) and its limits; the questions one may and may not ask.
**Neighbors and opposites:** inquisitiveness, interest, wonder, nosiness, prying; indifference, incuriosity, boredom, closed-mindedness. **Excess:** prying, snooping, morbidness, never settling on anything. **Deficiency:** apathy, incuriosity.

## 5. Connection

**Working definition:** the ties that link people to one another, and the work of making, using and keeping them: keeping in touch, introducing, networking, and the felt sense of being connected (or not).
**Facets:** social connection and its absence (loneliness, isolation); strong and weak ties (Granovetter, 1973); networks for getting things done (jobs, favors, permits); keeping in touch at a distance (calls, visits, remittances, letters); connecting others (introductions, matchmaking, brokering); ritual kinship (*compadrazgo*); digital connection; connection to place, land and the dead; connecting ideas.
**Neighbors and opposites:** ties, network, relationship, rapport, bond, contact; isolation, loneliness, disconnection, alienation. **Excess:** cronyism, nepotism, *amiguismo*, being always online. **Deficiency:** isolation, loneliness. (Belonging is Cancer's; friendship and community are Aquarius's; connection here is the links themselves and the act of linking.)

- **Words:** "connection", "connected", "networking", "keep in touch", "reach out", "plugged in", "it's who you know", "an in", "hook someone up", "six degrees of separation", "lonely", "touch grass" (online slang for getting offline); Spanish *conexión*, *contactos*, *palanca* and *tener palanca* (Mexican: a lever, a connection that gets things done), *compadrazgo*, *compadre* and *comadre*, *cuate* (Mexican: buddy, and also twin, from Nahuatl), *recomendado*, *amiguismo*, *el que tiene padrino se bautiza* (whoever has a godfather gets baptized: connections decide), *hueso* (a government job got through connections *check*), *mantenerse en contacto*.

## 6. Duality

**Working definition** (proposed; see the header): how a culture lives with two-ness: holding two identities, languages, loyalties or views at once; dividing the world into pairs; and how it treats people who show two faces.
**Facets:** double identity and biculturalism (*ni de aquí ni de allá*); double consciousness (Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, 1903); paired worldviews (body and soul, good and evil, sacred and profane, nature and culture), and whether the pairs are opposed or complementary; tolerating contradiction and mixed feelings; double lives and two faces (hypocrisy; *doble moral*); dual roles (the work self and the home self); dual loyalty and belonging to two countries; the border as a pair (twin cities); twins (see siblings).
**Neighbors and opposites:** ambivalence, contradiction, paradox, hybridity, *mestizaje*, two-facedness, hypocrisy; unity, wholeness, integrity (in its root sense of being one), consistency. **Excess:** two-facedness, duplicity, a split self. **Deficiency:** rigidity; black-and-white thinking; being unable to hold two views or two belongings.

- **Words:** "two-faced", "double life", "double standard", "both-and", "mixed feelings", "on the fence", "Jekyll and Hyde", "split personality" (a popular misuse), "hyphenated American", "code-switching", "talking out of both sides of one's mouth", "flip-flopper"; "Two-Spirit", a pan-Indigenous English term adopted in 1990 at a gathering in Winnipeg (whether its "two" means duality is for Two-Spirit people and each nation to say, not outsiders); Spanish *dualidad*, *doble cara*, *de dos caras*, *doble moral* (double standard; common in Mexican public debate), *hipócrita*, *ni de aquí ni de allá*, *mestizaje*, *nepantla* (Nahuatl for "in the middle", recorded by the friar Diego Durán in the 1500s and taken up by Anzaldúa), *la máscara* (Paz, "Máscaras mexicanas", in *El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950), *agridulce*, *cuates* (twins).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is two-ness a richness (biculturalism as an advantage; "two-eyed seeing"; Anzaldúa's *mestiza* consciousness) or a betrayal (*vendido*, *malinchista*; "no one can serve two masters", Matthew 6:24)? Opposed pairs (good against evil) against complementary pairs (Nahua thought on Ometeotl, the dual principle, as León-Portilla described it in *La filosofía náhuatl*, 1956; yin and yang in Chinese-American communities and in the Bay Area counterculture, as with Alan Watts in Sausalito); consistency as a virtue against tolerance of contradiction (Peng and Nisbett, 1999, on dialectical thinking); Christian body and soul; the mask as protection (Paz).
- **Feeling and the body:** mixed emotions, and whether people feel two at once (cross-cultural studies such as Miyamoto, Uchida and Ellsworth, 2010 *check*); bicultural stress; feeling "torn"; the face kept for others against the feeling behind it; dressing for two worlds (work clothes and regalia).
- **Everyday practice:** switching languages and registers between home and work; a work self and a home self; children living between two homes after divorce; dual citizens with two passports; daily border crossers; snowbirds who spend half the year in Baja California Sur (Los Cabos, La Paz, Todos Santos); second jobs and side hustles; pen names, online alter egos and second accounts ("finstas"); the masked *luchador*'s public second self.
- **Life course:** bicultural childhoods; losing and reclaiming a heritage language; adolescent identity splits; children of divorce; adults reconnecting with an Indigenous identity, as Sixties Scoop survivors and adoptees describe it; elders returning to the home country.
- **Ritual:** border gatherings (Friendship Park, *Parque de la Amistad*, at Tijuana and San Diego, where families met through the fence); Día de Muertos (the living and the dead side by side); carnival as the world turned upside down (Ensenada; La Paz, *check* its age); weddings that join two families; Halloween costumes as a night's second self; masked dances, as their communities present them.
- **Institutions:** dual-language schools and California's Seal of Biliteracy (2011, the first state seal); multiracial reporting in censuses (the U.S. since 2000; INEGI's Afro-Mexican self-identification, 2015 and 2020); hometown associations and consulates that keep migrants tied to two countries (see connection); cross-border art, music and community projects in the twin cities of the border; the Border Industrialization Program (1965) and its "twin plants"; lucha libre and the mask as a guarded second identity; Two-Spirit societies and gatherings, as their members describe them; workplaces that ask for one self at work and allow another at home.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise and pay for bilingual skill; the Seal of Biliteracy; sanctions on the two-faced and the hypocrite; taunts for people seen as one thing outside and another inside (*vendido*, *pocho*, and food-color insults, described, not repeated); suspicion of dual loyalty (the internment); politicians punished as flip-floppers; bicultural people rejected by both sides.
- **Expression:** Anzaldúa, *Borderlands/La Frontera* (1987); Paz on masks; John Okada, *No-No Boy* (1957, Seattle); Le Guin, *The Left Hand of Darkness* (1969); Los Tigres del Norte's "Jaula de oro" (1980s *check*), on a migrant caught between countries; the film *Ni de aquí ni de allá* (La India María, 1988 *check*); Zorro (Johnston McCulley, 1919), the Californio gentleman with a masked second self; *Twin Peaks* (1990, set in Washington), full of doubles; lucha libre's masks; border art; Two-Spirit writing, as its authors share it.
- **Exemplars:** La Malinche (translator, traitor, mother of *mestizaje*: the classic contested figure); the No-No Boys; Pauline Johnson (Tekahionwake), the Mohawk and English poet buried in Vancouver's Stanley Park, who performed half her recitals in Native dress and half in an evening gown (*check*); Richard Rodriguez; Anzaldúa; masked heroes (Zorro; El Santo); politicians accused of two faces; Jekyll and Hyde.
- **History:** the Spanish colonial caste system and *mestizaje*; 1848, which made Californios a people with two countries ("we didn't cross the border; the border crossed us"); internment and the 1943 "loyalty questionnaire", whose questions 27 and 28 made the "No-No Boys"; nations divided by borders, each in its own words (the Cocopah, or Cucapá, a Yuman people, on both sides of the U.S.-Mexico line; Coast Salish and Syilx nations split by the 49th parallel); the twin-plant program.

- **Variation:** immigrant generation (first, "1.5", second); mixed-race people; borderlands against the interior; religious traditions (opposed or complementary pairs); each nation divided by a border; political camps (how each punishes a changed mind).
- **Relations:** connection and mediation (bridging two sides) and siblings (twins) (this sign); identity (Aries); balance (Libra); deception, secrecy and betrayal (Scorpio); honesty (Sagittarius); boundary and dissolution (Pisces).
- **Who it applies to:** twins; places (twin cities across borders: San Diego and Tijuana, Calexico and Mexicali, the two Nogales); companies with two faces; tricksters who are two things at once, as the nations share their stories; machines that pass for human (present-day attitudes to bots, deepfakes and AI voices).
- **Silences:** double lives (closeted lives; hidden immigration status); what bicultural people hide from each side; hidden mixed ancestry and "passing"; institutions' *doble moral* on sex, drink and money.
- **Change:** more people reporting more than one race; dual-language schools spreading; online alter egos; deepfakes and AI impersonation; a harder border making two-country lives harder; remote workers living in two countries (Baja California Sur; Mexico City).
- **The senses:** tastes of two worlds: fusion foods born in the pilot (the Mission burrito of San Francisco; Korean tacos from Los Angeles food trucks, 2008; the California roll, claimed by both Vancouver and Los Angeles *check*), fruit with chile and lime, sweet and sour together; two musics heard in one place (bilingual radio; *banda* beside hip hop; songs that switch languages); an accent that shows a second language, heard with pride or with shame; masks that hide the face's feelings (Paz, under Words); two languages heard in one household (parents speaking one, children answering in the other); the body's sense of being "torn" or "split", in bicultural people's own words.
- **Space, place and things:** border places: twin cities (San Diego and Tijuana; Calexico and Mexicali), the fence, the line at the crossing, the pedestrian bridge; two homes: children of divorce with a bedroom in each house; snowbirds and retirees with a house in each country; migrants who build a house in the home village they may never live in *check*; the home altar beside the television, the saints beside the family photographs; objects of two worlds: bilingual signs, two sets of clothes (work clothes and regalia; the business suit and the *charro* suit), masks, the *luchador*'s mask; the hyphen on forms and the box marked "two or more races"; the borderland landscape read as evidence (factories along the Mexican side; outlet malls along the U.S. side).
- **Time and rhythm:** two calendars kept at once (the church year and the civic year; the Lunar New Year beside January 1; the nations' seasonal calendars beside the school year); two-ness by season: snowbirds half the year in each place, farmworkers who follow harvests between countries; the weekday self and the weekend self; day lives and night lives; switching between ways of keeping time between home and work (Hall's one-thing-at-a-time and many-at-once, *The Dance of Life*, 1983); two-ness across a life: the bicultural childhood, the teenager who rejects one side, the adult who reclaims it, the elder who goes "home".
- **The economy of it:** pay for being bilingual, and the jobs that require it (call centers, hospitals, schools); cross-border economies: the twin-plant factories (*maquiladoras*), shoppers who cross to buy, Americans who cross to Tijuana, Mexicali and Los Algodones for dentists and medicines, retirees in Baja; side hustles and second jobs as two working selves; marketing to bicultural customers (Spanish-language advertising; ads that switch languages); masks and costumes as a trade (lucha libre masks; Halloween); remittances that bind two economies (under Connection).
- **Conflict and crisis:** loyalty put to the test: internment and the loyalty questionnaire (under History); moments that ask bicultural people to pick a side (a Mexico-U.S. soccer match; the anthem booed or cheered; "go back where you came from"); families split by a border, and whether they visit, call or drift apart; hypocrisy exposed: preachers and politicians caught living double lives, and how their communities respond; a double life discovered by a spouse or children (a second family; a hidden past), and the crisis that follows; the 2020 closure of the land borders to non-essential crossings, which cut two-country lives for months *check*; inner conflict named as illness or as growth (bicultural stress; Anzaldúa's *nepantla*, under Words).
- **Margins and extremes:** the split self as a medical category (dissociative identity disorder, and the popular misuse of "split personality", under Words); impostors and con artists who keep two lives going for years; spies and double agents (cross-reference: Scorpio, betrayal); people who live entirely as two persons (the masked wrestler never seen unmasked); purity movements that reject all mixing (of languages, peoples or faiths), and language purists who condemn Spanglish; people rejected by both sides ("too Mexican for the Americans, too American for the Mexicans"); at one extreme, worldviews that split everything into absolute good and absolute evil; at the other, those that dissolve every opposite.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* paired words and ideas in the language (day and night, sky and earth, male and female, right and left, raw and cooked), and whether the pairs are spoken of as enemies or partners; beliefs about twins and twin stories, as each people shares them; tricksters who are two things at once, as the nations share their stories; masks and masked dances, as their communities present them; transformation stories, as their owners share them; beliefs about doubles, shadows and second souls; speaking two languages, and switching between them; dress for two settings (everyday and ceremonial; work and home); carnival and the world turned upside down; societies divided into two halves that marry and serve each other (moieties), as each people describes its own; proverbs on two-faced people and on serving two masters; riddles of things that are two at once; words for mixed feelings (bittersweet, *agridulce*).
  - *Writing:* memoirs of living between two cultures; immigrant and border literature; fiction of doubles, masked heroes and double lives; philosophy and religious teaching on dualism (body and soul, good and evil, spirit and flesh); letters written in two languages; bilingual dictionaries and books printed in two languages; diaries kept in a second language; writing under pen names.

  - *Mass media:* bilingual and border media; films and series about doubles, twins and double lives; telenovelas of switched identities; masked wrestling on television; comedy built on code-switching and on being caught between cultures; advertising aimed at bicultural audiences.

## 7. Siblings

**Working definition:** brothers and sisters, and how a culture understands the sibling bond: who counts as a sibling, what siblings owe one another, and how they rank, compete and care.
**Facets:** birth order and seniority (the eldest's role; the youngest's); older siblings caring for younger ones; sibling rivalry; twins; half-, step- and adopted siblings; cousins counted as siblings in some kinship systems; brothers and sisters (protection, reputation, gender); siblings and inheritance; siblings in the care of aging parents; adult closeness and estrangement; siblings as co-workers and co-migrants; "brother" and "sister" as words for non-kin.
**Neighbors and opposites:** brotherhood, sisterhood, kin, fraternity, solidarity; the only child, estrangement, rivalry. **Excess:** clannishness, favoritism, feuds, enmeshment. **Deficiency:** estrangement, neglect, siblings as strangers.

## 8. Mediation

**Working definition:** a third party helping two or more sides settle a conflict or reach an agreement without imposing a decision; and, more broadly, the go-between who stands between parties (brokers, interpreters, intercessors).
**Facets:** formal mediation (labor, family, community, commerce); informal go-betweens (elders, clergy, *compadres*, friends, a sibling); intercession (saints, the Virgin, clergy between people and God); peacemaking and restorative circles; cultural brokers and interpreters; paid brokers and fixers; mediation between nations; online dispute resolution; children caught in the middle.
**Neighbors and opposites:** arbitration, conciliation, negotiation, reconciliation, intercession, brokering, peacemaking, diplomacy (Libra); feud, taking sides, escalation, imposed judgment. **Excess:** meddling; "triangulation" (a family-therapy term); brokers who profit from both sides; collusion. **Deficiency:** no one to go between; conflicts that escalate or are buried.

## 9. Gossip

**Working definition:** talk about absent people, especially their private lives and conduct; rumor (unconfirmed reports passed from person to person) is researched here too.
**Facets:** everyday gossip (bonding, entertainment, news of the community); gossip as social control (enforcing norms); malicious gossip, slander and character assassination; rumor and urban legend; rumor in crises (war, riots, disasters); celebrity gossip and tabloids; workplace gossip; online gossip (call-outs, anonymous apps, review sites); "whisper networks" that warn of dangerous people; *el qué dirán*.
**Neighbors and opposites:** rumor, hearsay, scuttlebutt, chatter, scandal, slander, word of mouth, "tea"; discretion, confidentiality, minding one's own business. **Excess:** slander, harassment, mobbing, ruined reputations. **Deficiency:** being cut off from what everyone knows; a silence that protects wrongdoers.

- **Words:** "gossip" (from Old English *godsibb*, a godparent, then a woman's friends at a birth, then idle talk), "rumor", "hearsay", "scuttlebutt", "the grapevine", "dish", "spill the tea" and "tea" (from Black drag culture), "the rumor mill", "busybody", "gossipmonger", "talk of the town", "word on the street", "shoot the breeze" (how men may name their own gossip); Spanish *chisme*, *chismoso*, *chismear*, *comadrear* and *comadreo* (to gossip, from *comadre*: the same path from co-parent to gossip as in English), *mitote* and *mitotero* (Mexican, from a Nahuatl word for a dance: a ruckus, gossip), *argüende* and *argüendero* (Mexican: fuss and gossip), *radio pasillo* (the office grapevine *check*), *soltar un borrego* (Mexican: to start a false rumor *check*), *corre la bola* (word is going around *check*), *el qué dirán* (what people will say: fear of gossip as a social force), *lengua larga*, *lengua de víbora*, *dimes y diretes*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is gossip a sin (the Catholic sins of detraction and calumny; Jewish teaching on *lashon hara*; Proverbs on the whisperer who separates friends), a weakness (the stereotype that women gossip, to be tested, not adopted), the weapon of the weak (Scott, *Weapons of the Weak*, 1985, a Malaysian study, as a model), or the glue and police of community life (Gluckman, "Gossip and Scandal", 1963; Dunbar, *Grooming, Gossip and the Evolution of Language*, 1996)? "If you can't say anything nice..."; *el qué dirán* as a force that keeps people in line; teachings on speaking of others, as the nations state them.
- **Feeling and the body:** the pleasure of a secret shared; lowered voices, leaning in, looking around; the shame and anxiety of being talked about; "my ears are burning"; blushing; the cold shoulder that follows a rumor.
- **Everyday practice:** talk at the hair salon, barbershop, laundromat, church steps, market (*tianguis*), office kitchen, and the shared washing place of the *vecindad*; group chats; Nextdoor and neighborhood Facebook groups; anonymous apps (Yik Yak) and city-by-city "Are We Dating the Same Guy?" groups (*check*); review sites (Yelp, San Francisco, 2004); celebrity media (TMZ, Los Angeles, 2005; Mexico's *TVNotas*); gossip television (*Ventaneando*, TV Azteca, since 1996; *El Gordo y la Flaca*, Univision *check*); Hollywood's gossip columns.

### C1. Neighbors and neighborliness
- *Working definition:* the people who live nearby, and what a culture expects of them: knowing them, greeting them, helping them, putting up with them, and leaving them alone.
- *Why here:* neighbors are one of the sign's traditional areas, now one bullet in section 0, marked there as an area the concepts don't cover; the research notes give neighbors ("neighbors and familiar faces in the local world"); the investigation sheet's angles for this sign name neighbors and their complaints. *Also fits:* Aquarius (community), Cancer (home), Libra (quarrels between neighbors).
- *What it would add:* the nearest ring of people outside the family, with its own duties (the borrowed cup of sugar, the casserole for the bereaved, an eye kept on an empty house) and its own frictions (noise, fences, parking, pets, trees), which neither Connection nor Aquarius's community holds whole.

### C2. Short journeys and getting around
- *Working definition:* the everyday trips of ordinary life (to work, to school, to the store, to visit kin), how people make them, and what they mean.
- *Why here:* short journeys are one of the sign's traditional areas; section 0 holds them in one bullet, and travel itself has its proposed home in Sagittarius (the cross-reference at the end of this file). ### C30. *Sobremesa*
- *Working definition:* the Spanish word for the time spent talking at the table after a meal is over.
- *Why here:* it names a custom of unhurried talk (under Communication's senses) that English has no single word for; the research notes give conversation and nourishment ("what feeds us is conversation and information"). *Also fits:* Taurus (C3, food and eating), Cancer (family), Aquarius (friendship).
- *What it would add:* the meal as a frame for talk: how long people stay, who leaves first, and what the custom's survival or decline says about time and family.
- *Avenues:* time spent eating and talking at meals (ATUS, under Taurus); the Mexican mid-afternoon *comida* and its *sobremesa*; family dinners in surveys; coffee after the meal; restaurants that rush diners or let them linger (the check brought unasked, or only when asked); *sobremesa* in memoirs and films.

### C31. The palaver
- *Working definition:* the long public talk in which a community talks a matter through until it reaches agreement, named in West African French *palabre* and in the "palaver tree" (*arbre à palabres*) under which it is held *check*.
- *Why here:* it joins Communication and Mediation: talk itself as the way to settle a matter, with no single third party. "Palaver" passed into English as "idle talk", a telling loss of meaning. *Also fits:* Libra (consensus; settling quarrels), Aquarius (community).
- *What it would add:* consensus by talk as a cultural form: how long talk must go on, who may speak, and whether agreement must be unanimous.
- *Avenues:* the palaver in West African life and writing, as studied; the Quaker meeting for business ("the sense of the meeting"); town halls (under section 0); circles and consensus practices, as the nations describe them; village assemblies in rural Mexico, as studied; consensus in co-ops and activist groups.

### C32. *Ta'arof*
- *Working definition:* the Persian art of ritual courtesy, in which offers are made and refused, often more than once, before anything is accepted, and in which both sides know the words are not to be taken at face value.
- *Why here:* a named system of politeness (C6) in which what is said and what is meant are known to differ; Persian-speaking communities live in the pilot (Los Angeles; North Vancouver *check*). *Also fits:* Libra (diplomacy), Taurus (C21, hospitality).
- *What it would add:* ritual refusal as a cultural script: offers that must first be refused, payment that must be pushed away, and the confusion of outsiders who take the words literally.
- *Avenues:* *ta'arof* in Iranian American and Iranian Canadian life, as they describe it; ritual refusals in other traditions (refusing food once or twice before accepting; the fight over the bill, under Aries); the Mexican *es suyo* said of an object a guest admires (under Taurus, possession); studies of offers and refusals in pragmatics.

### C33. *Jeong*
- *Working definition:* the Korean word for the deep, lasting attachment that grows between people (and toward places and things) from long time together, whether or not they like each other *check*.
- *Why here:* it names a kind of connection made by shared time rather than by choice, which Connection's working definition (the ties, and the work of making and keeping them) does not name. *Also fits:* Cancer (belonging), Aquarius (friendship), Taurus (C18, loyalty).
- *What it would add:* bonds that grow without being chosen (old neighbors, coworkers, classmates, even rivals), and the obligations they carry.
- *Avenues:* *jeong* in Korean American and Korean Canadian life (Los Angeles's Koreatown; Vancouver and Coquitlam), as they describe it; long-time neighbors and the bond of "having been through things together"; the *cariño* people keep for those they grew up with; old-timers' clubs and reunions; studies of familiarity and liking (the "mere exposure" effect).

### C34. *Bildung*
- *Working definition:* the German ideal of forming oneself through learning, reading and culture, as lifelong work on the whole person.
- *Why here:* Learning's Beliefs line asks what learning is for; *Bildung* answers "to form the self", as Mexican *educación* answers "to be well brought up" (under Learning's Words). *Also fits:* Sagittarius (education, meaning), Leo (self-expression), Aries (C14, self-discovery).
- *What it would add:* learning as self-cultivation rather than a credential or a skill: the reader of the "great books", the worker at night school, the self-taught intellectual.
- *Avenues:* the "great books" movement and adult reading groups; Chautauqua assemblies and university extension as American forms of self-cultivation *check*; lifelong-learning programs for retirees (under Curiosity's Life course); Vasconcelos's cheap editions of the classics for the people (1920s *check*); workers' education run by unions; *Bildung* among German-speaking immigrants, as studied.

**Splits of the current concepts**

### C35. Conversation and small talk (split from communication)
- *Working definition:* talk for its own sake between people face to face: chatting, visiting, small talk with strangers, and long talks between friends.
- *Why here:* the questions at the top of this file keep communication and conversation apart as near neighbors; the research notes give conversation ("any exchange made better"); Communication's facets include face-to-face talk and small talk. *Also fits:* Aquarius (friendship), Leo (play).
- *What it would add:* the art of conversation as a value (being good company; *platicar*, under Communication's Words), apart from communication as the passing of meaning.
- *Avenues:* small talk with cashiers and strangers (under Communication's Everyday practice); the safe topics of small talk (weather, sports, traffic) and the barred ones; *platicar* and *la plática*; the visit (*la visita*) as a social form; talk in cafés and bars; studies of small talk and well-being (C17); the "lost art of conversation" as a complaint every generation makes.

### C36. Translation and interpreting (split from communication and mediation)
- *Working definition:* carrying meaning between languages, in speech and in writing, and the standing of those who do it.
- *Why here:* the investigation sheet's angles for this sign name interpreters, including children who interpret for their parents; the research notes give translation ("translator and ambassador among the speech professions"); Communication's facets include communicating across languages, and Mediation's include cultural brokers and interpreters. *Also fits:* Libra (diplomacy).
- *What it would add:* the go-between of languages as a figure, trusted or suspected (the Italian saying *traduttore, traditore*, "translator, traitor"), from La Malinche to the child at the clinic counter.
- *Avenues:* children who interpret for their parents (Orellana, *Translating Childhoods*, 2009, on Los Angeles and Chicago families *check*); medical interpreters (under Communication's Institutions); sign-language interpreters; Bible translation into Indigenous languages, and each nation's view of it; machine translation, and what people think of it; subtitles and dubbing (under section 0); interpreting in Chinuk Wawa at the councils of the 1850s, and the dispute over what was understood (under Mediation's History); translators' pay and standing.

### C37. News (split from information)
- *Working definition:* reports of what has just happened, and the customs of telling, getting and trusting them.
- *Why here:* Information's facets begin with news and the press; the research notes give journalism and transmission ("the messenger rather than the scribe"). *Also fits:* Sagittarius (truth), Scorpio (secrecy).
- *What it would add:* the news as a daily ritual and a shared good, apart from information in general (data, records, knowledge): who brings news, how good and bad news are announced, and what counts as news at all.
- *Avenues:* how bad news is broken to a family (a death, an illness); "don't shoot the messenger"; news as ritual (under Information's Ritual); local papers and news deserts (under Information's Measures); the *corrido* as a news ballad (under Information's Expression); "no news is good news"; news avoidance (Reuters, under Information's Feeling and the body); what makes a story news in a small town and in a city.

### C38. Rumor and misinformation at scale (split from gossip and information)
- *Working definition:* unconfirmed and false reports that spread through a whole population: urban legends, panics, conspiracy theories, hoaxes, false alarms.
- *Why here:* the investigation sheet's angles for this sign name rumor and misinformation; the research notes' gaps find "no collective scale ... nothing on rumor at scale, crowds, cascades"; Gossip now holds rumor, and Information holds misinformation. *Also fits:* Sagittarius (belief; conspiracy belief), Pisces (fear).
- *What it would add:* the crowd's rumor as a topic apart from gossip about a person: how a false story spreads, who believes it, and what it does in a crisis.
- *Avenues:* rumor research (Allport and Postman; Shibutani, under Gossip's History); urban legends (Brunvand, *The Vanishing Hitchhiker*, 1981); scares that spread without evidence, such as the fear of poisoned Halloween candy (Best and Horiuchi, 1985 *check*); misinformation in Spanish and on WhatsApp (under Information's Variation); chain messages; rumor in disasters (under Gossip's History); rumors that led mobs to kill (under Gossip's Conflict and crisis); surveys of conspiracy belief.

### C39. Apprenticeship (split from learning)
- *Working definition:* learning a craft or trade by working beside a master for years, and the bond between master and apprentice.
- *Why here:* Learning's facets include learning a trade; section 0's learning ways name apprenticeship and family trades. *Also fits:* Virgo (craft and work), Capricorn (hierarchy), Taurus (C19, craftsmanship).
- *What it would add:* the oldest form of teaching as a cultural institution: its hardships, its secrets, its rites (the first tool; the piece that proves mastery), and how it survives beside schooling.
- *Avenues:* union and trade apprenticeships (under Learning's Institutions); family trades handed from parent to child (the *taller*; the family restaurant); apprentices in carving, weaving and other arts, as the nations describe their teaching; Lave and Wenger, *Situated Learning* (1991), whose cases include Yucatec Maya midwives and tailors in Liberia *check*; "paying your dues"; the decline of shop class.

### C40. Exploration (split from curiosity)
- *Working definition:* curiosity about places: going to see what is there, off the trail, over the hill, into the cave.
- *Why here:* Curiosity's facets include curiosity about places (exploring); the research notes give adventure and movement. *Also fits:* Sagittarius (travel, long journeys), Aries (C8, being first).
- *What it would add:* exploring close to home, which travel (Sagittarius) does not hold: children's roaming, back roads, local hiking and caving, urban exploring, and the explorer seen as hero or as trespasser.
- *Avenues:* children's roaming range (under section 0's short journeys); hiking, caving and tide-pooling; explorers as heroes, and the peoples who were already there (under Curiosity's History); urban exploration (under Curiosity's Margins and extremes); "off the beaten path" in travel advertising; geocaching, whose first cache was hidden in Oregon in 2000 *check*.

### C41. Keeping in touch (split from connection)
- *Working definition:* keeping a tie alive across distance and time: calls, letters, visits, messages, cards, gifts and money sent home.
- *Why here:* Connection's facets include keeping in touch at a distance; section 0 and Communication hold the means (mail, phones, messaging apps). *Also fits:* Cancer (family), Taurus (remittances).
- *What it would add:* the duty of staying in touch, and who carries it: the kin-keeper, often a woman (di Leonardo, "The Female World of Cards and Holidays", 1987).
- *Avenues:* kin-keeping (di Leonardo); how often adult children call their parents; holiday cards and annual letters (under Communication's Time and rhythm); migrants' calls and visits home; reunions; "losing touch", and the guilt it brings; social media as a way of keeping up without talking.

### C42. Living between two cultures (split from duality)
- *Working definition:* the experience of belonging to two cultures, languages or countries at once, and of moving between them.
- *Why here:* Duality's facets include double identity, biculturalism and belonging to two countries; the concept now shares space with paired worldviews, contradiction and two-facedness. *Also fits:* Aries (identity), Cancer (belonging).
- *What it would add:* biculturalism as a topic of its own, apart from duality's moral side (hypocrisy, C43) and its side in thought (paired worldviews).
- *Avenues:* *ni de aquí ni de allá* (under Duality's Words); studies of acculturation (Berry's four strategies *check*) and of bicultural identity (Benet-Martínez and colleagues *check*); "third-culture kids"; people who move back to Mexico after growing up in the U.S., and how they are received; memoirs of growing up between languages (Rodriguez; Anzaldúa); the two-country lives of daily border crossers and snowbirds (under Duality's Everyday practice).

### C43. Hypocrisy and double standards (split from duality)
- *Working definition:* saying one thing and doing another; holding others to rules one breaks; judging the same act differently in different people.
- *Why here:* Duality's facets include double lives and two faces (*doble moral*), and its Silences name institutions' double standards on sex, drink and money. *Also fits:* Sagittarius (honesty), Libra (fairness), Scorpio (secrecy).
- *What it would add:* the moral charge against two-ness: what counts as hypocrisy, who gets accused of it, and the double standards a culture lives with (for men and for women; for the rich and for the poor).
- *Avenues:* *doble moral* in Mexican public debate (under Duality's Words); the sexual double standard, as surveys measure it; hypocrisy scandals (under Duality's Conflict and crisis); "do as I say, not as I do"; integrity, a Tier 2 concept under seven signs, as its opposite; experiments on moral hypocrisy (Batson and colleagues *check*).

### C44. Twins (split from siblings and duality)
- *Working definition:* children born together, and what a culture believes about them: blessing or danger, one soul or two, alike or opposite.
- *Why here:* the research notes call twins "the sign's figure rather than an idea of human life" and keep their content under siblings, duality and taking turns; beliefs about twins vary greatly across cultures. *Also fits:* Cancer (birth), Sagittarius (myth).
- *What it would add:* a topic that joins Siblings and Duality: twin names, twin customs, twin stories (as their owners share them), and the twin as the model of the double.
- *Avenues:* twin beliefs in ethnography (the link between twins and salmon among some Northwest Coast peoples, as the nations share it *check*; Yoruba *ibeji* figures, as an example from elsewhere); naming twins (*cuates*, under Siblings' Words; rhyming and matching names); twins' festivals and associations (under Siblings); twin studies, and how they shaped the debate over nature and nurture; the evil twin in fiction; twin birth rates, and how fertility treatments changed them (under Siblings' Measures).

### C45. Seniority and birth order (split from siblings)
- *Working definition:* rank among siblings and age-mates (eldest, middle, youngest), and the duties and privileges each place carries.
- *Why here:* Siblings' facets begin with birth order and seniority; the research notes give sibling rivalry (siblings "fighting over the last piece of cake"). *Also fits:* Capricorn (rank, age ways), Cancer (family).
- *What it would add:* seniority as a principle that reaches beyond kin: the Filipino *kuya* and *ate*, the words for an older brother and sister used for any older person *check*; the Japanese *senpai* and *kōhai*, senior and junior at school and work *check*; the eldest daughter's and the youngest son's roles.
- *Avenues:* birth-order research and its critics (under Siblings' Beliefs); the "eldest daughter" talk (under Siblings' Everyday practice); the *xocoyote*, the youngest who stays with the parents (under Siblings' Words; Taurus C36); *kuya* and *ate* in the pilot's Filipino families; *senpai* and *kōhai* in Japanese American and Japanese Canadian life; seniority at work and in unions ("last hired, first fired").

### C46. Brotherhood and sisterhood beyond kin (split from siblings)
- *Working definition:* calling and treating non-kin as brothers and sisters: in faith, in arms, in unions, in gangs, among friends.
- *Why here:* Siblings' Who it applies to lists non-kin called brother or sister (fellow believers, union members, soldiers, fraternities, gangs, *carnales*); the research notes give the sign's figure as a pair of brothers. *Also fits:* Aquarius (community, friendship), Capricorn (duty), Scorpio (loyalty and betrayal).

### C47. Intercession (split from mediation)
- *Working definition:* pleading with a power on another's behalf: saints and the Virgin before God, a patron before an official, a mother before an angry father.
- *Why here:* Mediation's facets include intercession, and its Beliefs line contrasts Catholic intercession with the Protestant teaching of one mediator. *Also fits:* Sagittarius (faith), Pisces (spirituality, compassion), Capricorn (authority).
- *What it would add:* the go-between who turns upward, toward power, as distinct from the go-between between equals: prayers to saints, vows to them (*mandas*, under the investigation sheet's ritual family), the *padrino* who "puts in a word", and the mother as intercessor in the family.
- *Avenues:* devotion to the saints and to the Virgin of Guadalupe; *ex-votos* (painted thanks for a miracle); prayer requests and prayer chains; "putting in a good word" at work; the *padrino* and the *palanca* (under Connection's Words); mothers who plead with fathers for their children, as families describe it; intercession in the other faiths of the pilot, as each describes it.

### C48. Go-betweens and brokers (split from mediation)
- *Working definition:* those who stand between two parties for any purpose (trade, marriage, paperwork, introductions), not only to settle a quarrel.
- *Why here:* Mediation's working definition reaches "more broadly" to the go-between who stands between parties (brokers, interpreters, intercessors); its Words include *gestor*, *coyote* and *celestina*. *Also fits:* Taurus (C6, exchange and trade), Libra (diplomacy).
- *What it would add:* the middleman as a cultural figure, trusted and resented, apart from peacemaking: the matchmaker, the fixer, the labor contractor, the real-estate agent, the influencer who stands between brands and buyers.
- *Avenues:* brokers in Mexican political life (the *cacique*, under Mediation's Margins and extremes); matchmakers and *celestinas*; labor contractors in farm work (under Mediation's Everyday practice); *gestores* (under Mediation's economy); suspicion of "the middleman" in sayings and politics ("cut out the middleman"); studies of brokerage in social networks (Burt's "structural holes" *check*).
