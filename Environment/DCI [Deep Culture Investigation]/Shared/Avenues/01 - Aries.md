# Avenues of investigation: 01 Aries (self and identity)

**What this file is.** For each Aries concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Aries concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (settled by the author, 2026-11-10): self-assertion, identity, autonomy, initiative, beginning, courage, independence, anger, competition, recklessness. Autonomy and independence are two entries.

## 0. The domain: self and identity

**Traditional areas:** the self, the body, appearance, beginnings, initiative. **The domain question:** what is a self here, and how does it assert itself?

- **What a self is.** Independent or interdependent selves (Markus and Kitayama, 1991); horizontal and vertical individualism and collectivism (Triandis); where the self is located: the individual, the family, the lineage or house, the clan and its crests, the land, the faith community, the trade. Studies that ask people to complete "I am..." twenty times show what people name first (*check* for regional studies).
- **Personhood.** When a person becomes a person (birth, naming, baptism, a first birthday, a first ceremony) and when they stop being one; the standing of the unborn, the dead, ancestors, animals and spirits.
- **The body.** Bodily ideals; fitness and outdoor culture; manual labor and the pride of a working body; modesty and nudity norms; tattoos and piercing (Pew's tattoo surveys *check*); cosmetic surgery (the American Society of Plastic Surgeons' regional figures *check*); sun, skin and colorism; hair.
- **Appearance.** Dress codes at work, school and worship; first impressions; how far appearance should show the self against hide it; regional dress (outdoor gear as everyday wear in the Pacific Northwest; *vaquero* and *norteño* dress); pageants; makeup and grooming norms by gender.
- **Naming ways** (Fischer's folkway for Aries):
  - first names: how common or rare (the Social Security Administration's baby names by state; British Columbia's and other provinces' name lists; Mexico's civil registry data *check*); Varnum and Kitayama (2011) found rarer names in frontier regions, a measure of individualism;
  - naming after kin, saints, virtues, places, celebrities; Spanish double surnames; whether women keep their names at marriage;
  - Indigenous names and naming ceremonies, as the nations share them; hereditary names that carry rank and privileges on the Northwest Coast;
  - nicknames and pet names; renaming (immigrants anglicizing names; reclaiming names; transitions);
  - names of businesses, teams, streets and places as identity statements.
- **Sport ways** (runner-up; home sign Leo): individual sports against team sports as a statement of the self.
- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* personal names and naming customs; kin terms (what one is called by each relative); how the language says "I" (dropped, softened, or replaced by a title or kin term); greetings and self-introductions; the people's origin stories; body decoration (tattoo, paint, piercing, scarring) and what each mark says; hairstyles and their meanings; dress and ornaments that mark age, status, marriage or mourning; masks; portraits in carving and painting; beliefs about the shadow, the soul and reflections; dream accounts (who one is in dreams); rites that make a new self; praise and boasting songs; songs owned by one person; personal crests and emblems, as the nations share them; the gesture people use to point to themselves (chest or nose); posture and bearing; children's role-play.
  - *Writing:* autobiographies and memoirs; diaries; letters, and how people sign them and describe themselves; spiritual autobiographies; confessions; epitaphs; personal mottoes; commonplace books; conduct books on self-presentation; poetry in the first person; family crests and heraldry.
  - *Public records:* census forms and the categories they offer; the fields on identity cards (what the state thinks defines a person); name-frequency statistics; school records; national costumes; portraits on money and stamps; citizenship study materials.
  - *Mass media:* magazine profiles and interviews; celebrity culture; fashion and fitness magazines; makeover shows; confessional scenes in reality TV; personality quizzes in magazines; portrait conventions in photography (smiling or solemn); baby-name books.
  - *Online:* bios; profile pictures and avatars; selfies and filters; usernames; "about me" pages; shared personality-test results; dating profiles; the characters people build in games; influencers' self-presentation.
  - *Commerce:* monograms and personalized goods; beauty and cosmetics sales; tattoo studios' portfolios; fashion retail; cosmetic-surgery marketing; personal-branding consultants; baby-name websites; school picture day and portrait studios.
  - *Organizations:* youth organizations that shape a young self (Scouts, Girl Guides, 4-H, the YMCA and YWCA), with their handbooks, pledges and badges; gyms, fitness clubs and sports clubs (the body as a project); dance, folk-costume and regalia groups that keep a people's dress, as the nations share them; heritage, lineage and genealogical societies; clan and house organizations, as the nations share them; pageant organizers; tattoo and body-art conventions; body-acceptance and size-acceptance groups; clubs built on a bodily trait (tall clubs, twins' associations, left-handers' clubs); speaking clubs that train self-presentation (Toastmasters); fraternities and sororities (initiation, nicknames, the remade self); religious orders and congregations that give a new name or a new self (confirmation names, names taken in religious life, adult baptism); adoptees' and donor-conceived people's search groups (finding one's origin); support groups for people changing their name, body or gender; self-advocacy groups of disabled people ("nothing about us without us").
  - *The people who handle it:* tattoo artists; barbers and hairdressers (and the talk in their chairs); portrait photographers; stylists; cosmetic surgeons; therapists (self-concept); career coaches; registrars of births; clergy at naming and baptism.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' first impressions of how people present themselves; guides telling newcomers how to introduce themselves; old ethnographic portraits, read critically.
  - *Scholarship and data:* self-construal scales; "I am..." tests; personality surveys by region; name-frequency studies; body-image surveys; ethnographies of personhood; studies of self-enhancement and modesty; linguistic studies of pronoun dropping and honorifics.

## 1. Self-assertion

**Working definition:** declaring and acting on one's own existence, wishes and claims in the presence of others.
**Facets:** speaking up; saying no; claiming credit; standing one's ground; self-promotion; complaining; asking for what one wants.
**Neighbors and opposites:** assertiveness, confidence, voice; deference, modesty, self-effacement. **Excess:** domination, arrogance, entitlement. **Deficiency:** passivity, being a pushover.

- **Words:** "assertive", "pushy", "speak up", "stand your ground", "hold your own", "humble", "entitled"; "aggressive" used as praise in business; Spanish *igualado* (someone who acts as the equal of their betters: a key Mexican word for assertion against hierarchy), *dejado* (someone who lets themselves be pushed around), *atrevido*, *aguantar*; Canadian politeness markers ("sorry").
- **Beliefs and standing:** is speaking up a right, a duty or rude? Religious teaching on humility and meekness; Quaker plainness; the squeaky wheel against the nail that sticks out; assertion as self-respect or as disrespect.
- **Feeling and the body:** eye contact, voice volume, handshake, interrupting, personal space (Hall's proxemics), pointing, posture.
- **Everyday practice:** negotiating salaries and prices; returning goods and complaining; disputes with neighbors; who talks in meetings and classrooms; who asks whom out; haggling in markets (*tianguis*) against fixed prices.
- **Life course:** "use your words"; teaching children to stand up to bullies; participation grades; what daughters and sons are told.
- **Ritual:** occasions where speaking up is formalized: town halls and public comment, debates and speech contests, roasts and toasts, testimony in church or meeting (Quaker meeting, evangelical testimony), protest marches; speakers at Northwest Coast feasts, as the nations describe them.
- **Institutions:** performance reviews that reward "leadership"; customer-service norms ("the customer is always right"); consumer-complaint systems (Better Business Bureau; Mexico's PROFECO).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praised when standing up to bullies; punished as "uppity", *igualado*, the tall poppy; passive-aggression as a sanction; the "Karen" meme as a sanction on entitled assertion.
- **Expression:** proverbs; self-help and assertiveness-training books; anthems of self-assertion ("My Way"); advertising tone.
- **Exemplars:** people who spoke up (civil-rights and farmworker leaders; Native leaders' recorded speeches); anti-exemplars (bullies, the entitled).
- **History:** the Yankee town meeting (every man's voice); Spanish colonial hierarchy and deference; rank asserted at Northwest Coast feasts, as the nations describe them (colonial-era accounts, such as Codere's *Fighting with Property*, 1950, are contested).
- **Measures:** GLOBE assertiveness (country); Hofstede's masculinity; extraversion by state (Rentfrow); consumer-complaint rates per capita (*check*); salary-negotiation surveys by gender.
- **Variation:** gender; class; code-switching between ethnic and workplace norms; regional politeness ("the Seattle Freeze"); newcomers against old families.
- **Relations:** anger, competition, autonomy; politeness (Gemini); hierarchy (Capricorn).
- **Who it applies to:** children toward adults; workers toward bosses; women; minorities; customers.
- **Silences:** salary and money talk; challenging elders or clergy.
- **Change:** "setting boundaries" and therapy language; social-media self-promotion; backlash against both.
- **The senses:** loudness as a claim: who may raise their voice, and where (the market, the stadium and the dinner table against the library, the bus and the church); the car horn and its etiquette (rare in some cities, constant in others); silence as refusal or as power, with its own meanings (Basso, "To Give Up on Words", 1970); voices heard as confident or weak (debates over "uptalk" and "vocal fry" in young women's speech *check*; accents heard as pushy); touch as a claim: the firm handshake, the hand on the shoulder, the hug nobody asked for; strong scents in shared air (perfume and cologne; scent-free workplaces); food as assertion: sending a dish back, special orders, insisting on paying the bill (the fight over the check), refusing food a host offers.
- **Space, place and things:** taking up space: elbows and armrests, "manspreading" debates, the aisle seat, the cart left in the way; who sits where (the head of the table; the front row; seating at feasts, as the nations describe it); queues and cutting in; claiming spots: towels on beach chairs, chairs saving shoveled parking spots in snowy cities *check*; fences, hedges, "keep out" signs, yard signs and flags as claims made to neighbors; loud trucks and modified exhausts; bumper stickers; the lectern and the microphone, and who controls them; the complaint counter and the customer-service desk as stages; the open-plan office against the closed door.
- **Time and rhythm:** who may make others wait (lateness as a claim of rank; Levine, *A Geography of Time*, 1997); interrupting, and how long a turn may run; how fast a "no" comes (at once, or softened after a pause); the seasons of asking: annual reviews and raise season, budget meetings, public-comment nights; holiday family gatherings, where old pecking orders return; claims on time itself: "my time", leaving work on time, not answering messages after hours, out-of-office replies.
- **The economy of it:** the confidence and assertiveness industry (coaching, workshops, books on saying no); negotiation courses and salary-negotiation services; public-speaking coaches; personal-branding services; complaint handling as a business cost (call centers; "retention" desks that reward the customers who push); price-matching, haggling and asking for discounts as everyday economics; who pays for saying no (lost shifts, being passed over) and who gains from always saying yes; "straight talk" sold as a regional or national brand.
- **Conflict and crisis:** when assertion turns into a fight: shoving matches, staredowns, and who backs down; standing up to a bully, and what adults do next; strikes, walkouts and sit-ins as collective assertion; shouting matches at school-board and council meetings; crises in which someone must speak up against a superior (crew resource management in aviation and surgery, which trains juniors to challenge seniors after crashes in which they had stayed quiet *check*); assertion under colonization and occupation, as the affected peoples tell it; the COVID-19 pandemic's disputes over masks in stores and on planes.
- **Margins and extremes:** the far ends: the bully boss, harassment, belligerent passengers; at the other edge, the very shy, the selectively mute, "doormats"; what is treated as illness (oppositional defiant disorder; social anxiety disorder; dependent personality disorder) and what as bad character (Conrad and Schneider, *Deviance and Medicalization*, 1980); hecklers and disrupters of meetings; styles built on extreme assertion (battle rap, trash talk, shock jocks); refusers who turn down every request on principle.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* recorded conversation (turn-taking, interruptions, overlapping talk); formulas for requests and refusals; boasting and self-praise traditions; verbal duels and insult contests; market haggling; children's quarrels and how adults settle them; who may speak at gatherings, and in what order (speaking-stick customs, as the nations share them); who greets first; gestures of dominance and deference (bowing, eye contact); proverbs on speaking up and keeping quiet; folktales of the bold and the meek; songs of defiance.
  - *Writing:* etiquette manuals (what counts as pushy); advice columns ("how do I stand up to..."); assertiveness and "saying no" self-help; negotiation books; complaint letters in archives; petitions and open letters; memoirs of standing up; sermons on humility and pride; conduct books that taught deference.
  - *Public records:* minutes of town meetings, school boards and church councils (who speaks, and how); transcripts of public comment at council meetings; consumer-complaint records; report-card comments ("needs to speak up"); classroom participation policies; student-council records.
  - *Mass media:* talk shows and call-in radio; conflict in reality TV; confrontations in soap operas and telenovelas; political debates and their limits on self-praise; trash talk in sport and at press conferences; letters to the editor; advertising tone (soft sell or hard sell).
  - *Online:* reviews and their complaint styles; "am I wrong?" forums; call-out posts; comment sections; viral confrontation videos; memes that punish entitlement; self-promotion on professional networks.
  - *Commerce:* customer-service scripts; sales training; negotiation workshops; salary surveys (who negotiates); return policies and return rates; tipping disputes.
  - *Organizations:* speaking and debate clubs (Toastmasters; school speech and debate leagues; Model United Nations); improv and theater groups used to build confidence; assertiveness and confidence workshops at community centers and women's groups; women's leadership and networking circles; unions, worker centers and tenants' associations, which train members to speak up together; neighborhood and parent-teacher associations (who speaks at their meetings); consumer associations; self-advocacy groups of people with intellectual disabilities (the People First movement, whose early conventions were held in British Columbia and Oregon *check*); youth councils and youth parliaments; church councils and congregational meetings; organizing groups and their training manuals (Alinsky's *Rules for Radicals*, 1971, as one tradition); groups for shy and socially anxious people; elders' councils, as the nations describe their order of speaking.
  - *The people who handle it:* customer-service and restaurant workers (their "customers from hell" stories); negotiation trainers; human-resources staff; teachers of shy pupils; therapists who teach assertiveness; coaches; flight attendants; union organizers.
  - *Outsiders:* culture-shock and business-culture guides on directness; expatriates' blogs; orientation materials for immigrants ("here you must speak up").
  - *Scholarship and data:* conversation analysis; politeness research; cross-cultural studies of requests and apologies; the ethnography of speaking; assertiveness surveys; studies of gender and negotiation.

**Key questions:** Is asserting oneself self-respect or disrespect? Toward whom is it allowed, and toward whom never?

## 2. Identity

**Working definition:** what a person or group takes itself to be: the answer to "who are you?"
**Facets:** personal, family, ethnic, regional, national, religious, occupational, generational and gender identity; chosen against given; hyphenated identities; identity stamped by deeds rather than found by looking inward.
**Neighbors and opposites:** self, belonging, heritage, roots; anonymity, alienation, identity crisis. **Excess:** tribalism, exclusion. **Deficiency:** rootlessness.

- **Words:** what people answer to "who are you?": a name, a job ("what do you do?"), a hometown, a family (*¿de quién eres hijo?*), a nation, house and clan (Northwest Coast introductions, as the nations' protocols describe them); regional self-labels ("Pacific Northwesterner", "West Coast", "Californian", "Cascadian", "Lower Mainland", *norteño*, *sudcaliforniano*, *choyero* *check*); "Chicano", "Mexican-American", "Latino", "Hispanic", *pocho* (a Mexican's word for a Mexican-American who has lost Spanish), *malinchismo* (preferring the foreign).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is identity given (blood, birth, land) or made (choice, deeds)? Reinvention (California as the place to remake oneself); authenticity ("be yourself"); ancestry against place.
- **Feeling and the body:** identity worn on the body: tattoos, dress, hair, regalia, jewelry; skin color and colorism.
- **Everyday practice:** introductions (what people say first); bumper stickers and license plates; regional merchandise (the Cascadia "Doug flag"); sports fandom; food as identity; heritage language at home.
- **Life course:** heritage-language schools; learning one's clan, house and lineage, as the nations share it; *quinceañeras*, confirmations, bar and bat mitzvahs; the teenage identity crisis; college as identity-making.
- **Ritual:** heritage festivals; powwows and potlatches as the nations describe them; Mexican Independence Day (September 16) against Cinco de Mayo in the U.S.; Canada Day; the Fourth of July; Pride; Día de Muertos.
- **Institutions:** census self-identification (U.S. race and ethnicity questions; Canada's ethnic origin and Indigenous identity; INEGI's self-identification as Indigenous or Afro-Mexican, against counts of speakers of Indigenous languages); citizenship classes and naturalization ceremonies; ethnic and hometown associations (Mexican hometown clubs, *clubes de oriundos*; Nordic lodges such as the Sons of Norway *check*); churches that serve one people or language; ethnic and diaspora media; heritage museums and cultural centers; sports clubs and leagues tied to a people or a hometown.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** charges of inauthenticity ("whitewashed", *pocho*); false claims to Native identity and how nations respond; "passing"; pride and shame about origins.
- **Expression:** Chicano and borderlands literature (Anzaldúa, *Borderlands/La Frontera*, 1987); Northwest regional writing; songs of place ("California Dreamin'", *corridos*); slogans ("Keep Portland Weird"); flags and seals.
- **Exemplars:** figures a place says embody "us"; contested icons.
- **History:** statehood and provincehood; the Chicano movement; the Red Power and fish-wars era; Expo 86 in Vancouver; each migration wave's identity.
- **Measures:** census self-identification; surveys of primary identity (Environics, Angus Reid on regional identity in Canada; Pew on national identity); Gallup's 50-state poll on state pride (2013-2014 *check*); polls on Cascadian identity or independence (*check*); language retention across generations.
- **Variation:** immigrant generation; urban and rural; nation membership against pan-Indigenous identity; multiracial identity.
- **Relations:** belonging (Cancer); community (Aquarius); recognition (Leo); heritage and tradition (Capricorn).
- **Who it applies to:** places and companies (brand identity); whether people ascribe identity to AI and robots (present-day attitudes only).
- **Silences:** "where are you really from?"; ancestry questions; hidden origins.
- **Change:** rising multiracial identification; secular identities; online identities; debates over gender identity.
- **The senses:** the tastes and smells of home as identity (a grandmother's kitchen; foods carried across borders; the dish every family claims to make best); food rules that mark who one is (kosher, halal, fasts, meatless Fridays in Lent; the foods a nation's gatherings call for, as the nations share them); accents heard as identity, and their loss or "reduction"; a people's soundscape (church bells, drums, *banda* from a passing car, a ferry horn, the languages heard in the street); music as a badge (*norteño*, country, hip hop, K-pop fandom); the sensory memories migrants name first (Howes, *Sensual Relations*; Classen, *Worlds of Sense*); imported groceries that recreate the taste of the old country.
- **Space, place and things:** places that hold identity: hometowns, family land, homelands named in origin stories (Basso, *Wisdom Sits in Places*, 1996); place names in a people's own language, and their restoring; ethnic neighborhoods (Chinatowns, *barrios*, Japantowns) with their gates, murals and shrines; which languages appear on public signs (Landry and Bourhis, 1997); heirlooms that carry a lineage (Miller, *Stuff*, 2010); home altars, family-photo walls, framed diplomas; flags flown at home and from trucks; the house type a people builds and keeps (Glassie); what museums and historical societies keep as "ours"; regalia, carved poles and crests in public places, as the nations share them.
- **Time and rhythm:** the calendars a household keeps (religious, national, the Lunar New Year, the nations' own seasonal round as they share it), and which are kept at home and which only in public; generational time: first, second and third generation, and the return of interest in roots among the grandchildren of immigrants (Hansen's "third-generation return", 1938 *check*); anniversaries of arrival, founding and loss; how many generations back people can name; whether identity is placed in the ancestors, in the present, or in what one will become; heritage time: weekend language schools, summer trips "back home", yearly family reunions.
- **The economy of it:** the roots industry (ancestry DNA kits, genealogy services, heritage and "roots" tourism); ethnic markets, food brands and restaurants as both livelihood and identity; identity in marketing (ads aimed at ethnic markets; authenticity as a selling point, and MacCannell's staged authenticity); who earns from a people's designs and art, and the nations' own responses; remittances as a tie to a home identity; a region's identity sold to tourists; jobs as identity (the timber town, the fishing town, the tech town), and what happens to "who we are" when the industry goes.
- **Conflict and crisis:** identity in conflict: ethnic and religious clashes, rivalries between towns and schools, hate incidents and how communities answer them; wars and removals that hardened or split identities (Japanese-American internment, and what it did to later generations); disasters that forge a "we" (the "___ Strong" slogans after fires and earthquakes); displacement and loss of land as crises of identity, as the affected peoples tell them; residential and boarding schools, as the nations tell what was taken and how it is reclaimed; the COVID-19 pandemic's harassment of Asian Americans and Asian Canadians, a test of who counts as "us".
- **Margins and extremes:** identity at its limit: ethnic nationalism and supremacist movements; cults that strip a member's name and past and hand out new ones; impostors who live as someone else; people who feel they belong nowhere (*ni de aquí ni de allá*, "neither from here nor from there"); the medical edge (dissociation, amnesia) and how a culture explains a lost or split self; identity thrown off entirely: drifters, hermits, people who cut off their family and change their name; fandoms and subcultures that become the whole self.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* what a people calls itself, and what it calls others; nicknames for a region's people; introduction customs (lineage, place, clan); recited genealogies; origin stories; crests and emblems, as the nations share them; dress and food that mark belonging; songs of place; dialect as a badge; jokes about neighboring groups; rituals of attachment to a place.
  - *Writing:* memoirs of mixed, hyphenated or changed identity; coming-of-age novels; family histories; diaspora literature; regional literature; editorials in ethnic newspapers; local and county histories (including the old county histories with settlers' biographies).
  - *Public records:* census questionnaires over time (the categories offered, and how they changed); national symbols, anthems and holidays; school heritage curricula; museum exhibits on "who we are"; official tourism branding.
  - *Mass media:* diaspora radio and television; regional news personalities; regional advertising; sports fandom; regional magazines; documentaries about "who we are".
  - *Online:* hashtags and regional communities; memes about where one is from; "you know you're from here when..." lists; identity quizzes; ancestry-test forums; heritage-language learners' communities; flags in bios.
  - *Commerce:* regional merchandise (T-shirts, decals, bumper stickers); ancestry DNA kits and their marketing; heritage tourism; ethnic grocery stores; regional food brands; the place names local businesses choose.
  - *Organizations:* ethnic and hometown associations; heritage and lineage societies; cultural centers; powwow committees, as the nations share them; language-revival programs; sports supporters' clubs.
  - *The people who handle it:* genealogists; curators of heritage museums; cultural-center staff; teachers of ethnic studies; tour guides (how they tell a place's story).
  - *Outsiders:* the names outsiders give the group, and how the group answers them; stereotypes in outside media; the "the people" sections of travel guides.
  - *Scholarship and data:* identity surveys; census self-identification over time; language retention across generations; ethnographies of ethnicity; studies of regional identity and dialect pride.

**Key questions:** Is identity found inside, inherited, or made by deeds? What comes first when a person introduces themselves?

## 3. Autonomy

**Working definition** (author, 2026-11-10): deciding for oneself: freedom from being controlled.
**Facets:** personal life choices; bodily autonomy; moral autonomy; autonomy at work; the autonomy of youth; collective autonomy: towns and Native nations governing themselves.
**Neighbors and opposites:** freedom, self-determination, sovereignty, consent; control, paternalism, obedience. **Excess:** refusal of every obligation. **Deficiency:** dependence on being directed.

- **Words:** "my choice", "my body, my choice", "it's a free country", "live and let live", "mind your own business", "don't tread on me", "self-determination", "sovereignty"; Spanish *autonomía*, *libre albedrío*, *cada quien su vida*, *usos y costumbres* (Indigenous self-government in Mexico).
- **Beliefs and standing:** who should choose a spouse, a career, a home: the person or the family? Attitudes to paternalism; ideals of consent; the ethics of self-determination.
- **Feeling and the body:** bodily autonomy (abortion, vaccination, assisted dying, drugs, tattoos, gender); who decides in medicine: the patient or the family (Blackhall et al., 1995, on Mexican-American and European-American attitudes to telling patients a diagnosis *check*).
- **Everyday practice:** choices of career, spouse and home; adult children living with parents; teenagers' privacy; homeschooling; living under homeowners' association rules; off-grid living.
- **Life course:** the age at which children choose their clothes, bedtimes, friends; curfews; "free-range" parenting and the neighbors who object to it; the age of leaving home (measurable: young adults living with parents, U.S. Census, Statistics Canada, INEGI).
- **Ritual:** the moments that grant self-rule: the eighteenth birthday, the first car, the first vote, leaving for college; independence days as celebrations of collective self-rule (July 4, September 16, Canada Day); sovereignty days as the nations observe them; Pride as a celebration of choosing one's own life.
- **Institutions:** local control of towns and schools (town meetings, school boards); the self-governing bodies of Native nations (elected councils, hereditary chiefs), as the nations describe them; school choice and homeschooling networks; church governance (congregations that run themselves against bishops who run them); remote and self-directed work; worker cooperatives; professions that set their own standards; self-directed care programs for disabled people and elders.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** mockery of "helicopter parents" and "control freaks"; busybodies; and on the other side, the family's sanction on a child who chooses alone.
- **Expression:** Oregon's motto *Alis volat propriis*, "she flies with her own wings"; the State of Jefferson and Cascadia independence movements; songs ("Don't Fence Me In").
- **Exemplars:** figures of personal and collective self-rule (sovereignty leaders; dissenters); contested ones.
- **History:** the Yankee town's self-government; the Spanish crown's and the missions' control over Native peoples; the West's rhetoric of autonomy against its dependence on federal money and water (Limerick; White); the fish wars; the *ejido*, communal farmland run by its own assembly.
- **Measures:** the World Values Survey item on how much freedom of choice and control people feel over their lives (by region where samples allow *check*); young adults living with parents; homeschooling share; share living under homeowners' associations (*check*); Pew privacy surveys; familism scales among Latino families (Sabogal et al., 1987).
- **Variation:** family-centered against individual decision-making; gender; age; religion.
- **Relations:** independence (its own entry); freedom (Sagittarius); duty (Capricorn); care and family (Cancer); community (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** children, the elderly, people with disabilities, Native nations as collectives, cities, companies; autonomous machines (present-day attitudes to self-driving cars, which run in San Francisco and Los Angeles; surveys by state *check*).
- **Silences:** unspoken family expectations; control inside families.
- **Change:** surveillance technology; remote work; fights over bodily autonomy; AI systems acting on their own.
- **The senses:** touch and consent: who may touch whom, and whether children must kiss or hug relatives ("give Grandma a kiss") or may refuse; food as a choice: clean-plate rules, children's say over what they eat, adults choosing a diet the family rejects (vegetarian, vegan, fasting, giving up a family dish); headphones, earbuds and closed doors as ways to claim one's own sensory space; the feeling of being watched (cameras, tracking, monitored keystrokes) and how people describe it; the sights and sounds of the open road and the outdoors invoked as freedom (cross-reference: freedom, Sagittarius).
- **Space, place and things:** a room of one's own and a lock on the door (a bedroom per child; whether parents knock; diaries with locks); the car and its keys as the vehicle of self-rule; the phone as a private world, and location-sharing apps between parents and teenagers or between partners; what homeowners' associations decide about paint, lawns, fences and flags; off-grid cabins, vans, boats and tiny houses as places beyond others' control; wheelchairs, hearing aids and other aids as tools of self-rule; lands under collective self-rule (*ejido* lands; reserves and reservations, as the nations describe them); the workplace itself: open-plan offices, cubicles, the home office.
- **Time and rhythm:** control over one's own hours: shifts set by others, against flextime and the gig worker's "be your own boss"; curfews, bedtimes and screen-time limits; the ages at which each choice is granted (clothes, friends, dating, driving, moving out; the milestones live in Life course, their timing here); free time against obligated time (sabbaths, Sundays, family meals); retirement as regained time; time-use surveys on discretionary time; waiting for permission as a mark of being controlled; seasonal work (fishing openings, harvests) that sets people's days for them.

## 4. Initiative

**Working definition:** starting things and acting without being told; taking the lead unasked.
**Facets:** at work; in business (entrepreneurship); in civic life (starting groups, volunteering first); in politics (citizens starting petitions and ballot campaigns); in the home (helping without being asked).
**Neighbors and opposites:** enterprise, drive, being proactive; waiting for orders, inertia. **Excess:** overstepping, "cowboy" behavior. **Deficiency:** passivity.

- **Words:** "self-starter", "go-getter", "proactive", "can-do", "hustle", "take charge", "Just Do It" (Nike, founded in Oregon); Spanish *iniciativa*, *echarle ganas*, *ponerse las pilas*, *acomedido* (a Mexican word for someone who helps without being asked).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is acting unasked admirable or presumptuous? Is it expected of subordinates, children, women? Its link to the work ethic.

## 5. Beginning

- **Beliefs and standing:** are second chances a right or is starting over running away? Being born again (shared with Scorpio's rebirth); progress against tradition; who was here first ("time immemorial") and why it matters.

## 6. Courage

**Working definition:** acting rightly in the face of fear, danger, pain or social cost.
**Facets:** physical courage; moral courage (standing up for the right); social courage (holding an unpopular view); endurance and fortitude; the courage to admit fault or show vulnerability; the courage to leave (migration).
**Neighbors and opposites:** bravery, valor, grit, fortitude; cowardice. **Excess:** recklessness, bravado. **Deficiency:** timidity.

- **Words:** "brave", "guts", "grit", "nerve", "chicken", "yellow"; Spanish *valiente*, *valor*, *echado pa'delante*, *aguantador*, *no rajarse* (not backing down; Octavio Paz, *El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950, on *rajarse*), *macho* in its older sense of brave; Mexican Spanish *coraje* means anger more often than courage (a revealing link between the two).
- **Beliefs and standing:** which courage is honored most, physical or moral? Courage and manhood; whether women's endurance counts as courage; martyrdom; warrior traditions as the nations describe them; stoicism; "vulnerability is courage" in therapeutic culture.
- **Feeling and the body:** rules for showing fear ("boys don't cry"); tolerance of pain; injury as pride; extreme sports.
- **Everyday practice:** whistleblowing and speaking out against wrongdoing; stepping in to stop harassment; volunteer firefighting and search and rescue; enlisting; dangerous trades (logging, commercial fishing); big-wave surfing; speaking at public meetings.
- **Life course:** children's dares; hazing and initiations; first hunts and first fights; Scout badges; how parents teach children to face fear.
- **Ritual:** Veterans Day and Remembrance Day; Mexico's Niños Héroes (September 13); medal ceremonies; memorials to firefighters and police.
- **Institutions:** military honors (the Medal of Honor, Canada's Victoria Cross, Mexico's decorations for valor); civilian bravery awards (the Carnegie Hero Fund, U.S. and Canada); awards for moral courage (the Profile in Courage Award *check*); volunteer fire companies and search-and-rescue teams; the armed forces and their academies; lifesaving awards from Scouts and lifeguard services; martial-arts schools.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** medals, hero status, songs; ridicule and shame for cowardice ("chicken", *rajón*).
- **Expression:** war stories; *corridos* of revolutionaries and outlaws (and *narcocorridos*, which praise a contested kind of nerve); Westerns; mountain-man tales; stories of war and peace as the nations share them; films.
- **Exemplars:** smokejumpers and firefighters; soldiers (the Nisei of the 442nd, whose families were interned); Fred Korematsu (moral courage, Oakland); César Chávez; the Niños Héroes; Native leaders.
- **History:** frontier danger; wars; internment and resistance; wildfires; earthquakes (1906); preparing for the Cascadia subduction quake.
- **Measures:** Carnegie Medal awards per capita by state (*check*); military enlistment by state and county (Defense Department population representation reports *check*); volunteer firefighters per capita (NFPA); surveys of bystander intervention; dangerous-occupation shares (BLS).
- **Variation:** gender; class (physical courage in working-class life against moral courage in middle-class ideals); military families; rural and urban; Indigenous warrior societies.

- **Who it applies to:** animals (brave dogs); institutions; machines sent into danger (present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** men's fear; PTSD; the cowardice of institutions.
- **Change:** emotional courage and vulnerability rising as ideals; fewer people serving in the military; the standing of first responders.
- **The senses:** food and drink as tests of nerve: chili-eating and hot-sauce bravado (Rozin and Schiller, 1980), eating what others won't, drinking contests; cold as a test: New Year's Day polar bear swims (Vancouver's at English Bay, held since the 1920s *check*); pain borne without a sound (tattoos, piercing, initiations, playing through injury); the sounds of nerve: war cries, battle songs, the drum before a contest, chants in the locker room, the silence kept under fire; the smell of smoke and the feel of heat in firefighters' accounts of what they walk into.
- **Space, place and things:** places that test nerve: cliffs and bridges to jump from, big-wave breaks (Mavericks, in California), peaks (Rainier, Shasta, Denali), the rodeo arena and the bullring, the boxing ring; objects of courage: medals, scars, helmets, turnout gear, weapons kept as heirlooms, regalia and honors as the nations share them; where memorials stand, and how large they are; the fire lookout, the smokejumper base, the lifeguard tower; dangerous workplaces as stages (the logging site, the crab boat's deck, the ironworkers' high steel).

  - *The people who handle it:* trainers of firefighters, soldiers and lifeguards; military chaplains; trauma therapists (what fear does, and how it is spoken of); coaches of combat sports.

  - *Scholarship and data:* studies of heroism and bystanders; enlistment data; dangerous-occupation data; studies of manhood ideals; the sociology of risky trades.

## 7. Independence

**Working definition** (author, 2026-11-10): not depending on others; self-reliance and self-sufficiency are researched here.

**Neighbors and opposites:** self-reliance, self-sufficiency; dependence, interdependence. **Excess:** isolation, refusing all help. **Deficiency:** helplessness.

The candidates come from five places: the sign's traditional areas; the Aries research notes (CurrentNovelDocs, `worldbuilding/Robot_Fundamental_Concepts/01 - The twelve meta-categories/01 - Aries (self and identity)/1 - Human understanding (research)/Low-level concepts (2026-10-30).md`); the Tier 2 concepts and near-synonym groups (`Shared/Concept_Lists_by_Sign.md` and the concept index it cites); concepts other languages name that English lacks; and splits of the current concepts.

**From the sign's traditional areas** (the self, the body, appearance, beginnings, initiative; the body and appearance have no concept of their own)

### C1. The body
- *Working definition:* how people live in, train, show, mark and care for their own bodies, and how far the body counts as part of the self.
- *Why here:* the body is one of the sign's traditional areas, and the domain section treats it only briefly. The research notes frame it as the body in action: physically assertive, but careless of its own protection (self-neglect, pain). *Also fits:* Virgo (health), Taurus (sensuality), Leo (being seen).
- *What it would add:* the current concepts meet the body only as the place of courage, anger or risk. A concept of its own would research bodily ideals, strength, fitness, modesty, body modification, the working body and the aging body, and who makes a body's choices (the line with autonomy).
- *Avenues:* bodily ideals for men and for women, by group (body-image surveys; the bodies in magazines and advertising over time); gym, fitness and outdoor cultures; the pride of a working body in the manual trades; tattoos, piercing and other marks; nudity and modesty norms (saunas, beaches, locker rooms, swimwear); the body in disability, as disabled people describe it; men's neglect of their own health (doctor visits by sex, from health surveys); how the dead body is treated (cross-reference: Scorpio).

### C2. Appearance and self-presentation
- *Working definition:* how people show themselves to others (dress, grooming, bearing, the face put on for work or for strangers), and how closely the shown self should match the inner one.
- *Why here:* appearance is a traditional area with no concept. The research notes list appearance (first impressions; a brisk walk, a firm handshake). The concept index groups appearance, appearances and self-presentation as near-synonyms, and the investigation sheet's Aries angles include self-presentation and self-enhancement (Goffman; Heine et al., 1999). *Also fits:* Leo (being seen), Taurus (dress ways, display), Gemini (first contact).
- *What it would add:* the managed self (Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959) as a topic of its own: when showing off is fine, when modesty is required, and what counts as "fake".
- *Avenues:* dress codes at work, school and worship; advice on first impressions and job interviews; grooming norms by gender and age; smiling or solemn portraits; profile pictures and filters; makeover media; ideals of effortlessness (the Italian *sprezzatura*) against visible effort; regional dress (outdoor gear as everyday wear; *norteño* hats and boots); what is said of people who dress above or below their station.

### C3. Names and naming
- *Working definition:* how people receive, carry, change and use names, and what a name is taken to say about the one who bears it.
- *Why here:* naming ways are this sign's folkway (Fischer, by `Shared/Folkways_Crosswalk.md`), now covered only by a bullet in the domain section. *Also fits:* Cancer (family and lineage), Gemini (forms of address), Capricorn (titles and rank).
- *What it would add:* a concept with unusually hard evidence (name-frequency data) that links identity, family, individuality and beginnings. Naming is where a culture says, at the start of a life, who the person is.
- *Avenues:* rare against common first names by region (Varnum and Kitayama, 2011); naming after kin, saints and places; surnames at marriage; nicknames and pet names; renaming at migration, conversion, adoption and transition; names and naming ceremonies, as the nations share them; hereditary names on the Northwest Coast, as the nations describe them; the names given to businesses, boats, pets, teams and streets; names people refuse or hide.

### C4. Personhood
- *Working definition:* who or what counts as a person, when personhood begins and ends, and whether it is held alone or through others.
- *Why here:* the domain question ("what is a self here?") rests on it, and it now sits in one domain bullet. The Nguni Bantu word *ubuntu*, usually glossed "a person is a person through other persons", names a relational personhood that English has no single word for. *Also fits:* Scorpio (death), Sagittarius (belief), Cancer (birth and family).
- *What it would add:* a base for every other Aries concept: independent and interdependent selves are two answers to what a person is.
- *Avenues:* the rites that make a person (naming, baptism, a first birthday, first ceremonies as the nations share them); beliefs about the unborn, the dead, ancestors, animals and spirits; *ubuntu* in South African public life and writing (Tutu, *No Future Without Forgiveness*, 1999, as one statement *check*); self-construal scales; present-day attitudes to whether animals, rivers or machines can be persons.

**From the sign's own research notes** (the 170 concepts in the Aries research folder's concept table)

### C5. Impatience and urgency
- *Working definition:* the wish for results now and the dislike of waiting; the felt need to act before someone else does.
- *Why here:* the research notes give impatience ("it needs results now") and urgency ("act now, before someone else gets there first"); impatience is a Tier 2 concept, under eight signs in the concept index. *Also fits:* Capricorn (time ways), Taurus (patience), Virgo.
- *What it would add:* the time side of the sign's concepts. No current concept asks how a culture values speed, waiting and delay.
- *Avenues:* pace-of-life studies (Levine and Norenzayan, 1999: walking speed, post-office service and accurate clocks across countries); queues and waiting rooms; delay-of-gratification research and its cultural replications (the "marshmallow test"); same-day delivery and fast food as markets for impatience; field experiments on how long drivers wait before honking *check*; proverbs for and against haste (*vísteme despacio, que tengo prisa*: "dress me slowly, I'm in a hurry").

### C6. Impulsiveness and spontaneity
- *Working definition:* acting on the urge of the moment without planning; prized as spontaneity, faulted as impulsiveness.
- *Why here:* the research notes give impulsiveness ("impulses skip past consequences"), spontaneity, decisiveness, and self-control (impulses redirected rather than forbidden). *Also fits:* Leo (play), Sagittarius, Capricorn (discipline and self-control).
- *What it would add:* the manner of acting that lies beneath initiative and recklessness: whether a culture admires the unplanned act or the planned one. The near-synonym group of discipline, self-discipline and self-control is its other pole.
- *Avenues:* personality-survey items on impulsivity and planning; impulse buying, and how stores invite it; improvisation in music, cooking and talk; how children's impulses are handled (redirected, punished, indulged); proverbs against haste; popular talk of ADHD, and how far it is treated as illness or as temperament.

### C7. Directness
- *Working definition:* saying what one means plainly, without softening; frankness.
- *Why here:* the research notes give directness ("blunt, frank and transparent") and honesty without pretense. *Also fits:* Gemini (directness and context, after Hall), Sagittarius (honesty).
- *What it would add:* the spoken form of self-assertion. It may serve better as a facet of self-assertion, or as a Gemini concept, than as a concept here.
- *Avenues:* high- and low-context communication (Hall); research on politeness and indirectness; feedback norms at work; regional reputations for niceness or frankness (the "Seattle Freeze"; "Midwestern nice"), as claims to test; business-culture guides on directness; how refusals are made, and how long they take.

### C8. Being first (pioneering)
- *Working definition:* the value placed on being the first to do, reach or try something, and on firsts as markers of honor.
- *Why here:* the research notes give pioneering ("the compulsion to move first and try the untested"), aspiration, an ambition "to be first right now", and glory (Achilles insisting on being first ashore). *Also fits:* Leo (glory), Capricorn (ambition).
- *What it would add:* beginning is about starts; this is about priority and the honor it carries, including "first in the family" achievements and disputes over who came first.
- *Avenues:* "firsts" in local histories and museums; first ascents and records; first-generation college students and the pride of being the first; priority disputes in science and invention; claims to have been first on the land, against the peoples who were there before; "early adopter" pride in technology.

### C9. Toughness and the proving of manhood
- *Working definition:* the demand to show strength, endurance and nerve to earn and keep standing, most of all as a man.
- *Why here:* the research notes give bravado ("a hard front"), contempt for weakness, self-neglect, and a fear of pain hidden under toughness. The investigation sheet's Aries angles include the body, appearance and manhood ideals, and the sheet gives manhood its own line because the author writes for men. *Also fits:* Leo (honor), Scorpio (power), Capricorn (age ways).
- *What it would add:* one home for manhood ideals now scattered across courage, anger, recklessness and the body, framed as questions to research for each group, never as types. Women's toughness ideals ("strong woman") are researched alongside.
- *Avenues:* precarious manhood (Vandello and Bosson, 2008); *machismo* and *caballerismo* (Arciniega et al., 2008); masculine-norms inventories (Mahalik's Conformity to Masculine Norms Inventory *check*); the masculinities of particular trades (logging, fishing, ranch work, the military); "man up", "boys don't cry" and their equivalents in each language; men's help-seeking (doctor visits, therapy use); fathers' advice to sons; initiation and hazing.

### C10. Aggression
- *Working definition:* acting to hurt, dominate or force others, by word or deed; distinct from the emotion of anger.
- *Why here:* the research notes give aggression, force, domination, bullying, and violence as "what Aries becomes when its self-belief is broken". *Also fits:* Scorpio (power, control), Pisces (wrongdoing).
- *What it would add:* anger is the feeling; aggression is the act. Cultures pull them apart (anger shown without aggression; aggression praised without anger, as in sport and sales), and a concept of its own would let that be researched.
- *Avenues:* honor-culture research (Nisbett and Cohen, *Culture of Honor*, 1996); surveys of bullying among schoolchildren; "aggressive" as praise in sport and business; hunting and predator metaphors; rough play, and how adults treat it; martial arts and their ethics of force.

### C11. Rebellion and defiance
- *Working definition:* openly refusing or resisting those who claim the right to direct one: parents, teachers, bosses, clergy, rulers.
- *Why here:* the research notes give rebellion (the natural rebel, Hotspur), disobedience, resistance to obedience, refusal of conformity, and disputes over legitimacy. *Also fits:* Aquarius (nonconformity, reform), Capricorn (authority and obedience).
- *What it would add:* autonomy is deciding for oneself; rebellion is the act against a particular authority, with its own heroes, songs and penalties.
- *Avenues:* youth rebellion and subcultures; survey items on conflict between teenagers and parents; rebel figures in film and song; "Don't tread on me" and other mottos of defiance; strikes and religious dissent; whether rebellion is taken as a phase, a virtue or a sin; how schools handle defiance.

### C12. Self-confidence
- *Working definition:* trust in one's own abilities and judgment, and how openly that trust may be shown.
- *Why here:* the research notes give self-confidence, conviction, optimism, and a faith in oneself that gets one back up after a fall. Self-worth is in Taurus's ten. *Also fits:* Taurus (self-worth), Leo (pride).
- *What it would add:* self-assertion is the act; confidence is the inner state behind it. Cultures differ on whether to build the self up or to criticize it (Heine et al., 1999).
- *Avenues:* self-esteem scales across groups; studies of self-enhancement and self-criticism; California's 1986 task force on self-esteem and the school self-esteem movement that followed *check*; confidence coaching; talk of "impostor syndrome"; studies of overconfidence; praise and its limits in child-rearing.

### C13. Individuality
- *Working definition:* being, and wanting to be, unlike others; the value placed on standing out.
- *Why here:* the research notes give individuality ("putting its own stamp on everything it does"), and an identity "built by stamping its own mark on what it does". *Also fits:* Aquarius (nonconformity), Leo (self-expression).
- *What it would add:* identity asks "who am I?"; individuality asks "how am I unlike the rest?", and it can be measured directly.
- *Avenues:* choosing the uncommon over the common (Kim and Markus, 1999, the study in which people chose a pen from a set); unique baby names; customized goods; fashion and conformity; "be yourself" slogans; need-for-uniqueness scales; the words a culture has against standing out (see C19).

### C14. Self-discovery
- *Working definition:* coming to know oneself, by looking inward, or by acting and seeing what one does.
- *Why here:* the research notes give self-discovery ("finding its strengths alone, on its own terms") and self-reflection, which the sign's framing avoids; the identity concept's facets include an identity stamped by deeds rather than found by looking inward. *Also fits:* Pisces (the hidden), Sagittarius (meaning).
- *What it would add:* whether a culture thinks the self is found by introspection, by deeds, by trials, or by the names and roles elders give.
- *Avenues:* journaling and therapy cultures; "finding yourself" journeys (road trips, gap years, the Camino de Santiago); personality tests and their popularity; quarter-life and midlife crises; seeking rites, as the nations share them; self-help genres.

### C15. Vulnerability
- *Working definition:* being open to harm, and letting others see one's weak or hurt side.
- *Why here:* the research notes give vulnerability "hidden behind bravado", intimacy refused, and shame as "the one thing that reliably breaks the type". *Also fits:* Pisces (compassion), Cancer (care), Scorpio (trust).
- *What it would add:* the other face of courage and self-assertion. The courage concept now notes only that therapy culture calls vulnerability courage.
- *Avenues:* men's disclosure and help-seeking; crying in public, and who may; therapy-culture books and their sales; disclosure on social media; proverbs on showing weakness; how injury and illness are spoken of at work.

### C16. Energy and vitality
- *Working definition:* the felt store of drive and liveliness: how it is valued, spent, shown and restored.
- *Why here:* the research notes give energy ("comes in bursts"), enthusiasm, busyness, burnout and a lack of stamina. Among the Ilongot (Bugkalot) of the Philippines, the word *liget* joins anger, energy and passion in one concept (Rosaldo, *Knowledge and Passion*, 1980, an outsider's account to read against the people's own). *Also fits:* Virgo (health, work), Leo (joy).
- *What it would add:* whether a culture prizes high, excited energy or calm (Tsai's ideal affect), and busyness as a mark of status.
- *Avenues:* Tsai's research on ideal affect; busyness as a status symbol (Bellezza, Paharia and Keinan, 2017 *check*); coffee and energy-drink cultures; talk of burnout; vacation days taken and left unused; morning people and night people.

### C17. Follow-through
- *Working definition:* carrying what one has started through to its end.
- *Why here:* the research notes call follow-through the sign's "chronic weakness", with persistence that "can manage a sprint but not a siege"; follow-through is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs. *Also fits:* Capricorn (discipline), Virgo (work), Taurus (stability).
- *What it would add:* the missing end of beginning and initiative: whether a culture honors its starters or its finishers.
- *Avenues:* proverbs on finishing; completion rates (college, apprenticeships); New Year's resolutions kept and dropped; "quiet quitting"; craft traditions that prize the finished piece; abandoned projects, and how people speak of them.

**From the Tier 2 concepts and the near-synonym groups** (`Shared/Concept_Lists_by_Sign.md`; the concept index's "Concepts under six or more signs" and "Names to reconcile later")

### C18. Endurance (*aguante*, *gaman*, *sisu*)
- *Working definition:* bearing hardship, pain or long effort without giving up or complaining.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group persistence, perseverance and endurance (endurance is under seven signs); the courage concept already asks whether enduring (*aguantar*) counts as courage. Several languages have a word English lacks: Mexican Spanish *aguante*; Japanese *gaman*, bearing the seemingly unbearable with dignity, a word Japanese-American internees used of their camp years *check*; Finnish *sisu*. *Also fits:* Capricorn (discipline), Taurus (stability), Pisces (suffering).
- *What it would add:* long effort and stoic bearing as a value of their own, apart from facing danger.
- *Avenues:* grit research (Duckworth's grit scale); *aguante* among football fans and in songs; the exhibit and book *The Art of Gaman* (Hirasuna, 2005 *check*); endurance sports; proverbs; stories of work and illness.

### C19. Humility and modesty
- *Working definition:* not claiming more than one's due; holding back from praising oneself.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group modesty and humility; the research notes say humility "has to be taught on purpose, against its pride". It is the opposite pole of self-assertion. Other languages name forms of it: the Scandinavian *Janteloven*, a code against standing out named in Aksel Sandemose's 1933 novel; Tagalog *hiya*, a restraining sense of shame; and the English "tall poppy". *Also fits:* Leo (pride), Capricorn (deference), Sagittarius (religious teaching).
- *What it would add:* a way to place a culture's ideal between assertion and restraint, which a concept of assertion alone can't show.
- *Avenues:* self-description studies (modesty in self-ratings); religious teaching on humility and plainness; boasting taboos; the speeches of athletes and other winners; job-interview norms; how compliments are taken or turned aside.

### C20. Self-centeredness and entitlement
- *Working definition:* putting oneself at the center of attention and concern, and expecting special treatment.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group egocentricity, self-absorption, selfishness and narcissism; the research notes give egocentricity, entitlement, and exemption (treating rules and chores as meant for ordinary people). *Also fits:* Leo (narcissism, being seen), Taurus (possessiveness).
- *What it would add:* the moral judgment of the self's excess, which no current concept holds whole.
- *Avenues:* the "narcissism epidemic" debate (Twenge and Campbell, *The Narcissism Epidemic*, 2009) and its critics; Narcissistic Personality Inventory scores over time; "selfish" in proverbs; the "me generation"; entitlement memes (cross-reference: self-assertion's "Karen"); claims about generations, tested against data.

### C21. Boredom and the need for excitement
- *Working definition:* the discomfort of too little challenge or novelty, and the drive to seek stimulation.
- *Why here:* the research notes give boredom, a hatred of routine, and excitement; boredom is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs. *Also fits:* Gemini (curiosity), Sagittarius (adventure), Pisces (escape).
- *What it would add:* the motive under beginning, initiative and recklessness.
- *Avenues:* boredom-proneness and sensation-seeking scales (Zuckerman); "nothing to do" among young people in small towns; novelty in what people buy; monotony at work; how boredom is judged (a sin of sloth, the sign of a dull mind, or normal).

**Concepts other languages name that English lacks** (besides those already in C4, C16, C18 and C19)

### C22. *Thymos* (spiritedness)
- *Working definition:* the ancient Greek word, in Plato's *Republic*, for the part of the soul that demands recognition and flares into anger when slighted.
- *Why here:* one word that joins self-assertion, anger, pride and identity; Fukuyama uses it to explain the politics of identity (*Identity*, 2018). *Also fits:* Leo (recognition, honor).
- *What it would add:* a lens for testing whether a culture ties anger to slighted worth.
- *Avenues:* dignity, honor and face cultures (Leung and Cohen, 2011); anger at disrespect, in surveys and ethnographies; the language of "respect" in politics and on the street (Anderson, *Code of the Street*, 1999).

### C23. *Chutzpah* (audacity)
- *Working definition:* the Yiddish word, taken into Hebrew and American English, for audacious nerve, admired or condemned by turns.
- *Why here:* it blends self-assertion, courage and recklessness, where English splits the same quality into praise ("nerve", "boldness") and blame ("gall", "cheek"). *Also fits:* Leo (being seen).
- *What it would add:* whether a culture has one word for bold self-assertion that can be good or bad, and when it tips from one to the other.
- *Avenues:* its use in American English; Israeli self-descriptions (Senor and Singer, *Start-up Nation*, 2009, as synthesis); humor; Spanish counterparts (*atrevido*, *descarado*, *conchudo*) and their tone in each region.

### C24. *Amae* (welcome dependence)
- *Working definition:* the Japanese word for presuming on another's indulgence, as a child presumes on a parent's; dependence felt as warm and right (Doi, *The Anatomy of Dependence*, 1973 in English).
- *Why here:* the counter-concept to independence: it shows dependence can be a value, not only a lack. *Also fits:* Cancer (nurture), Libra (partnership).
- *What it would add:* a way to ask which kinds of dependence a culture welcomes, which a concept of independence alone can't see.
- *Avenues:* Doi; attachment research across cultures; words for welcome indulgence in the unit's languages (Mexican Spanish *apapachar*, to cuddle and pamper; *consentir*, to spoil); *familismo* (under Independence); elders' dependence on their grown children.

### C25. *Ganas* (drive)
- *Working definition:* the Spanish word for wanting and the will to act, as in *echarle ganas* ("put everything into it").
- *Why here:* it names the inner force of initiative more bodily than "drive" or "motivation" do, and it is central to how Mexicans and Mexican Americans talk about school and work (in the 1988 film *Stand and Deliver*, the teacher Jaime Escalante tells his East Los Angeles students that *ganas* is all they need). *Also fits:* Virgo (work), Leo.
- *What it would add:* the motive behind initiative and competition, in a word the pilot's languages already have.
- *Avenues:* *ganas* in school and sports speeches; parents' advice; songs; studies of motivation among students of Mexican origin; comparison with "hustle" and "grit".

### C26. Resourcefulness (making do)
- *Working definition:* getting things done with whatever is at hand, by improvising.
- *Why here:* initiative and independence meet in it, and other languages have words for it: Portuguese *desenrascanço* (improvising a way out of a mess); *rasquachismo*, the Chicano aesthetic of making do (Ybarra-Frausto, 1989 *check*); New Zealand's "number 8 wire" for farm improvising. *Also fits:* Virgo (improvement, repair), Gemini (cleverness).
- *What it would add:* initiative under scarcity, which the current concepts don't separate from enterprise.
- *Avenues:* cultures of repair and reuse; do-it-yourself and maker culture; Chicano art and murals in the *rasquache* style; improvisation on farms and fishing boats; "MacGyver" in everyday speech; studies of informal work.

**Splits of the current concepts**

### C27. Moral courage (split from courage)
- *Working definition:* doing what one believes right against social cost: speaking out, refusing, standing alone.
- *Why here:* courage now holds physical and moral courage together; German *Zivilcourage* ("civil courage") names the second as a value of its own. *Also fits:* Libra (justice), Sagittarius (conviction).
- *What it would add:* exemplars, rewards and measures of its own (whistleblowers, dissenters, people who hid others from persecution), kept apart from soldiers and rescuers.
- *Avenues:* awards for moral courage (see Courage, Institutions); whistleblowers' memoirs; research on those who rescued Jews in Nazi Europe (Oliner and Oliner, *The Altruistic Personality*, 1988); bystander-intervention training; school lessons on standing up for others.

### C28. Heroism and rescue (split from courage)
- *Working definition:* acting at risk to oneself to save others, and the figure of the hero a community makes from it.
- *Why here:* the research notes give heroism, rescue ("it wants both to rescue and to be the one rescued"), protection, and service in emergency careers. *Also fits:* Leo (honor, glory), Cancer (protection), Pisces (self-sacrifice).
- *What it would add:* courage is about facing fear; heroism is the saving act and its public recognition, which has institutions of its own (hero funds, medals, the word "hero" in the news).
- *Avenues:* civilian bravery awards (see Courage, Institutions); who is called a hero in the news, and when the word is withheld; superhero media; rescue careers and their recruiting; everyday rescues (CPR by bystanders, lifeguards, Good Samaritans on the road).

### C29. Origins (split from beginning)
- *Working definition:* where a people, a place, a family or a thing is held to come from, and what that origin is taken to settle.
- *Why here:* beginning now holds both origins and fresh starts, which point opposite ways: back to roots, or forward to a new life. *Also fits:* Capricorn (tradition and heritage), Cancer (roots).
- *What it would add:* founding stories, "who was here first", and claims made from origins, as a concept of their own; beginning would keep the fresh start.
- *Avenues:* creation and origin stories, as their owners share them; founding legends and founders' days; family origin stories; first-settler societies; the opening galleries of museums; disputes over who came first.

### C30. Righteous anger (split from anger)
- *Working definition:* anger at a wrong, felt as justified, and often shared by a group.
- *Why here:* anger now holds temper, rage and moral outrage together, and some languages make the moral kind the main one (the Ifaluk word *song*, "justifiable anger", in Lutz, *Unnatural Emotions*, 1988, an outsider's account to read critically). The concept index groups anger, rage, temper, indignation and irritation. *Also fits:* Libra (fairness), Aquarius (reform).
- *What it would add:* a line between personal temper and collective indignation, which draw different rewards and sanctions.
- *Avenues:* the rhetoric of protest; sermons on righteous anger; research on moral outrage; which groups are granted indignation, and which are called "angry".

### C31. Grudges and grievance (split from anger)
- *Working definition:* holding on to a wrong over time, privately or as a group.
- *Why here:* the research notes call resentment absent from the sign's framing (anger discharged at once, nothing left to fester), which makes its presence or absence a question for each culture. Grudge is a Tier 2 concept, under nine signs, in the near-synonym group grudge, grievance and resentment. *Also fits:* Scorpio (revenge), Pisces.
- *What it would add:* the long time of anger, which the anger concept touches only in passing.
- *Avenues:* feuds and how they end; "forgive and forget" against "never forget"; historical grievances in politics; the Korean *han*, a long-held sorrow and resentment, as a contrast; estrangement in families.

### C32. Winning and losing (split from competition)
- *Working definition:* how winners and losers are expected to behave, and what victory and defeat mean.
- *Why here:* the research notes give victory ("the need to win can outweigh what is actually at stake") and rivalry; competition now holds the striving and its outcome together. *Also fits:* Leo (pride, humiliation).
- *What it would add:* sportsmanship, gloating, grace in defeat and the shame of losing, as a topic of their own.
- *Avenues:* post-game handshakes and rituals; concession speeches; teaching children to be "good sports"; sore-loser stories; participation trophies; how losing candidates, teams and towns are treated afterward.

### C33. Rivalry (split from competition)
- *Working definition:* a lasting contest between particular persons, towns, schools, firms or peoples.
- *Why here:* the research notes give rivalry ("fire against fire over who leads"); competition now holds one-time contests and standing rivalries together. *Also fits:* Libra (open enemies), Leo.
- *What it would add:* the rivalries a place defines itself by (Seattle and Portland; Los Angeles and San Francisco; the rivalry games under Competition).
- *Avenues:* rivalry games and their lore; jokes about the next town over; sibling rivalry; business rivalries; how rivalries begin and end.

### C34. Risk and caution (split from recklessness)
- *Working definition:* how much danger and uncertainty people accept, and the value placed on prudence.
- *Why here:* recklessness names only the excess. The research notes give risk ("staking everything on one throw") and caution, which the sign's framing reads as cowardice; caution is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs. Russian *avos'* (trusting to luck instead of preparing) and Spanish *si Dios quiere* show fatalism as a third stance beside daring and caution. *Also fits:* Taurus (security), Capricorn (responsibility).
- *What it would add:* most measures frame risk neutrally (risk-taking in the Global Preferences Survey). A neutral concept would hold them, and leave recklessness for the excess.
- *Avenues:* risk-preference surveys; insurance uptake; seatbelt and helmet use; saving and investing choices; safety culture and its critics; proverbs of caution and of daring.

### C35. Collective self-rule (split from autonomy)
- *Working definition:* a group deciding for itself: a town, a region, a congregation, a people.
- *Why here:* autonomy's facets now include towns and Native nations governing themselves, but personal and collective autonomy can pull apart: a culture may prize one and not the other. *Also fits:* Aquarius (community), Capricorn (authority).
- *What it would add:* self-government as a value in its own right, apart from personal choice.
- *Avenues:* town meetings and local control; *usos y costumbres* and *ejido* assemblies; congregations that govern themselves; the nations' own accounts of self-determination, as they describe them; regional autonomy movements; surveys of trust in local against distant government.

### C36. Personal and group identity (split of identity)
- *Working definition:* personal identity (who one is as a single person) and group identity (the peoples, places and faiths one belongs to).
