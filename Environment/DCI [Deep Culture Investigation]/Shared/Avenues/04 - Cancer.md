# Avenues of investigation: 04 Cancer (home and family)

**What this file is.** For each Cancer concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Cancer concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; each sign's list is settled with the author just before its pass): home, family, belonging, nurture, protection, shelter, care, abandonment, motherhood. Care was settled under Cancer by the author (2026-11-02). Belonging is also in Aquarius's ten; its proposed home is Cancer, so it has a full section here. Security, also in Cancer's ten, has its proposed home in Taurus and is a cross-reference at the end. Questions to settle:
- **Near-synonyms.** Nurture and care: the working split here is that nurture fosters growth (above all a child's), and care meets the needs of anyone who can't meet them alone. Home and shelter: home is the place lived from and what it means; shelter is refuge from danger and the elements, and the duty to give it. Protection and security: protection is the act of guarding; security (Taurus) is the state of being safe.
- **Motherhood without fatherhood.** The sign's traditional area is "parents", but the list names only mothers. Fathering is covered in section 0 for now; the author may want a "fatherhood" or "parenthood" entry.
- **Missing from the list:** roots and homesickness, hospitality, kinship and ancestry, the privacy of the home, childhood, loyalty. Section 0 covers them for now.

## 0. The domain: home and family

**Traditional areas:** home, family, parents, roots, nurture. **The domain question:** what is home, and who is family?

- **Household forms.** Who lives under one roof and who heads it: people living alone, couples, nuclear families, extended and multigenerational households, households of unrelated people, compounds of related houses. Census household and family tables (U.S. Census and ACS; Statistics Canada; INEGI's *hogares nucleares, ampliados y compuestos* by *municipio*); historical household typologies (Laslett and Wall, *Household and Family in Past Time*, 1972); Todd's family systems; the Hajnal line (1965); descent and post-marital residence codes in Murdock's *Ethnographic Atlas* (via D-PLACE), which are outsiders' codings to check against the nations' own accounts; the Human Relations Area Files, searched by the Outline of Cultural Materials categories for family, kinship, structures, settlements, infancy, socialization and old age (590, 600, 340, 360, 850, 860, 880 *check*).
- **Multigenerational living.** Grandparents, parents and children under one roof or on one lot: ACS multigenerational households; Statistics Canada's 2021 census counts of multigenerational households; Pew's reports on multigenerational living (2022 *check*); house forms built for it (the Vancouver Special, the basement suite, the *casita*, the granny flat); whether it is chosen, owed, a way to save money or a sign of failure.
- **Parents and fathering** (the list has motherhood but not fatherhood):
  - what a father is for: provider, protector, disciplinarian, teacher, nurturer, playmate;
  - words: "dad", "pops", "old man", "family man", "deadbeat"; Mexican Spanish *papá*, *apá*, *jefe* (slang for one's father), *el viejo*, *proveedor*; the asymmetry between *¡qué padre!* (how great) and the insults built on *madre* (see Motherhood);
  - Father's Day (U.S., permanent since 1972; Mexico, the third Sunday in June); paternity leave and whether fathers take it (California's Paid Family Leave program; Canada's extra parental weeks reserved for a second parent, 2019; Mexico's five days *check*);
  - measures: fathers' time with children (ATUS); father involvement (NSFG); Pew's surveys of fathers; the Traditional Machismo and Caballerismo Scale (Arciniega et al., 2008); fathers living apart from their children (CPS; ACS);
  - fathers' support groups and the fathers' movement; responsible-fatherhood programs; Indigenous fatherhood programs as the nations run them; obituaries that praise fathers; the absent father (see Abandonment); the respect (*respeto*) and authority owed to parents (Capricorn).
- **Roots.** Staying put against moving on: Stegner's "boomers" and "stickers" in the West (*Where the Bluebird Sings to the Lemonade Springs*, 1992 *check*); *patria chica*, *terruño*, *querencia*; hometown pride; family land (Oregon's Century Farm and Ranch Program *check*; *ejido* plots passed down); the cemetery where the family lies; return migration; hometown associations. Measures: the share living in their birth state (ACS); Pew's *Who Moves? Who Stays Put? Where's Home?* (2008); Statistics Canada's mobility tables; INEGI's place-of-birth and migration items. Homesickness and nostalgia (the word "nostalgia" was coined as a medical diagnosis by Johannes Hofer in 1688). Indigenous ties to territory, as the nations state them in their own publications, maps and place names.
- **Family memory.** Photo albums, family Bibles, heirlooms, recipes, stories retold at gatherings; who keeps them (the family's memory-keeper); genealogy (FamilySearch, run by the Latter-day Saints; DNA tests); family reunions; obituaries as family history. Heritage and tradition may belong to Capricorn and inheritance to Scorpio: to settle.
- **Hospitality at home.** *Mi casa es su casa*; feeding every visitor; open-door households; potlucks; who may drop in unannounced; the feast and the potlatch as hosting, as the Northwest Coast nations describe them, and how families kept it alive in secret through the decades of suppression (1884-1951). Hospitality is on no sign's list.
- **The home's walls.** Who may enter which rooms; front yards against back yards; walled and gated houses against open front lawns (a question to research on both sides of the border); curtains and fences (Frost, "Mending Wall", 1914); the home as a private sphere ("a man's house is his castle"; what neighbors may see, hear and say) and the end of that privacy as a shield for violence in the home.
- **Family meals and the kitchen.** How often families eat together (NSCH; ATUS eating with household members *check*); Sunday dinner; *sobremesa*; the Sunday *carne asada* of northern Mexico as a family gathering (*check*); the kitchen as the heart of the house; who cooks. Food itself is Taurus's (food ways).
- **The home's emotional climate.** Warmth, quarrels, silences; how family conflict is handled; measures such as the Family Environment Scale (Moos *check*) and the family-conflict items in child surveys.
- **What newcomers are told.** Immigrant orientation guides from the U.S. and Canadian governments (*check* titles), tenants' guides, consulates' and migrant shelters' material: what they say to expect about homes, renting, family life and children.
- **Building ways** (Fischer's folkway for Cancer):
  - house types and what they say: the Craftsman bungalow; the Victorian; the ranch house (Cliff May; *Sunset* magazine, published since 1898, as the West's guide to house and garden); Eichler homes; the Vancouver Special (about 1965-1985); laneway houses (Vancouver, 2009) and California's backyard ADUs (built in large numbers since the late 2010s); the "monster house" disputes in Vancouver in the 1980s and 1990s (Katharyne Mitchell, *Crossing the Neoliberal Line*, 2004); trailers and mobile homes; houseboats and float homes; the family cabin;
  - the Spanish and Mexican line: adobe; the courtyard house; Californio rancho houses; the flat-roofed northern house (*check* a regional term); the *jacal*; the *ramada* or *enramada* for shade; the self-built, room-by-room house (*autoconstrucción*, with rebar left standing on the roof for the next floor); INFONAVIT tract housing;
  - Indigenous dwellings, as the nations present them: Northwest Coast cedar plank houses and Coast Salish longhouses (still used for ceremony); Plateau pit houses (reconstructed at cultural centers); tule-mat lodges; Yurok plank houses (Sumêg Village, in Sue-meg State Park, renamed in 2021); California roundhouses; Great Basin brush houses; Yuman shade structures;
  - layout: who sleeps where (children sharing rooms; a room for the grandparent); the front room kept for guests; the porch; where the kitchen sits; garages turned into dwellings;
  - how houses are built: self-building with kin, house-raisings, contractors, tract developers; house-post and pole raisings, as the nations share them;
  - meetinghouses as Fischer counts them: the mission church, the longhouse, the grange hall;
  - sources and measures: ACS units in structure, year built, rooms and bedrooms; the American Housing Survey; INEGI's census items on wall, roof and floor materials (*piso de tierra*); Statistics Canada's dwelling types; the Census Bureau's Survey of Construction (new-house size); building permits; the Historic American Buildings Survey; McAlester, *A Field Guide to American Houses* (1984; 2013); studies of house numbers and price in Vancouver (Fortin, Hill and Huang, 2014 *check*);
  - runner-up Taurus (property and land).
- **Family ways** (Fischer's folkway for Cancer):
  - who counts as kin, kin terms, descent (through the father, the mother or both), house and clan systems as the Northwest Coast nations describe them, ritual kin (*compadrazgo*; Mintz and Wolf, 1950), in-laws, family size, authority within the family (age ways, Capricorn);
  - Fischer's angle: how the family is ordered, how large it is, how far it reaches, how it ties into the wider kin;
  - classics: Fischer for the four British founders; Larissa Lomnitz, *Networks and Marginality* (1977); Lomnitz and Pérez-Lizaur, *A Mexican Elite Family* (1987); Oscar Lewis, *Five Families* (1959; contested); Weston, *Families We Choose* (1991, the San Francisco Bay Area);
  - measures: see Family.
- **Child-rearing ways** (Fischer's folkway for Cancer):
  - Fischer's angle: what a child's will is and what is done with it: broken, bent, nurtured or strengthened across his four founding cultures (Fischer; Greven, *The Protestant Temperament*, 1977 *check* which goes with which);
  - parental ethnotheories and the developmental niche (Harkness and Super); *Children of Six Cultures* (Whiting and Whiting, 1975), which included a Mixtec town in Oaxaca and a New England town; Rogoff's work with Mexican-heritage and Indigenous American families on learning by observing and pitching in;
  - infant care: where babies sleep; carrying, and cradleboards as the nations share them; breastfeeding; swaddling; *la cuarentena*, the forty days of rest after birth;
  - discipline: spanking, how common it is and whether parents defend it (the GSS item, by region; the child-discipline items in UNICEF's MICS surveys, which in Mexico is ENIM *check*); *respeto*, *educación* and *consejos*;
  - what parents want children to become (WVS child qualities); children's chores and work; obedience against independence;
  - advice literature: Spock (1946); Dobson, *Dare to Discipline* (1970); attachment parenting; "gentle parenting" online;
  - Indigenous child-rearing teachings, as the nations publish them, and the boarding and residential schools that broke them;
  - runner-up Leo (play, and children as joy).
- **Gender ways** (runner-up; home sign Libra): who keeps house, who mothers and who fathers, the split of household work; see `07 - Libra.md`.

## 1. Home

**Working definition:** the place a person or group lives from and returns to, and what it means to them: a dwelling, a household's base, and the wider place called home.
**Facets:** the dwelling; the household that lives in it; home as a feeling (ease, safety, being oneself); home as origin (hometown, homeland, "back home"); home as territory; owning against renting; keeping house; losing a home (eviction, foreclosure, fire, displacement).
**Neighbors and opposites:** house, household, hearth, dwelling, homeland, place; the road, exile, displacement, homelessness, the public square. **Excess:** insularity; the home as a cage; house pride turned to exclusion (NIMBYism). **Deficiency:** rootlessness, never settling.

- **Words:** English splits "home" from "house"; Spanish *casa* covers both (*estoy en casa*), and *hogar* (from the hearth fire *check*) is the warmer, more formal word; "home sweet home", "homey" (in the U.S., cozy; "homely" means plain-looking in the U.S. and cozy in Britain), "homebody", "house-proud", "homemaker", "starter home", "forever home", "back home", "homie" (from "homeboy": someone from one's own neighborhood); "unhoused" replacing "homeless" in some West Coast official usage; Canadian "basement suite" and "mortgage helper"; Spanish *mi casa es su casa*, *estás en tu casa* (said to a guest), *la casa chica* (a man's second, hidden household), *el terruño*, *la patria chica*, *querencia* (a beloved place one returns to *check*); Chinuk Wawa *illahee* (land, country; in many place names *check*); the Northwest Coast "house" that is both a building and a kin group (see Family).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is home a building, a household, a people or a land? Owning a home as a moral good and the heart of the American Dream (the "Own Your Own Home" campaigns of the 1920s *check*; federal mortgage policy) against housing as shelter that should not be a commodity; the home as women's sphere (Welter, "The Cult of True Womanhood", 1966) and its critics (Friedan, *The Feminine Mystique*, 1963); the home as holy (the "domestic church" of Catholic teaching, *Lumen Gentium* 11; the Latter-day Saints' teaching of the home as a sacred place; home altars); the homeland as sacred ground, as Indigenous nations state their relationships to their territories; thinkers on home and dwelling (Bachelard, *The Poetics of Space*, 1958; Heidegger, "Building Dwelling Thinking", 1951; Tuan, *Topophilia*, 1974; Relph, *Place and Placelessness*, 1976; Rybczynski, *Home: A Short History of an Idea*, 1986; Mary Douglas, "The Idea of a Home", 1991).
- **Feeling and the body:** comfort and ease; shoes off or on indoors; the smells of home cooking; heat and cold (heating norms; evaporative "coolers" in the Sonoran heat *check*); clutter and order (UCLA's study of 32 Los Angeles family homes, *Life at Home in the Twenty-First Century*, Arnold et al., 2012); noise and quiet hours; homesickness felt as illness.
- **Everyday practice:** housekeeping and who does it (ATUS; INEGI's ENUT); decorating (family photos, religious images, flags, the home altar); yards and gardens (lawns against drought gardens; front-yard vegetable plots); entertaining at home against going out; locking the door or not; renovating and DIY; working from home; pets as household members; hosting kin for holidays; moving often or never.
- **Life course:** the childhood home; the age of leaving home (Aries, independence); buying a first home (the age of first-time buyers in realtors' and mortgage surveys *check*); marrying and setting up house; the "empty nest"; downsizing; aging in place against moving in with children or into care; dying at home (place of death in CDC data); selling or inheriting the family house.
- **Ritual:** housewarmings; house blessings (*la bendición de la casa*; chalking the door at Epiphany); carrying the bride over the threshold; going "home" for Thanksgiving and Christmas (AAA's holiday travel forecasts); Homecoming at U.S. high schools and colleges; Northwest Coast house-front and pole raisings, as the nations share them; the return of ancestors and belongings to their home communities (repatriation; the U'mista Cultural Centre in Alert Bay, opened in 1980 to receive returned potlatch regalia, whose name the center explains as the return of something important *check*).
- **Institutions:** mortgage and housing agencies (the FHA and Fannie Mae; CMHC; INFONAVIT and FOVISSSTE; public housing authorities; BC Housing); band housing departments on reserves and tribal housing authorities in the U.S.; homeowners' associations, neighborhood associations and tenant unions; realtors and their associations (the National Association of Realtors; the Canadian Real Estate Association; Mexico's AMPI *check*); builders, architects and home inspectors; Habitat for Humanity (a Christian house-building ministry, founded in 1976 *check*) and church work crews; home economics and shop classes in schools; home and garden media (*Sunset*; HGTV); county extension services and their master-gardener programs.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for a well-kept house ("Yard of the Month"); HOA fines and neighbors' complaints; the shame of a dirty house; the stigma of renting, of trailer parks ("trailer trash") and of homelessness; neighbors' fights over new housing (NIMBY against YIMBY); foreclosure and eviction as disgrace.
- **Expression:** sayings ("Home is where the heart is"; "There's no place like home"; *Cada quien en su casa y Dios en la de todos*; *Casa de herrero, cuchillo de palo*); songs ("Home on the Range"; "Canción mixteca", 1915 *check*, the classic song of a migrant's longing; "Volver, volver", 1972); literature (Marilynne Robinson, *Housekeeping*, 1980, set in Idaho; Joan Didion, *Where I Was From*, 2003; D. J. Waldie, *Holy Land*, 1996, on a postwar Los Angeles County tract; Sandra Cisneros, *The House on Mango Street*, 1984; Eden Robinson, *Monkey Beach*, 2000, Haisla territory; Tommy Orange, *There There*, 2018, Native Oakland); *Sunset* magazine; home-makeover television and real-estate browsing as entertainment; *The Wizard of Oz* (1939).
- **Exemplars:** homemakers and home designers (Cliff May; Joseph Eichler; *Sunset*'s editors); homesteaders; tenant organizers and housing activists; anti-exemplars: slumlords, flippers, absentee owners.
- **History:** Indigenous seasonal rounds (winter villages and summer camps: home as a cycle of places, as the nations describe it); the Spanish missions gathering Native people into fixed settlements; Californio ranchos; homesteading and the settlers' land rush of the 1850s and 1860s; redlining (the HOLC maps of Seattle, Portland, Los Angeles, Oakland and San Francisco, in the Mapping Inequality project) and racial covenants (the University of Washington's Seattle covenants database); Japanese American and Japanese Canadian removal (1942) and the homes lost; the Vanport flood (Portland, 1948); urban renewal and freeways (Portland's Albina; Los Angeles's Chavez Ravine; Vancouver's Hogan's Alley); the postwar tract suburb; the 2008 foreclosure wave; Mexico's 2000s housing boom and its abandoned tract homes (*check*); homes lost to wildfire (Paradise, 2018; Lytton, 2021; Los Angeles, January 2025) and insurers pulling back from California (2023).
- **Measures:** homeownership (ACS by county and tract; the Census Bureau's Housing Vacancies and Homeownership survey; Statistics Canada's census tenure; INEGI's census *vivienda propia*); years in the current home and "same house one year ago" (ACS); cost burden (HUD's CHAS data; Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies; CMHC's core housing need); crowding (ACS persons per room); housing quality (INEGI; CONEVAL's housing dimension of poverty); satisfaction with the dwelling (Statistics Canada's Canadian Housing Survey; INEGI's ENVI 2020 *check*); time spent at home (ATUS); Pew's 2008 mobility report (where people say "home" is); Google Trends ("homesick", "home decor").
- **Variation:** owners and renters; urban, suburban and rural; class; generation (young adults priced out); immigrants (crowded and shared housing); on and off reserve; coastal and inland California; the unhoused.
- **Relations:** shelter (the refuge), family, belonging, roots (section 0); security and possession (Taurus); privacy and inheritance (Scorpio); travel and freedom (Sagittarius) as the pull away from home; patriotism and the homeland (not yet placed).
- **Who it applies to:** animals (habitat; a pet's "forever home"); the dead (graves as homes; the "eternal home"); spirits and haunted houses; teams ("home field"); nations (the homeland); smart homes: present-day attitudes to voice assistants, home cameras and robot vacuums (Pew on smart speakers and privacy *check*).
- **Silences:** violence behind closed doors; hoarding; hidden homelessness (doubling up, couch-surfing); housing wealth as an unspoken divide; whose land the house stands on (land acknowledgments).
- **Change:** the housing-cost crisis; remote work; ADUs and multigenerational remodeling; short-term rentals; climate displacement and insurers' retreat; "unhoused" as a new word; vanlife.
- **The senses:** the smells that mean home (a family's cooking; wood smoke; tortillas on the *comal*; cedar; the smell of a new house, or of a house shut up after a death); what home sounds like (the kitchen radio; the television always on; rain on the roof through a Northwest winter; street vendors' calls and whistles in Mexican neighborhoods, such as the sweet-potato seller's steam whistle and the gas truck's jingle *check*; quiet hours, thin walls and noise complaints); light and dark (curtains open or drawn; the porch light left on; holiday lights; lamplight against the long dark of northern winters); warmth as comfort (how warm a house is kept; wood stoves; evaporative coolers in the desert); what is worn at home (slippers, bare feet, shoes left at the door) and the textures of home (quilts, wool blankets, the heavy plush Mexican blanket sold under the San Marcos name *check*); the tastes migrants mean by "back home"; homesickness brought on by a smell or a song.

## 2. Family

**Working definition:** the people a person counts as their own, by blood, marriage, adoption, ritual or choice, and what they owe one another.
**Facets:** the nuclear family; extended kin; the household against the wider kin network; descent and lineage (through fathers, mothers or both); in-laws; ritual kin (godparents, *compadres*); adoptive, foster and step families; chosen family; the family as an economic unit (the family farm or business); the family as a moral ideal and a political slogan; the dead as family.

## 3. Belonging

**Key questions:** What makes a newcomer belong, and how long does it take? Is belonging given by blood, by place, by papers or by contribution?

## 4. Nurture

**Working definition:** raising another toward growth, above all a child: feeding, warming, teaching and shaping them toward who they should become.
**Facets:** feeding and nourishment; affection and warmth; moral formation and manners; teaching skills; encouraging talent; mentoring the young adult; who nurtures (parents, grandparents, older siblings, godparents, the community, schools, paid carers); "nature against nurture" (what upbringing can do); tending animals, plants and land.
**Neighbors and opposites:** upbringing, rearing, fostering, mentoring, cultivation, nourishment; neglect, deprivation, coldness; "nature". **Excess:** coddling, spoiling, helicopter parenting, smothering (care that keeps the young from growing up). **Deficiency:** neglect, emotional coldness.

- **Words:** "nurture", "raise", "bring up", "rear", "foster", "nourish", "tend", "cultivate", "mentor", "coddle", "spoil", "tough love", "helicopter parent", "nature versus nurture"; Spanish *criar* and *crianza* (upbringing; also the aging of wine), *educar* and *educación* (in Mexican usage, upbringing in manners and respect, not only schooling), *bien educado*, *malcriado*, *consentir*, *chiquear* (Mexican: to pamper *check*), *apapachar* (Mexican: to cuddle and comfort; from Nahuatl *check*), *formar*, *dar consejos*, *hijos de crianza* (children raised by, but not born to, a family), *pilmama* (Mexican: nursemaid; from Nahuatl *check*), *nodriza* (wet nurse).
- **Beliefs and standing:** what children need, in the culture's own theory (parental ethnotheories: Harkness and Super); whether a child is born good, sinful or blank (original sin; Locke; Rousseau); breaking, bending or nurturing the will (section 0); "spare the rod"; nature against nurture in public belief (what people think genes decide); "it takes a village"; nurturing as a mother's nature or as a skill anyone can learn; Catholic teaching that parents are their children's first educators; *educación* as the parents' job and schooling as the school's (Valdés, *Con respeto*, 1996; Reese et al., 1995 *check*); Indigenous teachings on raising children and the place of grandparents, as the nations publish them; the child who became economically worthless but emotionally priceless (Zelizer, *Pricing the Priceless Child*, 1985).

## 5. Protection

**Working definition:** keeping someone or something from harm: guarding, defending and shielding the vulnerable, one's own and oneself.
**Facets:** physical defense of self, family and home; protecting children; protecting women (chivalry, and its critics); protecting the weak, the sick and the old; protecting property; protecting a reputation; protecting land, water and species; protection by the state (the military, emergency services, social protection); protection rackets; overprotection.
**Neighbors and opposites:** defense, guarding, watching over, safety, shelter; exposure, neglect, harm, predation, abandonment. **Excess:** overprotection, paternalism, control, vigilantism. **Deficiency:** neglect, failure to protect.

- **Words:** "protect", "guard", "defend", "shield", "look out for", "watch over", "have your back", "keep safe", "protective", "overprotective", "mama bear", "provider and protector", "protection" as a racket; Spanish *proteger*, *amparar* and *amparo* (shelter and protection), *resguardar*, *cuidar* (*te cuido*), *cuidar las espaldas*, *defender*, *derecho de piso* and *cobro de piso* (the "fee" criminals extort for "protection"), *padrino* as a protector, *palanca* (a protecting connection), *Protección Civil* (Mexico's emergency agency), *autodefensas* (armed community self-defense groups, mostly in southern and western Mexico), *Dios te guarde*; *mal de ojo* (the evil eye) and the amulets that guard babies against it (*check* regional forms).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is guarding one's own a duty, a right, or the state's job? ## 6. Shelter

**Working definition:** cover and refuge from danger and the elements: a roof, a safe place for those who have none, and the duty to give it.
**Facets:** the dwelling as shelter; emergency shelter (disaster, homelessness); refuge for the persecuted (sanctuary, asylum); shelters for people fleeing violence; lodging strangers and travelers; sheltering in place; animal shelters; a "sheltered" upbringing; a tax shelter.
**Neighbors and opposites:** refuge, haven, sanctuary, asylum, cover, roof, housing; exposure, homelessness, eviction, expulsion. **Excess:** a sheltered life that leaves a person unready; institutions that warehouse people. **Deficiency:** exposure; refusing refuge.

- **Words:** "shelter", "a roof over one's head", "refuge", "haven", "safe haven", "sanctuary", "asylum", "harbor", "shelter in place", "homeless", "unhoused", "houseless", "encampment", "tent city", "sweep" (clearing an encampment), "Hooverville", "doubled up", "couch-surfing", "sheltered" (naive); Spanish *refugio*, *albergue* (a shelter; *albergue para migrantes*), *casa del migrante*, *techo* (*tener un techo*), *abrigo*, *cobijo* (and *cobija*, a blanket in Mexico), *amparo*, *asilo* (asylum and, in Mexico, also an old people's home), *posada* (an inn; *dar posada*, to give lodging), *personas en situación de calle* (the official Mexican term for homeless people), *indigente*, *paracaidistas* (squatters; literally "parachutists"), *invasión* (a squatter settlement).

## 8. Abandonment

**Working definition:** leaving someone or something one was bound to care for or stay with; and the fear, and the experience, of being left.
**Facets:** abandoning children (foundlings; babies left at hospitals or in baby boxes); deserting a spouse or a family; abandoning elders; the family left behind by migration; abandonment by the state (neglected places, broken promises); abandoning animals; abandoned places (ghost towns, empty houses); deserting a post; the fear of abandonment (attachment); being "ghosted".
**Neighbors and opposites:** desertion, neglect, rejection, leaving, estrangement, betrayal; loyalty, steadfastness, commitment, belonging. **The other side:** the fear of being left, at its extreme, becomes clinging and possessiveness; refusing ever to leave can trap people in harmful homes.

- **Words:** "abandon", "desert", "walk out", "run off", "skip town", "deadbeat dad", "absent father", "dumped", "ditched", "ghosted", "left behind", "orphan", "foundling", "latchkey kid", "throwaway kids", "abandonment issues", "left at the altar"; Spanish *abandonar*, *abandono*, *dejar* (*me dejó*), *desamparar* and *desamparo*, *huérfano*, *expósito* (a foundling, historical), *niños de la calle*, *padre ausente*, *dejar plantado*, *abandono de hogar* (leaving the marital home), *padre desobligado* (a father who shirks his duties *check*), *los que se quedan* (those left behind by migration), terms for the wives of absent migrants (*check* regional terms).

## 9. Motherhood

## Candidate subtopics (PROPOSED, for the author to settle)

**What this section is.** Subtopics this meta-category could hold beyond its nine proposed concepts (the list itself is not yet settled; security is a cross-reference), explored for the author (2026-11-10: "One sign, explore possibilities for the subtopics, go in as much depth and detail as you need"). Every entry is PROPOSED. The concept list above stays as it is. The author decides whether each candidate becomes a concept, a facet of a current concept, a concept in another sign, or nothing. Each entry gives a working definition; why it belongs here, and which other sign could hold it; what it would add; and a few avenues. The candidates come from five places: the sign's traditional areas and folkways; the Cancer research notes (CurrentNovelDocs, `worldbuilding/Robot_Fundamental_Concepts/01 - The twelve meta-categories/04 - Cancer (home and family)/1 - Human understanding (research)/Low-level concepts (2026-10-30).md`, 153 concepts, and the gaps its notes file lists, among them architecture, music, funeral practice, the economy, and strangers and refugees at scale); the Tier 2 concepts and near-synonym groups (`Shared/Concept_Lists_by_Sign.md` and the concept index it cites); concepts other languages name that English lacks; and splits and merges of the current concepts. Candidates on marriage, children, adoption, inheritance, housing and membership are framed through custom, kinship, feeling, ritual, everyday practice, community, churches, schools and government services only.

**From the sign's traditional areas and folkways** (home, family, parents, roots, nurture; building ways, family ways and child-rearing ways, with gender ways as runner-up; parents and roots have no concept of their own, and the file's opening names roots and homesickness, hospitality, kinship and ancestry, the privacy of the home, childhood and loyalty as missing from the list)

### C1. Fatherhood
- *Working definition:* being a father, and the role, standing and ideals attached to it: begetting, providing, protecting, teaching and loving a child; and the father as a figure of the culture (the head of the house, God the Father, the founding fathers, the fatherland).
- *Why here:* parents are one of the sign's traditional areas, but the list names only mothers; the file's opening asks whether the author wants a fatherhood or parenthood entry, and section 0's "Parents and fathering" bullet holds fathering for now. The research notes give parenting ("no child without a parent") and provision (steady material support). The author writes for men, so fathering gets an entry of its own. *Also fits:* Capricorn (a father's authority), Taurus (C39, providing), Leo (pride in one's children).
- *What it would add:* the father's side of every Cancer concept (nurture, protection, care, abandonment), which the current list sees mostly through mothers: what makes a good father here, what a father owes, how fathers show love, and the grief and shame of fathers who cannot provide, protect or stay.
- *Avenues:* section 0's fathering bullet (the words; Father's Day; paternity leave and whether fathers take it; fathers' time with children in ATUS; the Traditional Machismo and Caballerismo Scale); fathers in obituaries and eulogies; what sons and daughters write about their fathers in memoirs; rites between fathers and children (the first fishing or hunting trip; teaching a son or daughter to drive or to work; the father's dance at a wedding or *quinceañera*); fathers' groups and stay-at-home dads; the father who works far away (migrant fathers; fishermen, loggers and camp workers on long rotations); single fathers, stepfathers and godfathers; the absent father (under Abandonment); "dad jokes", and the mild mockery of fathers in advertising and sitcoms; fathers' grief at a child's death, and how men are expected to carry it.

### C2. Filial duty
- *Working definition:* what grown children owe their parents, above all in old age: respect, presence, money, a room in the house, care to the end, and mourning.
- *Why here:* parents are a traditional area of the sign; Care's Beliefs line asks whether placing a parent in a home (*asilo*) is a failure, and its Measures include the item on grown children's duty to provide long-term care; the research notes give duty owed "to family and those it protects, not to rules or office". Other traditions name it as a central virtue: Chinese *xiao*, Korean *hyo* and Japanese *oyakōkō*, as each tradition teaches them *check*. *Also fits:* Capricorn (duty; respect for elders), Libra (reciprocity between generations).
- *What it would add:* the debt that runs upward, from child to parent, where the current concepts mostly look downward (nurture, protection, motherhood): who owes most (the eldest son, the youngest child, the daughters), whether repaying it is love or duty, and what is said of children who don't.
- *Avenues:* the WVS and ISSP items on duties to parents (under Care's Measures); the commandment to honor father and mother in sermons and catechisms; the youngest who stays with the parents (the *xocoyote*, under Gemini's Siblings); multigenerational households (section 0); remittances sent to parents; immigrant parents' hopes of the children they raise in a new country, as a question rather than an assumption; elders' own views on moving in with their children or living alone; guilt and gratitude between generations (C17).

### C3. Roots and rootedness
- *Working definition:* being anchored in a place and a line of people: staying put, knowing where one comes from, and having somewhere one is from.
- *Why here:* roots is one of the sign's traditional areas, now one bullet of section 0 ("Roots"), and the file's opening names roots as missing from the list; the research notes give roots ("putting down roots as a life phase that supports further growth") and ancestry ("belonging stretched back in time"). *Also fits:* Taurus (C30, *querencia* and *arraigo*: attachment to a place apart from owning it), Aries (C29, origins), Capricorn (heritage). One home is to be chosen among these.
- *What it would add:* staying against moving on as a value (Stegner's "boomers" and "stickers", under section 0), rootlessness as a loss or a freedom, and the uprooted: migrants, the displaced, the children of military families, people taken from their families and lands.
- *Avenues:* the measures in section 0's roots bullet (the share living in the state of their birth; mobility tables; Pew's 2008 report); "where are you from?", and what people answer; family land and century farms; *desarraigo* in migrants' talk and songs; Indigenous peoples' uprooting and their returns to their lands, as the nations tell it; place-attachment research (Taurus C30); genealogy as a search for roots (Alex Haley's *Roots*, the 1976 novel and the 1977 television series, credited with a boom in family-history research in the U.S. *check*); visits to a parent's home village; the rootless as a type (drifters; "military brats"; third culture kids, under Belonging's margins).

### C4. Kinship and ancestry
- *Working definition:* who is related to whom, and how a people reckons it: descent through fathers, mothers or both; kin terms; lineage, clan and house; and the ancestors as part of the family.
- *Why here:* the file's opening names kinship and ancestry as missing; family ways are this sign's folkway (Fischer: how the family is ordered, how far it reaches); the research notes give ancestry and kinship ("ties of blood set against kinship built on shared ideals"). *Also fits:* Capricorn (heritage, lineage and rank), Scorpio (the dead), Aries (C29, origins).
- *What it would add:* the system beneath Family: the customs by which a people sorts its relatives (whom one may marry; who carries a name; who belongs to which clan or house), and the ancestors who still count as kin, which Family holds only as facets.
- *Avenues:* kin terms and their systems (the classic typologies of Morgan and Murdock, read critically against each people's own account); descent and residence codes in the Ethnographic Atlas, read critically (section 0); clan and house systems, as the Northwest Coast nations describe them; *parentela*, *familia política* and cousins counted as close kin (under Family's Words); genealogy and DNA testing (section 0); honoring ancestors (Día de Muertos; memorial feasts, as the nations share them; visits to graves); naming children after ancestors; how many generations back people can name, as a question to measure; the anthropology of kinship from Schneider (*American Kinship*, 1968) to the "new kinship" studies.

### C5. Hospitality at home
- *Working definition:* receiving guests in one's home: who is invited or may drop in, what they must be offered, how long they may stay, and the duties of host and guest.
- *Why here:* the file's opening names hospitality as missing, and section 0's "Hospitality at home" bullet notes that it is on no sign's list; the research notes give hospitality ("hosting, feeding and gathering people on its own ground; sometimes hospitality that cannot be declined") and gathering. Taurus proposes the same idea as its C21 (hospitality as the sharing of one's possessions) and names Cancer as another home; one home is to be chosen. *Also fits:* Taurus (C21), Leo (generosity), Libra (reciprocity).
- *What it would add:* the home opened outward: the host's honor and burden, the guest's duties, and the food pressed on visitors that cannot be refused without hurting the giver (the research notes' "emotional blackmail"), which the current concepts touch only in Home's Words (*mi casa es su casa*) and in Shelter's lodging of strangers.
- *Avenues:* *mi casa es su casa* and *estás en tu casa* (under Home's Words); how many times food is offered and refused; whether guests bring something (a dish, a bottle, a gift for the house); dropping in against coming only when invited; the feast and the potlatch as hosting, as the Northwest Coast nations describe them (section 0); potlucks; the guest room and the couch; hosts' and guests' complaints in advice columns; Japanese *omotenashi* and Greek *philoxenia* (Taurus C21) as named ideals from elsewhere; hospitality in scripture (Abraham and the three visitors); how long relatives may stay before the house strains.

### C6. Childhood
- *Working definition:* what a culture takes childhood to be: when it begins and ends, what a child is and needs, what children may and must do, and what they are kept from.
- *Why here:* the file's opening names childhood as missing; child-rearing ways are this sign's folkway; the research notes give childhood ("how children are raised and disciplined, what labels do to them, and their need for privacy"); childhood is a Tier 2 concept, under seven signs. Gemini proposes the same idea as its C4 and asks whether it belongs there or here; Aries placed it "more at home in Cancer or Leo". *Also fits:* Gemini (C4: early learning and children's own culture), Leo (play), Capricorn (age ways).
- *What it would add:* the child as a kind of person (innocent, sinful, a small adult, a helper, a priceless treasure: Zelizer, under Nurture's Beliefs), seen from the home that raises and shelters it. Gemini's C4 sees childhood from children's own culture and learning, so the two signs could split the topic, or one could take it whole.
- *Avenues:* when childhood ends (first communion; *quinceañera*; confirmation; a first job; coming-of-age ceremonies, as the nations share them); children's work and chores (section 0's child-rearing ways); how much freedom children have, and the "free-range" debates (under Nurture's Margins); what children are told and kept from (sex, death, money, family secrets); the labels put on children ("the smart one", "the difficult one") and what they do to them (the research notes' labeling); a child's own room and privacy (C7); childhood remembered in memoirs as a happy time or a time to survive; Día del Niño (April 30, under Nurture's Ritual); the history of childhood (Ariès, 1960, in English as *Centuries of Childhood*, 1962, and his critics).

### C7. The privacy of the home
- *Working definition:* the home as a closed world: what outsiders may see, hear and know of it, which rooms and matters are kept from guests, and what members of the household keep from one another.
- *Why here:* the file's opening names the privacy of the home as missing; section 0's "The home's walls" bullet holds it now; the research notes give privacy ("a child's room and inner life as sacred") and intrusion. Gemini proposes privacy in general as its C26, framed through custom and feeling. *Also fits:* Gemini (C26), Scorpio (secrecy), Aries (autonomy). Framed here through custom, feeling and the layout of the house only.
- *What it would add:* the line between the household and the world, and the lines inside the household (a child's room, a diary, a closed door, a parent's phone), with the old belief that what happens at home stays at home, and its cost to people harmed behind closed doors.
- *Avenues:* curtains, blinds, hedges, fences and walls (section 0's "The home's walls"; Frost's "Mending Wall"); which rooms guests may enter, and whether bedroom doors stay open; knocking before entering a child's room, and from what age; parents reading a child's diary or phone, and what parents say about it (Pew's surveys of parents' monitoring of teens *check*); *la ropa sucia se lava en casa* (dirty laundry is washed at home); family secrets (under Family's Silences); what neighbors may hear through walls, and what they are expected to do about it; open-plan houses and the loss of private rooms; smart speakers and home cameras as a new breach (under Home's Who it applies to).

### C8. Housekeeping and household work
- *Working definition:* the daily work of keeping a home (cleaning, cooking, laundry, shopping, repairs, keeping order), who does it, and what a well-kept house is taken to say about the people in it.
- *Why here:* Home's Everyday practice lists housekeeping and who does it (ATUS; INEGI's ENUT); the research notes give chores ("heavy housework as a channel for suppressed anger"); gender ways (the split of household work) are this sign's runner-up folkway, with its home in Libra. *Also fits:* Virgo (work; order and cleanliness), Libra (gender ways).
- *What it would add:* housework as a moral and emotional field and not only a time-use figure: cleanliness as a virtue, the house as a verdict on the wife or the husband, chores as an outlet for feeling, and pride and shame in the state of one's house.
- *Avenues:* time-use data on housework by sex (ATUS; ENUT; Statistics Canada); the "second shift" (Hochschild, under Motherhood's Time and rhythm); men's share of housework, and how men speak of it; children's chores (section 0); spring cleaning and Saturday cleaning; the paid housekeeper and her place in the family (C40); home-economics classes and their history; cleaning-product advertising and whom it shows doing the work; the shame of a dirty house (under Home's Rewards and sanctions); cleaning influencers and decluttering books (Kondo, *The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up*, 2011 in Japanese *check*); the household appliance and whether it saved any time (Cowan, *More Work for Mother*, 1983).

### C9. The household and its forms
- *Working definition:* the people who live and eat together under one roof, how the group is made up, and who heads it.
- *Why here:* section 0 opens with household forms and multigenerational living; Family's facets set the household against the wider kin network; Home holds the household as one facet; the research notes give a family "broadly defined to include kin, chosen kin and absorbed friends". *Also fits:* Capricorn (the head of the household and his or her say), Virgo (running a house), Taurus (the household's goods).
- *What it would add:* the unit that is actually counted, fed and housed, which is neither the building (Home) nor the kin (Family): lodgers, boarders and friends in the house; the grandparents in the basement suite; the relative who lives with the family and never quite belongs (*arrimado*, under Belonging's Words); and what "head of household" means in daily life.
- *Avenues:* census household tables and their changing categories (section 0); the relationship-to-head question (under section 0's source types); Laslett and Hajnal on household forms in history (section 0); multigenerational and doubled-up households (section 0; Shelter); roommates and shared houses among young adults; boarders and lodgers, past and present; the household as a unit of pooled money and shared meals (Family's economy); who decides what in the household (surveys of household decision-making *check*); one-person households and living alone (Klinenberg, *Going Solo*, 2012).

### C10. Grandparents and grandparenthood
- *Working definition:* the place of grandparents in a family: carers of grandchildren, keepers of memory and custom, heads of the wider family, and people to be cared for in their turn.
- *Why here:* section 0 and Nurture name grandparents as child-carers and grandparent-headed households; the "grandmother hypothesis" (Hawkes et al., under Motherhood's Life course) treats grandmothers as central to human child-rearing; the research notes give memory and custodianship. *Also fits:* Capricorn (elders and age ways), Gemini (C13, teaching and mentoring).
- *What it would add:* a generation the current concepts mention everywhere and study nowhere: grandparents who raise grandchildren while the parents work or migrate, the grandmother as the family's center, the grandfather's stories, and what grandparents are owed.

### C25. *Storgē* (family affection)
- *Working definition:* the Greek word for the fond, familiar love between parents and children and among kin, which C. S. Lewis called "affection" (*The Four Loves*, 1960): love grown from long living together rather than from choice or passion.
- *Why here:* love is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs, with no home; the research notes give love ("being needed is the secure form of love") and warmth. English has one word, "love", where Greek splits family affection (*storgē*) from desire (*erōs*), friendship (*philia*) and charity (*agapē*). *Also fits:* Libra and Scorpio (romantic and sexual love), Aquarius (friendship), Pisces (compassion, the nearest to *agapē*).
- *What it would add:* the everyday love of home as a kind of its own, apart from romance: how families show it (words, touch, food, presence, teasing), whether "I love you" is said aloud between parents and children, and between fathers and sons, and what a culture calls the love that grows from sharing a roof.
- *Avenues:* Lewis's four loves, and scholars' debate over the Greek words; how often family members say "I love you" (surveys *check*); *querer* against *amar* in Spanish (*te quiero* and *te amo*, and who says which to whom), and *cariño*; the "five love languages" (Chapman, 1992) as a popular theory to test; affection in fathers' and sons' memoirs; pet names inside families; love for pets as family members; the love of kin in scripture and religious teaching.

### C26. *Familismo* (family first)
- *Working definition:* the value that puts the family's needs and good name before the individual's: loyalty, closeness, mutual help and duty among kin, as Spanish names it and as the research that uses the word defines it.
- *Why here:* Family's Beliefs and Measures name *familismo* and its scales (Sabogal et al., 1987; the Mexican American Cultural Values Scale); the research notes give loyalty ("fights a friend's enemies as its own") and family. Loyalty, a Tier 2 concept under ten signs, is proposed as Taurus C18, which names Cancer as another home; *familismo* is loyalty in its family form. *Also fits:* Taurus (C18, loyalty), Capricorn (duty), Aries (independence, its counterweight).
- *What it would add:* a named value that lets the family-first outlook be measured and compared across groups, with its critics' warning that it is easily turned into a stereotype; recorded here as a question to research in each group, not a finding.
- *Avenues:* familism scales and their findings (under Family's Measures); "amoral familism" (Banfield, under Family's Excess) as its excess, in an outsider's account; *la familia es primero* (under Family's Expression); family loyalty in choices about college, jobs and moving away; the burden it can carry (Family's Silences; C17); comparable values named elsewhere (filial duty, C2); studies of family ties and health among Latinos, in the debate over the "Hispanic paradox" (*check*).

### C27. *Compadrazgo* (ritual kinship)

### C28. *Heimat*
- *Working definition:* the German word for the home place in its widest sense: the region, landscape, dialect and people one grew up among and belongs to, with deep feeling attached.
- *Why here:* Home's facets include home as origin and as territory; the research notes give belonging that "widens from the clan to community, country"; English has no single word that joins home, hometown and homeland with this warmth. *Also fits:* Taurus (C30, *querencia*), Aries (identity), Capricorn (heritage). ### C33. Taking in the stranger (split from shelter)
- *Working definition:* the duty to receive travelers, refugees and strangers in need: to open the door, give a meal and a bed, and not turn them away.
- *Why here:* Shelter's facets include refuge for the persecuted and lodging for strangers and travelers; its Beliefs line opens with "the duty to take in the stranger" (Matthew 25:35; Las Posadas); the research notes' gaps name strangers and refugees at scale as missing from their sources. *Also fits:* Pisces (compassion), Sagittarius (faith; travel), Libra (reciprocity), Taurus (C21, hospitality).

### C34. The extended family (split from family)
- *Working definition:* the wide family beyond parents and children (grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, godparents), and how much of daily life it shares.

### C35. Chosen family (split from family)

### C37. Paternalism (split from protection)

### C38. The protector role (split from protection)
