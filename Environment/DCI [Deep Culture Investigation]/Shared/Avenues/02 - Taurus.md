# Avenues of investigation: 02 Taurus (possessions and value)

**What this file is.** For each Taurus concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Taurus concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. Indigenous-language terms appear only where a nation or its language program publishes them, and every spelling is marked *(check)*.

**The concept list** (proposed, **not yet settled**; each sign's list is settled with the author just before its pass): possession, ownership, self-worth, values, security, stability, wealth, possessiveness, stubbornness, sensuality.

**Questions to settle with the list:**
- **Possession and ownership** are two entries here, split the way autonomy and independence were split in Aries: **possession** is having and holding things, and the bond between people and their things; **ownership** is a claim to a thing that others recognize (property and tenure).
- **Security and stability** overlap. Proposed split: **security** is protection against loss and want (of livelihood, holdings and safety); **stability** is continuity, things staying as they are.
- **Security** is also in Cancer's ten. Its proposed home is Taurus, and `04 - Cancer.md` keeps a cross-reference. Cancer's **protection** and **shelter** would then cover the safety of persons and of the home; Taurus covers the security of livelihood and holdings.
- **Values** is close to the subject of DCI as a whole. This entry is kept to values as a category: the word, what people name as their values, how values are ranked, taught and fought over, what may not be priced, and valuation (what things are worth). **Self-worth** is the worth of a person.
- **Near-synonym groups** from the concept index that touch this list (not merged): envy / jealousy (under possessiveness); persistence / perseverance / endurance and grudge / grievance / resentment (under stubbornness); status / rank / reputation (under wealth and self-worth); appreciation, in the recognition group (under self-worth).
- **Money** is not on the list. It is covered in section 0, beside wealth.

## 0. The domain: possessions and value

**Traditional areas:** possessions, money, resources, values, self-worth. **The domain question:** what is worth having, and how is it held?

- **What is worth having.** Ask people to name their most cherished things and say why (Csikszentmihalyi and Rochberg-Halton, *The Meaning of Things*, 1981: a method that can be repeated anywhere); what people save from a fire (accounts from the Paradise, Lytton and Los Angeles fires of 2018, 2021 and 2025); what wills name and what heirs fight over; what is stolen most; what is set on Día de Muertos *ofrendas* or placed in graves; Pew's open-ended question on what makes life meaningful, where material well-being was one of the answers (17 advanced economies including the U.S. and Canada, 2021); the WVS question on how important work, family, friends, leisure, politics and religion are in life.
- **Money.** Words for it (*lana*, *varo*, *feria*, *billete*; "bucks", "dough"; Canada's "loonie" and "toonie"; Chinuk Wawa's word for money, as the Grand Ronde dictionary gives it *check*); how money is earmarked and given special meanings (Zelizer, *The Social Meaning of Money*, 1994; Thaler's mental accounting); who manages a household's money (Pahl, *Money and Marriage*, 1989); cash against cards and phones (INEGI and the CNBV's Encuesta Nacional de Inclusión Financiera; the Federal Reserve's Diary of Consumer Payment Choice); the unbanked (the FDIC's National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households, by state); remittances (Banxico's figures by receiving state); religion and money (tithing, including Latter-day Saint tithing; the prosperity gospel; Catholic teaching on usury and the just price).
- **Money talk and its silences.** Whether people say what they earn, what they paid, what they inherited; salary secrecy against openness about pay (pay ranges posted in job ads; salary-sharing sites such as Glassdoor); asking friends the price of things; money talk between spouses and with children; advice columns on money etiquette.
- **Saving, credit and debt.** Saving rates (BEA's personal saving rate; Statistics Canada's household saving rate); rotating savings groups (*tandas*); pension accounts (Mexico's Afores; Canada's RRSPs and TFSAs; 401(k)s); buying on trust at the corner store (*fiado*, and the shop sign *hoy no fío, mañana sí*); pawnshops (Mexico's Nacional Monte de Piedad, founded 1775); payday lenders and check-cashing stores, and who uses them *(check)*; household debt (the New York Fed's Household Debt and Credit Report, by state; the Urban Institute's county map of debt in collections *check*; Statistics Canada's debt-to-income ratio); whether debt is shameful or normal; *cuentas claras, amistades largas* (clear accounts, long friendships).
- **Thrift and display.** Thrift sayings ("use it up, wear it out, make it do, or do without"; "waste not, want not"; *el que guarda siempre tiene*); mending and reuse; thrift stores (Goodwill, Value Village), swap meets (Pasadena's Rose Bowl Flea Market) and *tianguis*; secondhand U.S. clothing sold by the bale (*pacas*) and in *segundas* along the border; *venta de garage*; coupons and bulk buying (Costco, based in Issaquah, Washington); against these, display: conspicuous consumption (Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899), lowriders, luxury brands, *quinceañera* and wedding spending, "stealth wealth"; the WVS child quality "thrift, saving money and things".
- **Consumption and brands.** Shopping as leisure (the American Time Use Survey's time spent buying goods); malls and their decline; brands born in the pilot (Amazon, Costco, Apple, Nike, REI, Starbucks, Lululemon, In-N-Out, Levi's) and what they say about the region; sales rituals (Black Friday; Mexico's *El Buen Fin*, since 2011); campaigns against consumption (Adbusters and Buy Nothing Day, both from Vancouver; *The Story of Stuff*, 2007, from Berkeley); spending patterns (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey by region and some metros; INEGI's ENIGH by state; Statistics Canada's Survey of Household Spending); advertising as a record of what is held worth having.
- **Resources.** Who holds natural resources, and how people use and share them: land, water, timber, fish, minerals.
  - water: who gets water and who goes without; the Owens Valley water wars with Los Angeles (from 1905); what the dams drowned (Celilo Falls, the great Columbia River fishing place, flooded in 1957); drought habits (brown lawns, short showers); CONAGUA's yearly water statistics (*Estadísticas del Agua en México* *check*); Worster, *Rivers of Empire* (1985); Reisner, *Cadillac Desert* (1986);
  - forests: logging towns and what timber meant to them; the timber wars (the spotted owl, 1990; Clayoquot Sound, 1993; Fairy Creek, 2020-2022); wise use against preservation (Pinchot against Muir; the Hetch Hetchy fight, 1913);
  - fish: Indigenous fishing as the nations describe it (the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission's salmon plan, *Wy-Kan-Ush-Mi Wa-Kish-Wit* *check*); the fish-ins of the 1960s and 1970s in Washington; Baja's fishing cooperatives for abalone and lobster *(check)*; the Cucapá's struggle to fish in the Colorado River delta *(check)*;
  - minerals: prospectors and mining towns, and their booms and busts (the Cariboo gold rush, 1860s; the copper town of Santa Rosalía, Baja California Sur, built by a French company from 1885 *check*); local and Native opposition to mines; the Mount Polley tailings spill (British Columbia, 2014);
  - personal resources: time, skills, kin and contacts (Lomnitz, *Networks and Marginality*, 1977, on reciprocity among the poor of Mexico City); resourcefulness ("make do"; *rascuachismo*, the Chicano aesthetic of making do *check*).
- **Land and attachment to place.** Place-attachment studies (Lewicka's review, 2011 *check*); Spanish *terruño*, *patria chica* and *querencia* (the place one loves and returns to; in bullfighting, the spot in the ring where the bull feels safe); land as kin and teacher in Indigenous teachings, as the nations state them (the Stó:lō Nation's *S'ólh Téméxw*, "our world" *check*); century farms (Oregon's Century Farm and Ranch Program); the family *rancho* in the Baja California Sur sierra.
- **Comfort and the body's needs.** Food, sleep, warmth and rest as goods in themselves; see sensuality (section 10).
- **Prices and fair exchange.** Haggling against fixed prices; anger at price gouging after disasters and in shortages; tipping; "you get what you pay for" and *lo barato sale caro* (the cheap turns out dear). Fairness and reciprocity themselves belong to Libra.
- **Food ways** (Fischer's folkway for Taurus):

  - First Foods and food sovereignty as the nations publish them: the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation's First Foods mission (water, salmon, deer, roots and berries, in the order served *check*); eulachon grease and the grease trails; herring roe on kelp, gathered and traded by the Heiltsuk as they describe it; the First Nations Food, Nutrition and Environment Study (British Columbia, 2008-2009);
  - meals: when people eat (the Mexican *comida* in mid-afternoon against the evening dinner), who cooks and who serves, eating together or alone (ATUS), *sobremesa* (lingering at the table), *buen provecho*;
  - feasting and fasting: Thanksgiving and its counter-observance, the sunrise gathering on Alcatraz each November; *posadas* and Christmas *tamales*; Lent; Ramadan among Muslim residents; feasts at potlatches, as the nations describe them;
  - rules and taboos: the Latter-day Saints' Word of Wisdom (no alcohol, coffee or tea); the Seventh-day Adventists of Loma Linda; kosher and halal eating; vegetarian and vegan diets; foods avoided in pregnancy, illness or mourning;
  - food and status: street food against fine dining; "foodies"; Michelin stars in California; who eats what at a wedding; the snobbery of *fifí* and *naco* food;
  - food programs: SNAP and WIC; school meals; Mexico's DIF school breakfasts; Canada's national school food program (2024 *check*); food banks;
  - measures: BRFSS fruit and vegetable intake by state; USDA's Food Environment Atlas (county); ENSANUT (Mexico, by state); the Canadian Community Health Survey; FAO food balance sheets (country); food spending in the CE, ENIGH and the Survey of Household Spending; ATUS eating time;
  - expression: cookbooks; food writing (M.F.K. Fisher; James Beard, born in Portland); *Como agua para chocolate* (Esquivel, 1989); food television;
  - change: delivery apps; plant-based meat (Beyond Meat, Los Angeles; Impossible Foods, Redwood City); weight-loss drugs.
- **Dress ways** (Fischer's folkway for Taurus; runner-up Leo, which takes dress as display):
  - everyday regional dress: outdoor gear as daily wear (Patagonia, Columbia, Arc'teryx, Filson, Eddie Bauer, REI); athleisure (Lululemon, Vancouver, 1998); denim (Levi Strauss, San Francisco, 1853); grunge flannel; the tech hoodie and fleece vest; Western wear and the Pendleton Round-Up (Oregon, since 1910); *vaquero* and *norteño* dress (*texana* hat, boots, *cinto piteado*); the *charro* suit; *buchón* style; Pendleton blankets, given at Native ceremonies as the nations describe;
  - Native regalia as the nations show and describe it (button blankets; woven cedar-bark hats; Chilkat and Ravenstail weaving; Plateau beadwork), and the nations' guidance to call it regalia, not costume;
  - plain against display; natural against synthetic fibers; comfort against formality; who may wear what (rank, age, gender, office);
  - dress codes at school, work, worship, restaurants and clubs; Mexican school uniforms; debates over gendered school dress codes;
  - sayings: "clothes make the man"; "dress for success" (Molloy, 1975); *como te ven te tratan* (as they see you, they treat you); *aunque la mona se vista de seda, mona se queda*; *el hábito no hace al monje*;
  - appropriation debates (headdresses at music festivals);
  - measures: apparel spending (CE; ENIGH's *vestido y calzado*; Statistics Canada's SHS); Google Trends;
  - history: dress as a mark of caste in New Spain *(check)*; gold-rush workwear; the counterculture;
  - change: fast fashion against resale (ThredUp's resale reports *check*); repair and mending; the used-clothing trade across the border.
- **Wealth ways** (Fischer's folkway for Taurus; the concept of wealth is section 7):
  - how wealth is gained: work, land, inheritance, extraction (gold, timber, fish, oil), speculation, tech fortunes, patronage, crime;
  - how it is shared out: inequality by county (the ACS Gini index); wealth surveys (the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances; Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security; INEGI's ENIGH); Fischer's regional patterns of inequality and their heirs in the pilot (Woodard's Left Coast, Far West and El Norte);

  - how it is given away: the potlatch and giveaways as the nations describe them (the U'mista Cultural Centre in Alert Bay holds regalia seized after the 1921 Cranmer potlatch and later returned); *padrinos* who share the costs of a wedding or *quinceañera*; philanthropy (the Gates Foundation; the Hewlett and Packard foundations); church tithes; remittances;

## 1. Possession

**Working definition:** having and holding things: the bond between people and the things they keep, use, carry, store, lend, lose and give away.
**Facets:** everyday belongings; cherished objects and keepsakes; heirlooms; collections; clutter and hoarding; lending, borrowing and sharing; losing and finding; theft; gifts; possessions as part of the self; the possessions of the dead.
**Neighbors and opposites:** belongings, having, keeping, property in its everyday sense; dispossession, loss, destitution, nonattachment. **Excess:** hoarding, acquisitiveness, materialism. **Deficiency:** destitution; or, held up as an ideal, renunciation.

- **Words:** "mine", "stuff", "things", "belongings", "keepsake", "heirloom", "junk", "clutter", "pack rat", "hoarder", "collector", "treasure", "retail therapy", "finders keepers", "dibs", "shotgun" (claiming the front seat), "possession is nine-tenths of the law", "one man's trash is another man's treasure", "you can't take it with you"; the English word's second sense, spirit possession; Spanish *pertenencias*, *bienes*, *cosas*, and in Mexico *chivas* (*agarra tus chivas*), *tiliches*, *triques*, *cachivaches*; *apartar* (to set aside, to claim); *el que se fue a la Villa perdió su silla* (whoever leaves loses their seat); the courtesy *es suyo* or *está a sus órdenes*, said of an object a guest admires; the grammar of having (*tener*; whether a language marks some things, such as body parts and kin, as possessed differently from others: alienable against inalienable possession, found in many Indigenous languages of the region *check*); an old English slur for taking back a gift, usually traced to settlers' misreading of Native gift exchange (described, not quoted; *check*).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is having things good, neutral or a spiritual danger? Christian teaching on treasure on earth and the rich young man; Franciscan poverty (the California missions were Franciscan); nonattachment as taught in the region's Zen centers (the San Francisco Zen Center); minimalism as a secular ideal; teachings on sharing as the nations state them (the Dene Laws published by the Dene Nation *check*); possessions as part of the self (Belk, "Possessions and the Extended Self", 1988); objects kept even while given (Weiner, *Inalienable Possessions*, 1992); the gift and its obligations (Mauss, *The Gift*, 1925); materialism as a cost to well-being (Kasser, *The High Price of Materialism*, 2002).
- **Feeling and the body:** attachment to objects; the grief of losing them (survivors of the Paradise, Lytton and Los Angeles fires); touching a thing makes people value it more (Peck and Shu, 2009); the stress of clutter (Saxbe and Repetti, 2010, from the UCLA study of Los Angeles families); comfort objects in childhood and old age; hoarding disorder (in the DSM since 2013); the thrill of acquiring.
- **Everyday practice:** what people keep and how much (UCLA's *Life at Home in the Twenty-First Century*, 2012, which cataloged the belongings of 32 Los Angeles families); garages and self-storage; yard sales, swap meets, *tianguis*; lending tools and books (tool libraries; Little Free Libraries); giving things away (Buy Nothing groups, begun on Bainbridge Island, Washington, in 2013; Freecycle; Craigslist's free section; curbside "free" piles); marking things as one's own (names on lunch boxes; *apartar* a seat or a parking spot with an object); what people carry every day; how guests treat a host's things; what people do with a lost wallet.
- **Life course:** the toddler's "mine!"; learning to share in preschool ("sharing is caring"); a child's own room and own things; allowances and the first things bought with one's own money; the first car; what each spouse brings to a marriage; downsizing in old age; "death cleaning"; estate sales; what happens to the belongings of the dead: kept, divided, sold, burned or given away (customs of burning or giving away a dead person's things among some Yuman and Great Basin peoples, as the nations themselves describe them *check*).
- **Ritual:** gift days (Christmas, birthdays, Mexico's Día de Reyes on January 6); housewarming, wedding and baby-shower gifts, and registries; the *arras*, thirteen coins given at a Catholic wedding; giveaways at powwows and potlatches, as the nations describe them; blessings of objects, vehicles and animals (the Blessing of the Animals on Olvera Street, Los Angeles); objects placed on *ofrendas* for the dead.
- **Institutions:** museums and their return of objects to the peoples they came from (the Royal BC Museum's repatriation work; Mexico's campaigns to stop sales of its archaeological pieces abroad, such as #MiPatrimonioNoSeVende, 2021 *check*); consumer agencies (Mexico's PROFECO); pawnshops (the Nacional Monte de Piedad), secondhand dealers, thrift stores and self-storage firms; lost-and-found offices; repair cafés and the right-to-repair movement *(check)*; professional organizers and their association (NAPO *check*); hoarding task forces (San Francisco's, 2009 *check*); storage for homeless people's belongings (the Bin on Los Angeles's Skid Row *check*).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the tidy and the thrifty; mockery of hoarders and of clutter; shame for losing things; the shaming of thieves, and everyday words for kinds of theft (*robo hormiga*, small repeated theft by employees; *cristalazo*, the car smash-and-grab); *ladrón que roba a ladrón tiene cien años de perdón* (a thief who robs a thief earns a hundred years' pardon); the return of lost property as a test of honesty.
- **Expression:** *Citizen Kane*'s Rosebud (1941); *Toy Story* (Pixar, 1995), where a child's name on a toy's boot marks it as his; the "mine" gulls of *Finding Nemo* (2003); George Carlin's routine "A Place for My Stuff" (1981); Tolkien's ring and "my precious"; reality shows about hoarders, decluttering (*Tidying Up with Marie Kondo*, 2019) and storage-unit auctions; songs about things lost and kept.
- **Exemplars:** collectors (William Randolph Hearst and Hearst Castle); Sarah Winchester's ever-growing house in San Jose; minimalists and renunciants; people known for giving everything away; contested figures: collectors of Native objects (museum founders; private collectors).
- **History:** the founders' attitudes (Yankee thrift; the missions' control over Native goods and labor; gift exchange and trade networks along the coast and across the Plateau, as the nations tell them); the fur trade; the gold rush (claims, caches, theft and lynching); the seizure of Native regalia and objects (the 1921 Cranmer potlatch seizure); the property losses of interned Japanese Americans and dispossessed Japanese Canadians (the University of Victoria's Landscapes of Injustice project); mass consumption after 1945.
- **Measures:** self-storage businesses and used-goods stores per capita (Census County Business Patterns, NAICS 531130 and 459510 *check*); household goods (INEGI's census asks which appliances and vehicles a dwelling has; the ACS asks about vehicles and computers; Statistics Canada's Survey of Household Spending on household equipment); spending on goods (CE, ENIGH, SHS); time spent shopping (ATUS); theft as households report it (INEGI's ENVIPE by state); lost-wallet return rates (Cohn et al., 2019, 40 countries); the endowment effect across cultures (Maddux et al., 2010 *check*; Apicella et al., 2014, among the Hadza *check*); the Material Values Scale (Richins and Dawson, 1992); hoarding prevalence *(check)*.
- **Variation:** class (having little against having too much); rural sheds and barns against urban storage units; immigrants who arrived with little; generation (heirlooms the young don't want); gender (who keeps the family's things and memories); renters and the homeless (whose belongings are most at risk).
- **Relations:** ownership (the recognized claim behind possession); possessiveness (its excess); wealth; generosity (Leo); reciprocity and sharing (Libra); inheritance and the commons (Scorpio); home (Cancer); independence (Aries).
- **Who it applies to:** children's things and who controls them; animals and pets (pets held as possessions; animals' own caches and territories); the dead and their goods; spirits (possession in the other sense); digital possessions (files, games, accounts, photos); whether people think machines or AI could possess anything (present-day attitudes).
- **Silences:** what people hide owning (guns, debts, stored clutter); the shame of hoarding; stolen or looted objects in family and museum collections; the belongings of the homeless.
- **Change:** renting and subscribing in place of owning (streaming, software); the sharing economy (Airbnb, 2008, and Uber, 2009, both from San Francisco); decluttering and minimalism; resale; digital goods and NFTs (the 2021 boom); the right-to-repair movement; panic buying (2020).
- **The senses:** the smell and feel of cherished things: a dead parent's sweater kept unwashed for its smell; a tool handle worn smooth by a grandfather's hand; the "new" smell of a new car, new shoes or a new book, against the musty smell of attics and thrift stores; the sounds of having: keys on a ring, the click of a lock, coins dropped in a jar, the tape torn open in an unboxing; touching goods before buying, where it is expected (the *tianguis*, the farmers' market) and where it is forbidden ("look with your eyes, not your hands"); the textures of heirloom cloth (quilts, *rebozos*, christening gowns; blankets and regalia, as the nations describe them); the eye's sense of clutter, against the calm of a bare room (the minimalist look); food kept as one's own (a child's stash of Halloween candy; the treat hidden from the rest of the family).
- **Space, place and things:** where things live: the garage that holds no car, the attic, the basement, the shed, the spare room, the self-storage unit at the edge of town; the junk drawer; the china cabinet (*vitrina*) with the good dishes and figurines that are never used; shelves that display collections; the home altar with its photographs and objects; lockers at school and at the gym; the car as a second closet; what people without homes keep, and where (carts, tents, bags; storage lockers run by charities); the lost-and-found box; the curbside "free" pile; graves as places of possessions (toys, coins, cans of beer and favorite foods left for the dead); what migrants carry and send home: the packed car driven south for Christmas, the boxes Filipino families send home (*balikbayan* boxes), parcels sent to family in Mexico and Central America.

**Key questions:** Is having many things a good, a burden or a fault? What must be shared, and what may a person keep for themselves?

## 2. Ownership

**Working definition:** the recognized claim to have, use, keep others from, and give away or sell a thing, and what that claim rests on.
**Facets:** private, common, collective, state and open-access property; tenure (how land and goods are held); what can be owned (land, water, animals, houses, businesses, ideas, names, songs, crests, data, once people); who may own (women, children, foreigners, Native nations, corporations); how ownership begins (first arrival, labor, purchase, inheritance, grant, conquest, long use); how it ends (sale, gift, seizure, abandonment, loss).
**Neighbors and opposites:** property, title, tenure, stewardship, custodianship; dispossession, trespass, tenancy, the commons. **Excess:** enclosure; treating everything, people included, as property. **Deficiency:** insecure tenure; landlessness.


**The left-right axis lives here** (author, 2026-11-10). The author measures where a society sits between left and right by how much of what it produces its government spends: government spending as a share of GDP. 0% is the far right (anarchy: a government that owns nothing can't exist); 100% is the far left (communism: the government owns everything that is produced). The question it asks is one of ownership: how much of what a society produces stays in private hands, and how much is handed to the government?

- **Beside it:** the left-right readings of the law (Libra, the letter of the law) and other measures; where they disagree, the disagreement is recorded, not reconciled.

## 3. Self-worth

**Working definition:** the value a person holds themselves to have, and what that value is believed to rest on.
**Facets:** worth given at birth (dignity); worth granted by others (honor, face, reputation); worth earned by deeds, work or wealth; worth from God; self-esteem and self-respect; worth after loss (unemployment, illness, old age, disgrace); the worth of a people as its members feel it.
**Neighbors and opposites:** self-esteem, self-respect, self-regard, dignity, pride; worthlessness, shame, inferiority, insecurity. **Excess:** arrogance, vanity, narcissism, entitlement. **Deficiency:** low self-esteem, self-loathing, being a doormat.

- **Words:** "self-esteem", "self-respect", "self-worth" against "net worth" ("he's worth five million"), "know your worth", "you're worth it", "worthless", "good-for-nothing", "insecure", "imposter syndrome", "people-pleaser", "doormat", "self-love"; Spanish *autoestima*, *amor propio*, *dignidad*, *valer* (*tú vales mucho*), *sentirse menos*, *hacer menos* (to belittle), *acomplejado*, *apocado*, *el qué dirán* (what people will say); Mexican *valer* in not-caring (*me vale*, *vale madre*: worth and indifference share a verb); *no eres monedita de oro* (you're not a gold coin, to please everyone); *tanto tienes, tanto vales* (you're worth what you have).
- **Beliefs and standing:** dignity, honor and face as three logics of worth (Leung and Cohen, 2011); is worth inherent or earned? - **Feeling and the body:** pride and shame and how they show (posture, gaze, voice); body image and worth; depression and suicide after the loss of a role; dress and grooming as self-respect; the felt sense of being looked down on.
- **Everyday practice:** what people boast of and what they hide; self-promotion online; "what do you do?" as a test of worth; how service workers are treated; accepting or refusing charity; how people speak of themselves (self-deprecation or self-praise as the norm); who sits where.
- **Life course:** praise styles (praise for effort, after Dweck's work at Stanford); "every child is special"; grades and rankings; adolescent self-esteem; the first job; worth after layoffs and in retirement; elders' worth; what a eulogy or obituary praises.
- **Ritual:** graduations, *quinceañeras*, confirmations and honoring ceremonies; naming and honoring ceremonies, as the nations share them; Orange Shirt Day and Canada's National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (September 30), from Phyllis Webstad's account of the new orange shirt taken from her at a residential school near Williams Lake, British Columbia; employee awards; Mother's and Father's Days.
- **Institutions:** schools and their self-esteem programs (the California Task Force to Promote Self-Esteem and Personal and Social Responsibility, 1986-1990); therapy and who can get it; welfare programs and the test of worthiness (the "deserving poor"); performance reviews; churches (Latter-day Saint worthiness interviews); residential and boarding schools, which attacked children's worth, as survivors and nations tell it.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise, titles, honors; being "taken down a peg"; the tall poppy; ridicule of the vain; the stigma of welfare and unemployment; *hacer menos* as a sanction.
- **Expression:** self-help books and affirmations; advertising ("Because I'm worth it", L'Oréal, 1971); José Alfredo Jiménez's *El Rey* (1971), whose singer has no throne or queen and is still king; *Nosotros los pobres* (1948) and the dignity of the poor; *pobre pero honrado* (poor but honest); country songs of the proud poor; survivors' memoirs.

- **History:** Yankee worth through work and grace; Spanish *honra* and the colonial hierarchy of class and color (the caste paintings); Native peoples' standing in the colonial order and in the residential schools; the Chicano movement's "brown pride"; California's self-esteem wave of the 1980s.
- **Measures:** the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale across 53 nations (Schmitt and Allik, 2005); the Contingencies of Self-Worth Scale (Crocker et al., 2003), which shows what worth rests on (appearance, approval, school, family, God, virtue, competition); the Narcissistic Personality Inventory over time (Twenge); self-enhancement across cultures (Heine et al., 1999); honor values (Rodriguez Mosquera et al., 2000, Spain against the Netherlands); status anxiety and inequality (Wilkinson and Pickett, *The Inner Level*, 2018); suicide by county (CDC WONDER); YRBS hopelessness by state; INEGI's self-reported well-being survey (ENBIARE, 2021 *check*); the Canadian Community Health Survey's self-rated mental health.
- **Variation:** gender; class; race and colorism; immigrant generation; disability; whether men's worth is tied to providing; each Native nation's own account of what the residential schools did and what renewal restores.
- **Relations:** dignity, pride, recognition and humiliation (Leo); values; wealth (worth as net worth); identity (Aries); work and usefulness (Virgo); status and reputation (Capricorn); shame.
- **Who it applies to:** groups and peoples; workers replaced by machines; animals and nature (inherent worth); whether people grant machines and AI any worth or moral standing (present-day surveys *check*).
- **Silences:** men's low self-worth; worth after a fall from grace; the worth of unpaid care work; class shame.
- **Change:** social media and comparison; therapy language ("know your worth"); the backlash against "participation trophies"; debates over young men's sense of worth.
- **The senses:** the voice used with those held to be worth less (talking down to waiters, maids and children; the slow, loud voice used with foreigners); being called by name, by title, or by "hey, you" (and in Mexico *oiga*, *joven*, *güero*, *mijo*, each placing the speaker and the one addressed); accents heard as signs of worth or of its lack; cleanliness and scent as dignity (scrubbed children sent to school; pressed clothes and cologne on a working man's day off), and smell used to mark others as lesser; food as a statement of worth: who is served first, who gets the best piece, who eats the leftovers; touch that grants or denies worth (a handshake, against a pat on the head); being looked at, or looked through (service workers who feel invisible); the feel of new clothes against hand-me-downs.

- **Conflict and crisis:** disrespect as a cause of fights and feuds; layoffs and plant closings (the timber and mill towns of Oregon and Washington from the 1990s, and what the men there said they had lost); bullying and its lasting marks; racism and colorism as attacks on worth, and the movements that answer them; disasters that turn proud households into seekers of aid; the "essential workers" of the COVID-19 pandemic, praised in public and paid little; veterans' worth after service; men's suicides after job loss, divorce or retirement (Case and Deaton, *Deaths of Despair*, 2020, on the U.S. as a whole).
- **Margins and extremes:** grandiosity and narcissism at one edge (Aries, C20); self-hatred, self-harm and depression at the other; people treated as worthless or disposable (people without homes, people with addictions), as they describe their own worth; recovery testimonies that rebuild worth from the bottom; impostors who claim titles, degrees or medals they never earned; extreme humility in religious teaching (Isaac Watts's hymn line "for such a worm as I" *check*); body dysmorphia and eating disorders, where worth is fixed on the body.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):

  - *Writing:* religious teaching on human dignity, worthiness, pride and humility; self-help and self-esteem books; books of affirmations; memoirs of shame, recovery and regained dignity; letters and diaries of the unemployed, the poor and the disgraced; conduct and etiquette books (who defers to whom); national-character essays on inferiority or pride; writing on the dignity of labor; children's books that tell a child it is special.
  - *Public records:* school self-esteem and social-emotional curricula; report-card comments; honor rolls and school awards; public health surveys of mental health, hopelessness and suicide; forms for public aid and charity (how applicants must show they deserve help); citizenship ceremonies and what they tell newcomers about their worth; official apologies to peoples and groups.
  - *Mass media:* advertising that speaks to worth ("because you're worth it"); makeover shows; talent and reality competitions (being judged in public); confessional talk shows and stories of redemption; songs of pride and of the proud poor; telenovelas of the humble who rise; motivational speakers; obituaries (what a life was worth); athletes' interviews (modesty or boasting).
  - *Online:* follower counts, likes and ratings as measures of worth; self-improvement and "glow-up" content; forums on low self-esteem and impostor feelings; online shaming and pile-ons; "humblebragging"; affirmation and "self-love" posts; comment sections on bodies and looks.
  - *Commerce:* beauty, grooming and fashion marketing; the confidence and self-improvement trade (coaches, seminars, retreats); luxury goods sold as a reward to oneself; personal-branding services; job interviews and résumés as performances of worth; pay scales, and what they say about whose work is worth more; employee recognition programs.
  - *Organizations:* support groups (for the unemployed, the bereaved, people in recovery); mentoring and confidence programs for young people; men's groups; women's empowerment groups; veterans' groups; disability pride and self-advocacy groups; congregations (teaching on worthiness; testimonies); ethnic pride and heritage organizations; seniors' centers; unions and their claims to dignity at work.
  - *The people who handle it:* therapists and counselors; school counselors; teachers; coaches; clergy; social workers; career counselors and outplacement staff (worth after a layoff); barbers and hairdressers (and the talk in their chairs); personal trainers; nurses and aides who care for the old and the dying.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' remarks on pride, deference and dignity; culture guides on "face" and on giving praise and criticism; expatriate managers' accounts of giving feedback.
  - *Scholarship and data:* self-esteem surveys across groups; studies of dignity, honor and face cultures; studies of status anxiety and inequality; ethnographies of shame and respect; research on what self-worth rests on (contingencies of self-worth); studies of unemployment and its effect on identity; mental-health and suicide statistics; studies of self-enhancement and self-criticism.

**Key questions:** Is a person's worth given at birth, granted by others, earned, or shown by what they own? Can it be lost, and how is it regained?

## 4. Values

**Working definition:** what a person or a culture holds worth having, doing or being, and how those things are ranked; and valuation, how worth is assigned to things.
**Facets:** personal values; shared, regional or national values; family values; religious values; work and corporate values; the ranking of values when they collide; sacred values that may not be traded; economic value and price; the word "values" itself and what it is used to do.
**Neighbors and opposites:** principles, morals, ethics, ideals, priorities, worth; nihilism, cynicism, hypocrisy. **Excess:** moralism, rigidity, the culture war. **Deficiency:** drift; having a price for everything.

- **Words:** "values", "family values", "core values", "values voters", "Canadian values", "American values", "principles", "priorities", "what matters", "worth it", "priceless", "sacred", "sellout"; Spanish *valores*, *principios*, *buenas costumbres*, *respeto*, *educación* (which in Mexico means upbringing and manners: *no tiene educación*), *bien educado*, *la familia es primero*, *vale la pena* (it's worth the pain), *no tiene precio*, *vendido* (a sellout); terms scholars use for Latino values (*familismo*, *respeto*, *personalismo*, *simpatía*; Marín and Marín, 1991; Triandis et al., 1984), to be tested rather than assumed; principles nations publish in their own languages (the Nuu-chah-nulth *hišukʔiš c̓awaak*, "everything is one", with *ʔiisaak*, respect, and *ʔuuʔałuk*, taking care of *check*; the Plateau *tamánwit*, the law of the land, as the Yakama and Umatilla publish it *check*); how often "values" appears in print over time (Google Ngram).
- **Beliefs and standing:** where values come from (God, nature, tradition, reason, choice, the land); are they universal or each person's own? How values are ranked (Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck's value orientations, 1961, studied in five neighboring communities of the U.S. Southwest); sacred values and taboo trade-offs (Tetlock et al., 2000); what money must not buy (Sandel, *What Money Can't Buy*, 2012; Zelizer, *Pricing the Priceless Child*, 1985); materialist against postmaterialist values (Inglehart, *The Silent Revolution*, 1977); the gap between the ideal and the real.
- **Feeling and the body:** the moral emotions that guard values (outrage, disgust, pride, guilt); the outrage when something sacred is given a price; the body as a carrier of values (modesty, purity, fitness, sobriety).
- **Everyday practice:** how people spend time and money, as revealed values; choices of school, church and neighborhood; buying by values (fair trade, local, organic, union-made, "buy Canadian") and boycotts; volunteering; voting; arguments at family dinners; mission statements and "values" posters at work; what people refuse to do for money.
- **Life course:** what parents say they want in children (the WVS list of child qualities; the GSS's Kohn items on obedience against thinking for oneself); catechism, Sunday school, Latter-day Saint seminary; character education; Mexico's civics and ethics courses (*Formación Cívica y Ética*) and the curriculum that replaced them *(check)*; values said to be "taught at home"; elders as keepers of values; values at the deathbed (what people say mattered).
- **Ritual:** pledges, oaths and flag ceremonies (the Pledge of Allegiance; Mexico's Monday *honores a la bandera* in schools); citizenship ceremonies; wedding vows; baptisms and confirmations; Thanksgiving; Día de Muertos as remembrance; school assemblies that name a "value of the month".
- **Institutions:** schools and their curricula; churches; youth groups (Scouts, 4-H, church youth groups) and their oaths and pledges; service clubs (Rotary's Four-Way Test); corporate codes and certifications (B Corporations *check*); professional ethics codes; broadcasters' own standards; foundations and think tanks that promote values.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for "principled" people; charges of hypocrisy and of "selling out" (*vendido*); shunning; boycotts and cancellation; expulsion from a profession or association for breaking its code.
- **Expression:** sermons and proverbs; political speeches that invoke "our values"; *Ecotopia* (Callenbach, 1975); *Habits of the Heart* (Bellah et al., 1985), drawn in part from interviews in California; corporate slogans (Google's "Don't be evil", dropped from its code's preface in 2018); songs and *corridos* of right and wrong; political advertising.
- **Exemplars:** figures held up as embodying a place's values (John Muir; César Chávez and Dolores Huerta; Benito Juárez; Native leaders, as their nations honor them; town and church founders); contested figures, praised by one side and condemned by the other.
- **History:** the founders' values (Yankee communal moralism; Spanish Catholic values; Native values, as the nations state them); what each migration added; the gold rush; the counterculture; the culture wars; secularization; the Juárez Reform and the long church-state conflict in Mexico.
- **Measures:** the World Values Survey and the Inglehart-Welzel cultural map (country; regional analyses where samples allow *check*); the postmaterialism index (WVS); Schwartz's values (the Portrait Values Questionnaire items in the WVS *check*); the Rokeach Value Survey (1973); the Moral Foundations Questionnaire; PRRI's American Values Atlas (state and metro); Pew's political typology and its 2021 study of what makes life meaningful; GLOBE's "as is" against "should be" scores (the gap between practice and ideal); Hofstede; the Environics research on social values (Adams, *Fire and Ice*, 2003, the U.S. against Canada by region); Mexican values surveys (INEGI's ENCUCI, 2020; Banamex's national values survey *check*).
- **Variation:** religion; generation; region; party; urban and rural; class; immigrants and the native-born; each Indigenous nation's own teachings; whether elites and ordinary people name the same values.
- **Relations:** self-worth; wealth; faith, belief and conviction (Sagittarius); justice (Libra); duty and tradition (Capricorn); community and idealism (Aquarius).
- **Who it applies to:** companies (corporate values); nations; whether machines and AI can have or follow "values" (present-day debates on aligning AI with human values).
- **Silences:** values people won't admit to (money, status, comfort); the gap between stated and lived values.
- **Change:** secularization; postmaterialism and its reversals; polarization; "values" as political shorthand.
- **The senses:** disgust as a guard on values (the moral disgust felt at waste, cheating or impurity; Haidt's work on disgust and morality); sacred smells and sounds (incense and copal, church bells, the drum; sweetgrass and cedar, as the nations share their use); silence as a value (the silence of a Quaker meeting; silence before elders); plain food as a virtue, against abundance as hospitality; fasting as a value made bodily; "clean" eating as virtue; food chosen for values (vegan, local, organic, halal, kosher); music heard as a badge of values (country songs of faith and family; protest songs); plain or modest dress as a value anyone can see.
- **Space, place and things:** values written on walls: mission statements in lobbies, scripture over doorways, "Live, Laugh, Love" signs at home, yard signs that list a household's beliefs (the "In this house, we believe..." signs, from 2016 *check*), flags and banners; the Mexican town built around its church and plaza, a plan that states what comes first; the town square against the mall; value-of-the-month posters in schools; monuments and their removal (the statues of Junípero Serra pulled down in California in 2020); sacred places that must not be built on, as the nations describe them; homes kept without a television, or without alcohol; the bookshelf as a display of values.
- **Time and rhythm:** sabbaths and days of rest as values set in time (Sunday closing; the Saturday sabbath of the Adventists of Loma Linda); family dinner against overtime, and which one gives way; the yearly calendar of values (Thanksgiving and gratitude; Earth Day, since 1970; Lent and self-denial; Día de Muertos and remembrance); values over a lifetime (youthful ideals, middle-aged compromise, a return to faith in old age), as questions to test; generational labels (boomers, millennials, Gen Z), tested against data; talk of "the way things used to be"; how long a boycott or a pledge is kept.
- **The economy of it:** buying by values (fair trade, organic, local; the "buy Canadian" campaigns of 2025); values-based investing and the backlash against it; values as branding (Patagonia; REI closing its stores on Black Friday since 2015 *check*); what may not be sold (organs, children, votes, sacred objects), the markets that exist anyway, and how people feel about them; paying for an education in values (religious and private schools); giving as values put into money (studies of charitable giving by state and province); the price of refusing work on principle; artists who sell their songs to advertisers, and the charge of "selling out"; the moral economy: a crowd's shared sense of a fair price (E. P. Thompson, 1971).
- **Conflict and crisis:** the culture wars; school-board fights over books and lessons; church splits over values (the United Methodist split over sexuality, 2019-2024; the Anglican diocese of New Westminster's blessing of same-sex unions in 2002, and the parishes that left it *check*); family rifts over politics; protest movements; crises that reorder what people say matters (the COVID-19 pandemic; the 2008 crash); moral panics; clashes between groups whose values meet in one place (loggers and environmentalists in the timber wars, under "Resources" in section 0).
- **Margins and extremes:** zealots, and those who live by one value above all others (fundamentalism is Sagittarius's); high-control groups that impose a whole system of values; nihilism and cynicism at the other end; people who live their values to the limit (tree-sitters such as Julia Butterfly Hill, who lived in a California redwood from 1997 to 1999; Catholic Worker houses; conscientious objectors); the medical framings: moral scrupulosity as a form of obsessive-compulsive disorder; psychopathy as the absence of moral feeling.

- **Source types** (by how widely they exist; specific sources are chosen when a culture is researched; see `Shared/Source_Types.md`):
  - *Everywhere:* proverbs and maxims, the most compact statements of values; fables and folktales with a moral; teaching stories, as the nations share them; what elders tell the young; blessings and curses (what is wished on others); oaths and vows; taboos (what may not be done, eaten, said or traded); what may not be sold (people, land, sacred things, children, the dead, a vote); rules of hospitality; how a family or a village talks through a question of right and wrong; words of praise and blame; children named after virtues; songs that teach right and wrong; children's own rules of fair play ("no fair!", "cheater"); gossip, and what people are criticized for; the gifts given at life events (what a culture says is worth giving); sacred objects and places that may not be priced.
  - *Writing:* scriptures, catechisms and religious teaching; sermons; conduct books and etiquette manuals; the culture's own moral philosophers and thinkers; political speeches and manifestos; the mission statements of churches, schools and companies; school readers and civics textbooks; children's moral literature (fables, primers, picture books with a lesson); advice columns; ethical wills and parents' letters to their children; utopian writing; self-help books on finding one's values.
  - *Public records:* national mottos, anthems, pledges and oaths; school curricula on civics, ethics and character; citizenship study guides; speeches of office (inaugurals, national days); public-service campaigns (what the state urges people to do); citations for honors and awards (what is rewarded); official values surveys; census questions on religion; statements of "our values" in public documents.
  - *Mass media:* political advertising; editorials and opinion columns; talk radio and debate shows; family sitcoms and telenovelas (what a "good family" does); advice columns; children's television; religious broadcasting; commercials that appeal to values (family, freedom, nature, tradition); documentaries with a cause.
  - *Online:* "core values" and mission pages; moral debates in forums and comment sections ("am I wrong?" threads); online petitions and boycotts; values quizzes; influencers' statements of what they stand for; call-out and cancel campaigns, and their targets; online sermons and devotionals.

  - *Organizations:* churches and religious orders; youth organizations and their oaths, codes and badges (Scouts, Guides, 4-H); service clubs and their creeds (Rotary's Four-Way Test); civic and fraternal orders; advocacy and movement groups; ethics committees of hospitals and professions; think tanks and foundations; parent-teacher associations; character-education programs; schools founded on a set of values (religious schools, Montessori and Waldorf schools); environmental groups; cultural centers that teach a people's values, as the nations share them.
  - *The people who handle it:* clergy; teachers; parents and grandparents; counselors and therapists (values clarification); ethicists and ethics consultants; human-resources staff who write and apply codes of conduct; coaches; journalists and editors; speechwriters; youth leaders.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' and missionaries' judgments of local morals, read critically; culture-shock guides (what newcomers must learn); immigrant orientation materials on "our values"; foreign correspondents' accounts.
  - *Scholarship and data:* values surveys (the World Values Survey, the European Values Study, Schwartz's surveys); moral-foundations research; ethnographies of morality; studies of sacred values and taboo trade-offs; content analyses of textbooks, proverbs and advertising for the values they teach; the history of ideas; studies of value change across generations; experiments on what people refuse to sell.

**Key questions:** When values collide, which wins? What is sacred: what may not be bought, sold or traded?

## 5. Security

**Working definition:** protection against loss and want: keeping one's livelihood, holdings and person safe now and in the future.
**Facets:** financial security (savings, insurance, pensions); job security; housing and tenure security; food security; the security of property against theft and violence; social security (public programs); security in old age; the inner sense that one's world is secure (ontological security, Giddens, 1991).
**Neighbors and opposites:** safety, protection, certainty, peace of mind, a safety net; insecurity, precarity, risk, want. **Excess:** fear of all risk; the fortress mentality; stockpiling against every danger. **Deficiency:** precarity; recklessness (Aries).

- **Words:** "security", "safety net", "nest egg", "rainy-day fund", "peace of mind", "job security", "tenure", "financially secure", "insecure" (about money, and about oneself); Spanish *seguridad* and *seguro* (safety, certainty and insurance in one family of words); in Mexico *la inseguridad* means crime and violence; *seguro social*; *colchón* (a cushion of savings); *guardadito* (money put aside); *tener plaza* or *ser de base* (holding a permanent post, in Mexican public employment); *Dios proveerá* (God will provide); *más vale prevenir que lamentar*; *más vale pájaro en mano que cien volando* (a bird in the hand).
- **Beliefs and standing:** who should provide security: oneself, the family, the community, the employer, the state or God? Saving against trusting in Providence; insurance as prudence or as a lack of faith (the Amish, elsewhere, refuse most commercial insurance); the "risk shift" from employers and the state to households (Hacker, *The Great Risk Shift*, 2006); freedom against security (the WVS item that asks people to choose *check*); the ant and the grasshopper as a moral.
- **Feeling and the body:** worry about money (the American Psychological Association's Stress in America surveys); sleep lost to money; the calm of a secure job; fear of crime and how it shapes movement at night; the relief of a full pantry.
- **Everyday practice:** saving and emergency funds; buying insurance; locks, alarms and bars on windows (*protecciones*, *rejas*), walls topped with broken glass; dogs; guns kept for protection; gated communities (*fraccionamientos cerrados*, *privadas*); preparing for earthquakes and fires; keeping cash or dollars at home; choosing a "safe" career; joining a union; mutual aid.
- **Life course:** piggy banks (*el cochinito*); children's savings accounts (California's CalKIDS, 2022); advice to get a secure post; pensions and retirement; old-age security (Social Security; Canada's Old Age Security; Mexico's universal pension for older adults, since 2019).
- **Ritual:** earthquake drills (the Great ShakeOut, begun in Southern California in 2008; Mexico's national drill each September 19, the anniversary of the 1985 and 2017 earthquakes); house blessings; New Year customs for prosperity (yellow underwear; lentils *check*); saints and amulets for protection; retirement parties.
- **Institutions:** social insurance (U.S. Social Security, 1935; Canada's Old Age Security, 1952, and the Canada Pension Plan, 1966; Mexico's IMSS, 1943, ISSSTE and the Afores, 1997); health coverage (Medicare and Medicaid; Canada's medicare; Mexico's Seguro Popular, then INSABI, then IMSS-Bienestar); unemployment insurance; secure posts (academic tenure, the civil service, union seniority); tenant unions and associations; insurers and their retreat from wildfire zones; private security firms and neighborhood watch groups; food programs and food banks; mutual-aid societies (Mexican American *mutualistas*; Chinese benevolent associations; fraternal insurance orders).
- **Rewards and sanctions:** praise for the prudent; scorn for the improvident; the shame of losing a home; and the reverse, mockery of the overcautious and of preppers.
- **Expression:** Aesop's ant and grasshopper; "Fortress L.A." in Mike Davis's *City of Quartz* (1990); prepper media; insurance advertising; songs of hard times; Depression-era stories.
- **Exemplars:** Francis Townsend of Long Beach, whose old-age pension movement (1933) helped bring about Social Security; Henry Kaiser and the health plan born in his wartime shipyards (Kaiser Permanente, 1945); Upton Sinclair's End Poverty in California campaign (1934); savers and preppers; anti-exemplars: the spendthrift, the swindler who takes people's savings.
- **History:** famine and plenty in each people's history, as the nations tell it; frontier insecurity; the Depression and the Dust Bowl; Mexico's peso crises (1976, 1982, 1994-1995) and the lessons they taught about savings; the internment, which cost Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians nearly everything; the 2008 crash and its foreclosures; wildfires and insurance.
- **Measures:** the Federal Reserve's Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking (the ability to cover a $400 emergency); FINRA's National Financial Capability Study (by state); the CFPB Financial Well-Being Scale; food security (USDA's Current Population Survey supplement, by state; the Canadian Income Survey; the First Nations Food, Nutrition and Environment Study; the food-access measure in Mexico's official poverty data, made by CONEVAL and now by INEGI *check*); housing insecurity (the Census Household Pulse Survey); Statistics Canada's Survey of Financial Security and Canadian Financial Capability Survey; INEGI's ENIF and its survey of financial health (ENSAFI *check*); perceived insecurity (INEGI's ENVIPE by state and ENSU by city); feeling safe walking alone at night (Gallup's Law and Order Index, country); guns owned for protection (Pew); household gun ownership by state (RAND's estimates *check*); gated and walled communities (the American Housing Survey *check*); insurance coverage (Census; Mexico's insurers' association, AMIS *check*); job tenure (BLS); the Global Preferences Survey's patience item (country).
- **Variation:** class; age; gender (fear of crime); gig workers against salaried workers; renters against owners; Native nations and food security; regions exposed to quakes and fires; whether security is sought through family or through institutions.
- **Relations:** stability; wealth; ownership; protection and shelter (Cancer); fear (Pisces); trust (Scorpio); independence (Aries); freedom (Sagittarius).
- **Who it applies to:** the nation (national security); companies; the land and the food supply; data and computer security; whether people feel machines and AI make them more or less secure, at work and at home (present-day surveys).
- **Silences:** money worries kept from children; denied insurance claims; how unprepared people really are.
- **Change:** the gig economy; housing costs; insurers leaving fire country; climate risk; fear of crime in politics and the news; AI and job security.

## 6. Stability

**Working definition:** continuity: things, places, relationships and routines staying as they are over time.
**Facets:** residential stability (staying in one place); job and career stability; marital and family stability; routine and habit; stable prices and money; political and institutional stability; emotional stability; continuity across generations (family farms, firms, names).
**Neighbors and opposites:** steadiness, continuity, permanence, constancy, being settled, roots; change, upheaval, flux, instability, restlessness. **Excess:** stagnation, rigidity, complacency, the rut. **Deficiency:** chaos, rootlessness, volatility.

- **Words:** "stable", "steady", "settled", "settle down", "steady job", "put down roots", "old-timer", "a rolling stone gathers no moss", "if it ain't broke, don't fix it", "stick-in-the-mud", "in a rut"; Spanish *estabilidad*, *estable*, *sentar cabeza* (to settle down), *echar raíces*, *arraigo* (rootedness), *chamba estable*, *más vale malo conocido que bueno por conocer* (better the bad you know than the good you don't); "since time immemorial", as nations speak of their presence on the land.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is stability a virtue or a sign of a closed mind? - **Feeling and the body:** calm and boredom; the comfort of routine; the stress of moving and of job loss; emotional stability (the reverse of neuroticism in the Big Five).
- **Everyday practice:** daily routines; Sunday meals; staying in one house; staying with one employer; brand loyalty; keeping the same barber, doctor and church; and, against these, the habits of frequent movers and job-changers.
- **Life course:** routines for children; changing schools; the age of "settling down" (marriage, the first home, *sentar cabeza*); midlife changes; aging in place.
- **Ritual:** anniversaries of weddings, jobs and towns; service pins; centennials; century-farm ceremonies; the weekly and yearly round of meals and feasts; seasonal ceremonies that renew the world, as the nations share them.
- **Institutions:** tenure and seniority; the civil service; central banks (Banxico's inflation target; the Bank of Canada's); family firms; parishes and congregations that keep the same families for generations; long-lived associations (the Grange halls of rural Oregon and Washington); neighborhood associations that fight change; historical societies and preservation groups.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** respect for old families and long service; suspicion of job-hoppers and drifters; and the reverse, mockery of the stuck ("townie").
- **Expression:** songs of home and of staying ("Home on the Range") against songs of leaving; novels of family land; the old farmhouse in art and advertising.
- **Exemplars:** century farmers; long-lived family firms (Nordstrom, Seattle, 1901); the Hudson's Bay Company (1670) and its liquidation in 2025 *(check)*; elders who stayed; Edith Macefield, who would not sell her Ballard house (2006).
- **History:** Native continuity in place against settler mobility; the region as a place of churn (the gold rush, booms and busts); Mexico's long one-party stability (1929-2000) and its end; the peso crises; the post-war boom.
- **Measures:** years in the current home and moves in the past year (ACS; Statistics Canada; INEGI); job tenure (BLS's biennial Employee Tenure supplement); divorce and the length of marriages; emotional stability by state (Rentfrow et al., 2008); tightness and looseness (Harrington and Gelfand, 2014); uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede; GLOBE); inflation (BLS CPI by metro; INEGI's INPC by city *check*); political stability (World Bank governance indicators, country); counts of century and centennial farms (Oregon; Washington *check*).
- **Variation:** rural and urban; old families and newcomers; class (stability as a luxury, or as being stuck); immigrants and refugees; generation.
- **Relations:** security; stubbornness; beginning and change (Aries); order (Capricorn); home and roots (Cancer); rebirth and crisis (Scorpio).
- **Who it applies to:** institutions, currencies and governments; ecosystems and the climate; the land; machines and systems (reliability and uptime).
- **Silences:** the costs of stability: people stuck in bad jobs, marriages and places.
- **Change:** housing costs and gig work making stability rarer; remote work; disaster displacement.
- **The senses:** the comfort of the familiar: the same smells in a grandparent's house, the same songs and dishes at every holiday, the diner that never changed its menu; the sounds that mark the hours (church bells; the noon whistle of a mill or a fire station); the shock of a changed place (a landmark torn down; a view blocked by towers); the body's routines (the same breakfast, the same chair, the same walk); the senses in a new place after a move (new light, new sounds, new smells), and how long it takes before it smells like home.

## 7. Wealth

**Neighbors and opposites:** riches, prosperity, affluence, abundance, comfort; poverty, want, scarcity. **Excess:** greed, avarice, luxury. **Deficiency:** poverty; or, held up as an ideal, holy poverty.

## 8. Possessiveness

**Working definition:** holding on to what one sees as one's own, including people, beyond what the bond or the thing warrants, and the urge to keep others away from it.
**Facets:** over partners (jealousy, control); over children; over objects (refusing to share or lend); over territory (turf, surf breaks, parking spots, "my seat"); over knowledge and credit; over places and resources (localism).
**Neighbors and opposites:** jealousy, territoriality, clinging, guarding; generosity, sharing, letting go, trust. **Excess:** control, coercion, violence. **Deficiency:** indifference, neglect.

## 9. Stubbornness

**Working definition:** holding to a position, habit or course against persuasion, evidence or pressure.
**Facets:** intellectual (refusing to change one's mind); behavioral (refusing to change habits); moral firmness (holding to principle); tenacity (refusing to give up); refusing to admit error or apologize; refusing to sell or move (holdouts); collective (communities and nations that will not yield).
**Neighbors and opposites:** firmness, tenacity, perseverance, resolve, conviction, obstinacy; flexibility, open-mindedness, compromise, yielding. **Excess:** pig-headedness, dogmatism, the feud. **Deficiency:** spinelessness, the pushover, the "flip-flopper".

- **Words:** "stubborn", "bullheaded", "pig-headed", "mule-headed", "ornery", "cussed", "contrary", "set in his ways", "dig in one's heels", "won't budge", "holdout", "die on that hill", "my way or the highway", "double down"; in praise, "steadfast", "dogged", "tenacious"; "flip-flopper" as the insult for the opposite; Spanish *terco*, *necio* (in Mexico, stubborn or insistent rather than foolish), *cabezón*, *cabeza dura*, *testarudo*, *obstinado*, *aferrado*, *más terco que una mula*, *no dar su brazo a torcer* (not letting one's arm be twisted), *sostenella y no enmendalla* (hold to it and don't amend it *check*), *chaquetero* (a turncoat).
- **Beliefs and standing:** is holding firm a virtue (principle, constancy, loyalty) or a vice (pride)? Is changing one's mind wisdom or weakness? Admitting error as humility or as loss of face; martyrs and holdouts; honor cultures and not backing down; religious steadfastness.
- **Feeling and the body:** the felt certainty of being right; crossed arms, silence, refusal; anger when pressed; identity-protective cognition (Kahan); holding fast to a belief after it fails (Festinger et al., *When Prophecy Fails*, 1956).
- **Everyday practice:** negotiation and haggling; family feuds; quarrels between neighbors; refusing to sell (holdout homes); boycotts and strikes; refusing new technology or rules; meetings run by consensus, where one person can block.
- **Life course:** the "strong-willed child" (Dobson, *The Strong-Willed Child*, 1978); teenage rebellion; elders "set in their ways"; deathbed reconciliations or their refusal.
- **Ritual:** ritual refusals (vigils, sit-ins, hunger strikes, picket lines; the occupation of Alcatraz by Indians of All Tribes, 1969-1971); public apologies as the opposite (Canada's 2008 residential-schools apology; the 1988 apologies to Japanese Americans and Japanese Canadians; Governor Newsom's 2019 apology to California Native peoples; Mexico's 2021 apologies to the Yaqui and Maya peoples).
- **Institutions:** unions and long strikes; standoffs with the state (Malheur, 2016; the Wet'suwet'en camps against the Coastal GasLink pipeline, 2019-2020, which supporters call defense of their territory and opponents call obstruction); church splits over doctrine; bodies that decide by consensus, where one member can block (Quaker meetings; co-op boards); mediators, as a profession for breaking deadlocks.
- **Rewards and sanctions:** honor for those who held out; mockery of the stubborn; the losses of impasse; the labels "principled" and "obstinate", and who hands them out.
- **Expression:** *The Story of Ferdinand* (Leaf, 1936); Melville's Bartleby and his "I would prefer not to" (1853); the mule in Western tales; rancheras of pride; holdout stories in the news.
- **Exemplars:** Edith Macefield of Ballard; Billy Frank Jr. (Nisqually), who kept fishing through the fish wars of the 1960s and 1970s; Hiroo Onoda, the Japanese soldier who held out until 1974 (elsewhere); contested figures: the Bundys; leaders who never apologized.
- **History:** Native peoples' long refusal to give up their land and fishing places, as the nations tell it; Yankee dissenters; Mexican traditions of resistance; long strikes; the Hetch Hetchy fight.
- **Measures:** Big Five openness and agreeableness by state (Rentfrow et al., 2008); need for cognitive closure across cultures (Webster and Kruglanski, 1994); dogmatism (Rokeach, 1960); grit (Duckworth et al., 2007); conformity across cultures (Bond and Smith, 1996); the length of strikes (BLS work-stoppage data); polarization (Pew); the WVS child quality "determination, perseverance"; how often people say they changed their mind on an issue *(check)*.
- **Variation:** gender (who is called stubborn and who determined); age; class; party; region; whether a group's firmness is read as principle or obstruction depending on who does the reading.
- **Relations:** stability; conviction and fundamentalism (Sagittarius); pride and honor (Leo); discipline (Capricorn); grudge and revenge (Scorpio); indecision and peace at any price (Libra), its opposites.
- **Who it applies to:** animals (the mule, the bull); institutions and bureaucracies; nations; machines that "won't" do what people want (present-day frustration with technology, and with AI that refuses requests).
- **Silences:** regret; mistakes never admitted; the cost of a feud to the family.
- **Change:** polarization; algorithms that reinforce views; changing norms of public apology.

- **Space, place and things:** holdout houses surrounded by new building (Edith Macefield's, under Exemplars; the Chinese term "nail house", *dingzihu*, for the same thing); people who will not leave a home in a fire or flood zone; protest camps and picket lines; land a family refuses to sell to developers; the old car kept running for decades; place names people refuse to give up, or to take up (Mount Rainier and Tahoma; the California ski resort renamed Palisades Tahoe in 2021, which some still call by its old name *check*).

## 10. Sensuality

**Working definition:** enjoyment of the senses (taste, touch, smell, sound and sight) and of the body's comfort and pleasure.
**Facets:** food and drink; touch, warmth and texture; scent; music and sound; beauty to the eye; rest and comfort; bathing, massage and hot springs; dance; luxury; nature felt through the senses; sexuality as sensual pleasure (sex itself is Scorpio's).
**Neighbors and opposites:** pleasure, enjoyment, comfort, indulgence, hedonism; asceticism, self-denial, numbness, prudishness. **Excess:** gluttony, hedonism, addiction. **Deficiency:** joylessness, puritanism, sensory deprivation.

  - *Mass media:* cooking and food-travel shows; restaurant reviews; food and wine magazines; advertising for food, drink, perfume, bedding and comfort; radio formats built on mood (boleros, slow jams, "quiet storm"); dance shows; romance novels and films that dwell on the senses.
  - *Online:* food photographs and food videos; ASMR videos (pleasure in sound and touch); recipe sites and their comments; "cozy" and comfort aesthetics; spa and self-care content; eating broadcasts (*mukbang*).
  - *Commerce:* menus and the categories of restaurants; spas, massage, saunas and hot springs; perfume, candle and incense sales; mattress and bedding marketing; comfort clothing; the chocolate, coffee, wine and craft-beer trades; cannabis shops; luxury travel; sensory testing (how companies test taste, smell and feel on local panels); the flavor and fragrance industry.
  - *Organizations:* wine and food societies and tasting clubs; Slow Food chapters; dance clubs and dance halls; choirs and music societies; gardening clubs; naturist clubs; temperance and abstinence groups, on the other side; religious communities that fast or abstain; cooking schools.
  - *The people who handle it:* cooks, chefs and bakers; sommeliers and bartenders; massage therapists; spa workers; perfumers and flavorists; hairdressers; dancers and dance teachers; midwives and doulas (touch in birth); caregivers of the old (touch at the end of life); sensory scientists who run taste panels.
  - *Outsiders:* travelers' accounts of local food, smells, music and touch; visitors' complaints and delights; guides on whether to greet with a kiss, a hug or a handshake.
  - *Scholarship and data:* the anthropology of the senses; food studies and the anthropology of food; studies of touch and personal space across cultures; studies of ideal affect and of indulgence; nutrition and time-use data; sensory science (taste panels across cultures); histories of pleasure, diet and asceticism.

**Key questions:** Are the pleasures of the senses a good, a danger or a sin? Which pleasures are public and which private, and for whom?

## Candidate subtopics (PROPOSED, for the author to settle)

**What this section is.** Subtopics this meta-category could hold beyond its ten proposed concepts (the list itself is not yet settled), explored for the author (2026-11-10: "One sign, explore possibilities for the subtopics, go in as much depth and detail as you need"). Every entry is PROPOSED. The concept list above stays as it is. The author decides whether each candidate becomes a concept, a facet of a current concept, a concept in another sign, or nothing. Each entry gives a working definition; why it belongs here, and which other sign could hold it; what it would add; and a few avenues. The candidates come from five places: the sign's traditional areas and folkways; the Taurus research notes (CurrentNovelDocs, `worldbuilding/Robot_Fundamental_Concepts/01 - The twelve meta-categories/02 - Taurus (possessions and value)/1 - Human understanding (research)/Low-level concepts (2026-10-30).md`, 148 concepts, and the gaps its notes file lists); the Tier 2 concepts and near-synonym groups (`Shared/Concept_Lists_by_Sign.md` and the concept index it cites); concepts other languages name that English lacks; and splits of the current concepts. Candidates on property, money, debt and inheritance are framed through custom, practice, feeling, commerce and community only.

**From the sign's traditional areas** (possessions, money, resources, values, self-worth; and the sign's folkways, food and dress; money and resources have no concept of their own)

### C1. Money
- *Working definition:* the means of payment and store of value a people uses, and the meanings, uses and feelings attached to it.
- *Why here:* money is one of the sign's traditional areas and has no concept; the questions at the top of this file note that it "is not on the list" and is covered in section 0, beside wealth. The research notes give money, and their gaps name money as a system (debt, trade, earning a living) as missing. *Also fits:* Scorpio (shared resources), Virgo (work and pay), Capricorn (ambition).
- *What it would add:* wealth asks what abundance is; money asks how the medium itself is handled: earmarked, hidden, talked about or kept silent, kept apart from love, given as a gift or refused as one (Zelizer, *The Social Meaning of Money*, 1994).
- *Avenues:* the money bullets in section 0 (words, earmarking, who manages a household's money, cash against cards, money talk and its silences); "dirty" and "clean" money; money as a gift (cash at weddings, red envelopes at the Lunar New Year, the *baile del billete*) and where cash as a gift is an insult; children's first money (allowances; the tooth fairy); religious teaching on money; tipping.

### C2. Resources
- *Working definition:* what the land, the water and the living world provide, and how a people regards, takes, shares and keeps it: as a gift, as kin, as stock or as a commodity.
- *Why here:* resources are a traditional area of the sign, now one bullet of section 0 (water, forests, fish, minerals, personal resources). The research notes give resourcefulness ("knowing what can be done with what is at hand") and custodianship. *Also fits:* Scorpio (the commons and shared resources, where Possession's Relations place the commons), Cancer (the land as home), Aquarius (the common good).
- *What it would add:* a home for the ethics of taking from nature (respect for the first fish or the first fruits; "take only what you need"; waste; extraction; stewardship), which no current concept holds whole.
- *Avenues:* the resources bullet in section 0; hunters', fishers' and gatherers' ethics of taking, as each people states them; "wise use" against preservation; how each community feels about mining, logging and dams near it; conservation habits at home (water, energy); communities that manage a shared resource themselves (Ostrom, under Ownership); surveys of environmental attitudes.

### C3. Food and eating
- *Working definition:* what a people eats; how it gets, prepares, shares and judges food; and what eating means beyond nourishment.
- *Why here:* food ways are Fischer's folkway for this sign (the long food-ways bullet in section 0); the research notes give appetite and nourishment; Sensuality holds food only as a pleasure. *Also fits:* Cancer (feeding and nurture), Virgo (health and diet), Leo (feasting as celebration).
- *What it would add:* a concept for one of the best-recorded areas of any culture, with its own measures (diet surveys, food spending, time spent eating), in place of a bullet in section 0 and a facet of Sensuality.
- *Avenues:* the food-ways bullets in section 0; meal times, and who eats with whom; the foods of feasts, fasts and mourning; food rules and taboos; food and status; food as identity (Aries, identity); hunger and food aid; children's food rules (the clean plate); cookbooks and food television.

### C4. Dress and clothing
- *Working definition:* what people wear; how clothing is made, bought, kept, mended and passed on; and what it says about the wearer.
- *Why here:* dress ways are Fischer's folkway for this sign (runner-up Leo), now one bullet of section 0; the research notes give dress as natural fabrics in earthy colors, or conspicuous jewelry worn to show substance. *Also fits:* Leo (display), Aries (C2, appearance and self-presentation).
- *What it would add:* clothing as a possession and a value (materials, care, mending, cost, plainness), apart from clothing as display, which an Aries or Leo concept would take.
- *Avenues:* the dress-ways bullet in section 0; natural against synthetic fibers; mending and hand-me-downs; Sunday best and work clothes; uniforms; regalia, as the nations describe it; dress codes; spending on clothing; fast fashion and resale.

### C5. Thrift and waste
- *Working definition:* careful use of money and things, so that nothing is wasted; and how a culture judges waste.
- *Why here:* thrift is half of section 0's "Thrift and display" bullet, and a child quality in the World Values Survey ("thrift, saving money and things"); the research notes give resourcefulness and the accounting of resources. *Also fits:* Virgo (order, improvement), Capricorn (discipline).
- *What it would add:* a value with strong measures and many sayings that neither Security nor Wealth holds: saving and mending as virtues; waste as a sin, or as a mark of status (C29, *mottainai*, gives one language's word for the feeling).
- *Avenues:* the thrift sayings and the WVS item in section 0; saving rates; mending, reuse and repair; leftovers and food waste; coupons and bargain hunting; Depression-era habits passed down in families; frugal-living communities online; how the thrifty are mocked as cheap (C42).

### C6. Exchange and trade
- *Working definition:* how goods change hands for other goods or for money (buying, selling, bartering, bargaining), and what people think a fair price is.
- *Why here:* section 0's "Prices and fair exchange" bullet; the research notes' gaps name trade and the economy as missing, and the notes give reciprocity as fair exchange and earning. *Also fits:* Libra (fairness and reciprocity, where fairness itself lives), Gemini (markets as places of talk).
- *What it would add:* the market as a cultural institution: where haggling is expected and where it is rude, whom one may sell to, the pride or shame of selling, and the line between trade and greed.
- *Avenues:* markets and market days (*tianguis*, swap meets, farmers' markets); haggling against fixed prices; barter between neighbors; anger at price gouging; "you get what you pay for" and *lo barato sale caro*; the moral economy, a crowd's shared sense of a fair price (Thompson, 1971); relations between shopkeepers and their regular customers; selling to friends and kin, and whether one may profit from them.

### C7. Debt and credit
- *Working definition:* owing and being owed (in money, goods and favors), and how people feel about it.
- *Why here:* section 0's "Saving, credit and debt" bullet; the research notes' gaps name debt as missing from the sign's material. Framed here through custom, feeling and commerce only. *Also fits:* Scorpio (shared resources, power), Capricorn (obligation), Libra (reciprocity).
- *What it would add:* the moral weight of owing (shame, gratitude, dependence, power over the debtor), which neither Security nor Wealth names.
- *Avenues:* buying on trust at the corner store (*fiado*), and the sign *hoy no fío, mañana sí*; *cuentas claras, amistades largas*; loans between kin and friends, and what they do to friendships; credit cards, and pride in "owing nothing"; whether debt is felt as shame or as normal; favors owed, and the Japanese *on* and *giri*, debts of gratitude and of social obligation *check*; religious teaching on lending and on forgiving debts (the Jubilee year); the household-debt figures in section 0.

### C8. Poverty and want
- *Working definition:* lacking what a culture holds necessary; and how that lack is explained, named, lived, shamed or honored.
- *Why here:* poverty is wealth's opposite and possession's deficiency; the research notes' gaps name poverty, and note that the sign's material never describes a life without possessions. *Also fits:* Pisces (compassion), Aquarius (equality), Libra (fairness).
- *What it would add:* the view from below: how the poor name their own condition, and whether poverty is a shame, a misfortune, a test, a virtue (holy poverty) or an injustice.
- *Avenues:* whether people say the poor are lazy or unlucky (the WVS and GSS items, under Wealth); *pobre pero honrado*; ethnographies of poverty; how aid is asked for and given; whether people call themselves poor; children's sense of being poor; voluntary poverty in religious orders; the words for the poor and how they change ("the deserving poor", "low-income", *los de abajo*).

**From the sign's own research notes** (the 148 concepts in the Taurus research folder's concept table)

### C9. Attachment and letting go
- *Working definition:* the bond to things, places and people, and the ability, or the duty, to give them up.
- *Why here:* the research notes give attachment, letting go (what the sign's material says it resists) and preservation (Midas, whose gold kills what it keeps); release / letting go / surrender is a near-synonym group. *Also fits:* Scorpio (purging), Pisces (surrender).
- *What it would add:* Possession is having; this is the release: decluttering, death cleaning, giving away, moving on, and teachings against attachment to things.
- *Avenues:* decluttering methods and their popularity (Kondo; the Japanese *danshari*, "refuse, discard, separate" *check*; the Swedish *döstädning*, "death cleaning"); how the bereaved part with a dead person's things; religious teaching on not storing up treasure; minimalism; downsizing in old age; letting go of a house, a farm or a homeland.

### C10. Accumulation
- *Working definition:* gathering and keeping more than present use requires: stores, savings, collections, hoards.
- *Why here:* the research notes give hoarding ("accumulating instead of knowing its own measure"; "the hoard never satisfies") and consolidation; Possession now holds accumulation only as its excess. *Also fits:* Scorpio (power), Capricorn (ambition).
- *What it would add:* whether a culture admires the one who stores up (the ant, the full granary, the nest egg) or the one who spends and gives away, as a question apart from hoarding disorder.
- *Avenues:* granaries and stores in each people's history; collecting as a pastime; "more is better" against "enough" (C25); stockpiling; the parable of the rich fool who built bigger barns (Luke 12); attitudes to the very rich who keep accumulating; savings rates read as a value.

### C11. Materialism
- *Working definition:* the importance a person or a culture gives to owning things as a path to happiness, success and standing.
- *Why here:* the research notes give materialism ("measuring worth by what can be held or owned"); Possession names it as an excess. Other languages name its smug, homebound form: Russian *meshchanstvo* (a petty, comfortable philistinism *check*); German *Spießbürger*. *Also fits:* Sagittarius (meaning), Leo (display).
- *What it would add:* a value orientation that can be measured (the Material Values Scale, Richins and Dawson, 1992; Kasser's work on materialism and well-being) and set against postmaterialism (under Values).
- *Avenues:* materialism scales across groups; advertising and the "good life"; critiques of consumerism (Buy Nothing Day, from Vancouver); religious teaching against "the things of this world"; children's wish lists; what people say they would do with a windfall.

### C12. Greed
- *Working definition:* wanting more than one's share or need, and taking it.
- *Why here:* the research notes give greed among the vices in the sign's material ("greed and unrestrained hedonism"); Wealth names greed and avarice as its excess. *Also fits:* Scorpio (power), Pisces (wrongdoing), Libra (fairness).
- *What it would add:* the moral judgment of wanting too much, with its own words, stories and sanctions, which Wealth (a condition) and Possessiveness (guarding what one has) don't hold.
- *Avenues:* avarice among the seven deadly sins, and in sermons; "greed is good" (*Wall Street*, 1987) and how people quote it; the greedy in folktales (King Midas; the fisherman's wife); words for the greedy (*codicioso*, *avaro*; in Mexico *agandallarse*, grabbing more than one's share *check*); anger at "corporate greed"; children taught not to take the biggest piece; economic games that show what people count as taking too much (the ultimatum game across societies, Henrich et al., under Possessiveness).

### C13. Comfort and coziness
- *Working definition:* ease of body and mind in a warm, safe and familiar setting, and the value placed on it.
- *Why here:* the research notes give comfort (in the sign's material "a symptom, not the core need"), warmth and rest; section 0 gives comfort one line ("Comfort and the body's needs"). Other languages name the cozy kind directly: Danish *hygge*, Dutch *gezelligheid*, German *Gemütlichkeit*, Mexican Spanish *a gusto*. *Also fits:* Cancer (home and shelter), Leo (joy).
- *What it would add:* whether comfort is a good to seek, a reward to earn, or a softness to fear ("get out of your comfort zone"), and the material culture of comfort (the armchair, the blanket, the warm kitchen).
- *Avenues:* *hygge* and its export as a lifestyle trend (around 2016 *check*); comfort food; "comfort zone" talk; heating, insulation and how warm homes are kept; slippers, pajamas and loungewear; who denies themselves comfort (fasting, hard beds, cold showers), and why.

### C14. Rest and idleness
- *Working definition:* not working: rest, leisure and idleness, and the line a culture draws between deserved rest and laziness.
- *Why here:* the research notes give rest, laziness (contested in the sign's material: laziness, or energy saved on purpose?) and restlessness (Io, stung so that she can never rest); procrastination is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs. Other languages praise idleness: Italian *dolce far niente*, Dutch *niksen*; Mexican Spanish *flojera* and *hueva* name it with a smile or a sneer. *Also fits:* Virgo (work), Capricorn (discipline), Leo (play).
- *What it would add:* the other side of work, which Virgo's concepts would treat only as an absence.
- *Avenues:* time-use data on leisure and sleep; vacation days taken and left unused; the siesta; the sabbath; claims of laziness aimed at groups (recorded as claims, never adopted); "hustle culture" against the slogan "rest is resistance"; retirement as earned rest; sayings on idleness ("the devil finds work for idle hands").

### C15. Routine and habit
- *Working definition:* doing the same things in the same way and at the same times, and the value placed on that sameness.
- *Why here:* the research notes give routine (a routine held as a possession) and habit; routine is a Tier 2 concept, under seven signs. *Also fits:* Virgo (order), Capricorn (discipline).
- *What it would add:* the everyday side of Stability: whether routine is felt as comfort, as discipline, or as a rut.
- *Avenues:* daily schedules from time-use data; bedtime and mealtime routines for children; the same breakfast, the same seat; habit praised as virtue (Benjamin Franklin's chart of daily virtues) or scorned as deadness; self-help books on habits and their sales; how people react when a routine breaks (a store closes; a show is canceled; a bus route changes).

### C16. Patience and waiting
- *Working definition:* bearing delay calmly, and letting things ripen in their own time.
- *Why here:* the research notes give patience (with long, slow processes others abandon) and slowness; patience is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs; Aries proposes impatience (Aries C5), with patience as its pole. *Also fits:* Aries (C5, impatience), Capricorn (time and discipline), Pisces (surrender).
- *What it would add:* the slow virtues (waiting, slow work, deferred reward) as a value of their own.
- *Avenues:* delay-of-gratification studies across cultures; proverbs of patience (*poco a poco se va lejos*; "Rome wasn't built in a day"); waiting in lines and offices (under Self-worth, Time and rhythm); slow movements (slow food, slow travel); the patience of farmers and craftsmen; patience in religious teaching (Job); how long courtships and engagements last.

### C17. Reliability
- *Working definition:* being someone, or something, others can count on: keeping one's word, showing up, finishing, being on time.
- *Why here:* the research notes give reliability (in the sign's material, the one others build long-term plans around "because it actually shows up"), punctuality, commitment and follow-through; reliability is a Tier 2 concept, under seven signs. *Also fits:* Capricorn (duty, responsibility), Scorpio (trust), Virgo (work).
- *What it would add:* the dependable person as a cultural ideal, with measures of its own (punctuality, promises kept) and words of praise and blame ("flaky"; in Mexico *cumplido* for the one who keeps his word, and *quedar mal* for letting someone down *check*).
- *Avenues:* punctuality norms (Levine, *A Geography of Time*, 1997); "flakiness" and the "Seattle Freeze" as regional claims to test; promises and how they are kept; the tradesman who never shows up; the reliability of machines and of public services, and how people talk about it.

### C18. Loyalty
- *Working definition:* staying faithful to a person, a group, a place, a brand or a cause through time and at a cost.
- *Why here:* the research notes give loyalty ("deep and unglamorous"); loyalty is a Tier 2 concept, under ten signs, and has no home yet. *Also fits:* Scorpio (trust and betrayal), Cancer (family), Aquarius (friendship), Leo (honor). Its home may be better in one of those.
- *What it would add:* faithfulness kept over time, the steady side of three Taurus concepts (Stability, Possessiveness and Stubbornness).
- *Avenues:* loyalty to employers, and its decline; brand loyalty; fans who stay with a losing team; loyalty to a hometown; friendships across decades; turncoats and traitors (*chaquetero*, under Stubbornness); loyalty programs as the market's imitation of it.

### C19. Craftsmanship
- *Working definition:* making things well with skilled hands, and pride in work that is not careless or half-done.
- *Why here:* the research notes give craft (artisans, laborers, farmers and builders; "skilled hands that refuse careless or half-finished work"), method and art. *Also fits:* Virgo (work, perfectionism), Leo (creativity).
- *What it would add:* the value placed on things made well and made to last, which joins Possession (well-made things), Values and Self-worth (pride in one's work).
- *Avenues:* craft traditions and their apprenticeships; handmade against mass-made; folk arts as their makers share them (carving, weaving, pottery, saddle-making, the stitched leather of *piteado* belts); repair culture; "they don't make them like they used to"; the maker movement; heritage fellowships and craft awards.

### C20. Cultivation
- *Working definition:* growing things (gardens, crops, orchards and herds), and the patience and attachment this asks of people.
- *Why here:* the research notes give cultivation (growing plants patiently from seed and staying near the garden), fertility and land. *Also fits:* Virgo (work), Cancer (nurture).
- *What it would add:* the gardener's and the farmer's bond with land and growth, which is not the same as owning land.
- *Avenues:* home gardens and community gardens; the family *milpa* and the orchard; farmers' markets; seed saving; county fairs and their prize vegetables; the farm as identity; gardening in time-use and hobby surveys; planting and harvest festivals, as each people shares them.

### C21. Hospitality
- *Working definition:* receiving and feeding guests, and the duties of host and guest.
- *Why here:* the research notes give hospitality (in the sign's material, "without limits") and the remembering of occasions; hospitality rules appear in this file only among section 0's source types. Greek *philoxenia*, "love of strangers", names it as a virtue. *Also fits:* Cancer (home), Leo (generosity), Libra (reciprocity).
- *What it would add:* the code for sharing one's possessions with others, with its own rules of offering, refusing and returning.
- *Avenues:* *mi casa es su casa*; what a host must offer, and how many times a guest must refuse before accepting; potlucks against hosted dinners; *sobremesa*; the scale of feasts; how strangers and newcomers are received; the hospitality trade (hotels, restaurants) against hospitality at home.

### C22. Earning and spoiling
- *Working definition:* the belief that what one has should be earned by one's own work, and its opposite, being given what one has not earned (spoiling).
- *Why here:* the research notes give earning ("everything should be earned"), reward, entitlement and spoiling (in the sign's material, the known failure of raising children). *Also fits:* Capricorn (merit), Virgo (work), Cancer (child-rearing), Aries (C20, entitlement).
- *What it would add:* the link a culture draws between work and having, and how it raises children to it (chores, allowances, "you have to earn it").
- *Avenues:* allowances tied to chores, or not; words for spoiled children (*chiqueado*, *consentido*, *malcriado*; "spoiled brat"); inherited wealth and "trust-fund kids"; who is "deserving" of help; tips and bonuses; studies of paying children for grades or chores; parenting advice on spoiling.

### C23. Practicality
- *Working definition:* preferring what works, and what can be seen and touched, to theory, show and talk.
- *Why here:* the research notes give practicality ("grounded in what works, not what sounds impressive"), realism, skepticism of anything unproven, and truth settled by what the senses report. German *Bodenständigkeit* (being rooted, down to earth) names the quality. *Also fits:* Virgo (usefulness), Sagittarius (truth), Capricorn.
- *What it would add:* the "down-to-earth" ideal, and suspicion of intellectuals and theory (Hofstadter, *Anti-intellectualism in American Life*, 1963), as a value of its own.
- *Avenues:* "common sense"; "book smart" against "street smart"; practical against academic schooling; the trades against university; pragmatism as a philosophy; "show me" (Missouri's nickname, elsewhere); proverbs of practicality (*del dicho al hecho hay mucho trecho*: from saying to doing is a long way).

### C24. Beauty
- *Working definition:* what a culture finds beautiful in things, places and people, and the worth it places on beauty.
- *Why here:* the research notes give beauty (an earthy, unpolished beauty rooted in nature) and appreciation; Values includes valuation, and beauty is one of the kinds of value. *Also fits:* Libra (harmony), Leo (art), Virgo (perfection).
- *What it would add:* aesthetic value beside money value and moral value: what is kept because it is beautiful, and what beauty is worth.
- *Avenues:* what people decorate, and how; natural beauty and scenic places, and the fights to protect a view; ideals of bodily beauty (Aries, C1 and C2); folk art; how homes are decorated; "pretty" against "useful"; spending on beauty; C34 (*wabi-sabi*) for the beauty of the worn.

### C25. Contentment and "enough"
- *Working definition:* being satisfied with what one has, and knowing when one has enough.
- *Why here:* the research notes give contentment ("private contentment") and its shadow, complacency. Other languages have words for "enough": Swedish *lagom* ("just the right amount"); Chinese *zhī zú*, "knowing what is enough", from the *Daodejing* *check*. *Also fits:* Pisces (surrender), Leo (joy), Sagittarius (meaning).
- *What it would add:* the limit on wanting: the opposite pole of Greed (C12), Materialism (C11) and Accumulation (C10).
- *Avenues:* life-satisfaction surveys; "enough" in religious teaching (Paul: "I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content"); simple-living movements; *lagom* as an exported idea; how a household decides it has enough; the "number" people say they need to retire.

**From the Tier 2 concepts and the near-synonym groups** (`Shared/Concept_Lists_by_Sign.md`; the concept index's "Concepts under six or more signs" and "Names to reconcile later"; routine, patience, reliability and loyalty, above, are Tier 2 concepts too)

### C26. Resistance to change, and stagnation
- *Working definition:* resisting change even when it is needed, and the stillness that follows.
- *Why here:* stagnation is a Tier 2 concept, under seven signs, and change is one, under six; the research notes give change (resisted even when clearly needed), stagnation, complacency and "calcification". *Also fits:* Aries (beginning), Scorpio (crisis), Aquarius (reform).
- *What it would add:* the judgment of stability's excess (the rut) as a topic of its own; it is now one line under Stability.
- *Avenues:* openness to change in values surveys (Schwartz's conservation against openness); "if it ain't broke, don't fix it"; complacency in firms and towns; stagnant economies and dying towns, as their people describe them; blame between generations ("OK, boomer"); research on status-quo bias.

### C27. Envy
- *Working definition:* pain at another's good fortune, and the wish to have what they have, or for them to lose it.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group envy / jealousy sits under Possessiveness; envy is the shadow of Wealth (whose Beliefs line asks how a culture holds envy in check). *Also fits:* Aries (competition), Leo (pride), Scorpio.
- *What it would add:* jealousy guards what one has; envy covets what others have. Many cultures build customs around it: the evil eye, leveling, the "crab in the bucket", the tall poppy.
- *Avenues:* evil-eye beliefs and remedies (*mal de ojo*), as communities describe them; Foster's "image of limited good" (1965, from a Michoacán town) and its critics; envy in proverbs (*la envidia mata*); German *Schadenfreude*, joy at another's misfortune; the crab mentality (Aries, competition); research on benign and malicious envy (van de Ven and colleagues *check*); envy on social media.

### C28. Class
- *Working definition:* the ranked groups a society sorts people into by wealth, work, schooling, family and taste, and how people place themselves and others among them.
- *Why here:* the near-synonym group status / rank / reputation touches Wealth and Self-worth (the questions at the top of this file); Wealth's facets include old and new money, and its Words include *gente bien*, *naco* and *fifí*. *Also fits:* Capricorn (hierarchy), Leo (being seen), Aquarius (equality).
- *What it would add:* Wealth is having; class is the standing that wealth, taste and family turn into, with its many markers (Bourdieu, *Distinction*, 1979; Fussell, *Class*, 1983).
- *Avenues:* the class people say they belong to, in surveys; class markers in speech, dress, food and names; *naco* and *fifí* as class insults; mobility between classes (under Wealth); class in jokes and telenovelas; "middle class" as an identity; class at school.

**Concepts other languages name that English lacks** (besides those already in C7, C9, C11, C13, C14, C23 and C25)

### C29. *Mottainai* (regret over waste)
- *Working definition:* the Japanese word for the regret felt when something of worth is wasted, and the cry "what a waste!".
- *Why here:* one word that joins Possession, thrift (C5) and Values: things have a worth that waste insults. The Kenyan environmentalist Wangari Maathai took the word up in her campaigns (2005 *check*). *Also fits:* Virgo (order), Capricorn (discipline).
- *What it would add:* whether waste is felt as a wrong done to the thing itself, and not only as a loss to its owner.
- *Avenues:* thrift in Japanese American and Japanese Canadian households, as they describe it; words for waste in the pilot's languages (*desperdicio*; *¡qué desperdicio!*); food-waste data; campaigns against waste; repair and reuse.

### C30. *Querencia* and *arraigo* (the place one belongs to)
- *Working definition:* Spanish *querencia*, the place one loves and returns to, where one feels safe (in bullfighting, the spot in the ring the bull makes its own); *arraigo*, rootedness; *terruño*, one's home soil.
- *Why here:* section 0's "Land and attachment to place" bullet; the research notes give land, territory and refuge. English has no single word for the safe place one has chosen and keeps returning to. *Also fits:* Cancer (home and belonging), Capricorn (heritage).
- *What it would add:* attachment to place as a value apart from owning it: people deeply attached to places they don't own, and owners attached to none.
- *Avenues:* place-attachment research (under section 0); talk of a "home place"; going home to die; migrants' *terruño*; the nations' own teachings on land and belonging, as they state them; the Welsh *cynefin*, a habitat or place of belonging *check*; the region's songs of place.

### C31. Inalienable treasures (*at.óow*, *taonga*)
- *Working definition:* things a group keeps out of exchange forever because they carry its identity: crests, regalia, relics, crown jewels, family treasures.
- *Why here:* Weiner's "inalienable possessions" (*Inalienable Possessions*, 1992, under Possession's Beliefs); the Tlingit *at.óow*, owned clan treasures, as the nations describe them (under Ownership's Words); the Māori *taonga*, treasures that include language and knowledge, as Māori describe them *check*. *Also fits:* Capricorn (heritage), Sagittarius (the sacred), Cancer (lineage).
- *What it would add:* the class of things that may never be sold, which neither Possession nor Ownership names.
- *Avenues:* relics and church treasures; regalia and crests, as the nations share them; family heirlooms never to be sold; museums' returns of objects; national treasures; the shame of "selling the family silver"; sacred objects offered at auction, and the protests that follow.

### C32. Connections as wealth (*guanxi*, *palanca*, *compadrazgo*)
- *Working definition:* a person's web of kin, friends and contacts, counted as a resource that can be drawn on.
- *Why here:* section 0 names personal resources (time, skills, kin and contacts; Lomnitz on reciprocity among the poor of Mexico City); Chinese *guanxi*, Mexican Spanish *palanca* (leverage: a contact who can pull strings; *tener palancas* *check*) and *compadrazgo* (the bonds of godparenthood) name it, where English has only the scholars' "social capital". *Also fits:* Gemini (connection), Aquarius (community), Capricorn (hierarchy).
- *What it would add:* wealth held in people rather than things: who you know, as part of what you have.
- *Avenues:* *compadrazgo*, and the *padrinos* who share the costs of a celebration (under section 0); *guanxi* in the region's Chinese communities, as studied; finding work through contacts (Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties", 1973); the exchange of favors; networking culture; measures of social capital (Putnam).

### C33. Giving owed from wealth (*tzedakah*, *zakat*)
- *Working definition:* giving from one's wealth treated as a duty owed, not as a free kindness.
- *Why here:* Hebrew *tzedakah* (giving, from a root meaning "righteousness") and Arabic *zakat* (the alms owed on wealth, one of the pillars of Islam) frame giving as owed, where English "charity" frames it as kindness; church tithing (under section 0) is the region's best-known form. *Also fits:* Leo (generosity), Pisces (compassion), Capricorn (duty), Sagittarius (faith).
- *What it would add:* whether giving is a duty that comes with wealth, and to whom it is owed.
- *Avenues:* tithing in practice; giving by congregation; Jewish and Muslim giving in the region's communities, as they describe it; giveaways and the potlatch, as the nations describe them; *noblesse oblige* and the duties of the rich; surveys of giving.

### C34. *Wabi-sabi* (beauty in the worn and imperfect)
- *Working definition:* the Japanese ideal of beauty in things that are simple, imperfect, aged and passing.
- *Why here:* it joins Possession (old and worn things), Beauty (C24) and thrift (C5), and sets the worn against the new in a way English has no word for; *kintsugi*, mending broken pottery with gold, shows it in practice. *Also fits:* Libra (harmony), Pisces (impermanence).
- *What it would add:* whether a culture values the patina of age or the shine of the new.
- *Avenues:* antiques and patina; "distressed" furniture as a fashion; mending made visible (*kintsugi*; the visible-mending movement in clothing); the Japanese gardens of the region; *wabi-sabi* as an imported lifestyle idea, and how it changed in the move.

**Splits of the current concepts**

### C35. Sharing and lending (split from possession)
- *Working definition:* letting others use what is one's own, for a time or for good, and the customs that govern it.
- *Why here:* Possession now holds lending, borrowing and sharing as facets; the research notes give generosity toward guests and a wariness of obligation. *Also fits:* Libra (reciprocity), Leo (generosity), Aquarius (community).
- *What it would add:* the everyday norms of "what's mine is yours", with institutions of their own (tool libraries, gifting groups) and their own lessons for children.
- *Avenues:* sharing taught in preschool; borrowing tools and cups of sugar from neighbors; what may never be lent (a car, a toothbrush, a spouse's things); returning what was borrowed; sharing food; experiments on children's sharing (House et al., 2013, under Possessiveness); the "sharing economy" and how much sharing is in it.

### C36. Heirlooms and inheritance customs (split from possession and ownership)
- *Working definition:* how things, land and houses pass from the dead to the living by custom and family feeling, and what is kept as an heirloom.
- *Why here:* Possession and Ownership each hold inheritance as a facet; the research notes give inheritance among the things fought over between "my resources" and "our resources"; Possession's Relations now place inheritance with Scorpio. This candidate covers customs and family feeling only. *Also fits:* Scorpio (where the file now places inheritance), Cancer (family).
- *What it would add:* the moment possessions cross generations: who gets what, what is fair among heirs, and what may never be sold.
- *Avenues:* inheritance customs (equal shares; the youngest son who stays with the parents; the eldest; the child who cared for the parents to the end; Robichaux's Mesoamerican family system, under Ownership); family quarrels over heirlooms; heirlooms and their stories; how the bereaved divide things; what the young no longer want (the "brown furniture" of their grandparents); ethical wills.

### C37. Owning the intangible (split from ownership)
- *Working definition:* the feeling, and the custom, that songs, stories, names, recipes, designs and ideas belong to someone.
- *Why here:* Ownership's facets include ideas, names, songs and crests; its Words give owned songs, crests and stories on the Northwest Coast, as the nations describe them; its Who it applies to gives artists' anger at AI trained on their work. Framed through custom and feeling only. *Also fits:* Leo (creativity), Gemini (information).
- *What it would add:* claims that don't rest on holding a thing: who may tell a story, sing a song, cook a family's recipe or use a design.
- *Avenues:* the protocol of asking first, as the nations share it; the secret family recipe; comedians' anger at "stolen" jokes; plagiarism as a shame at school; arguments over cultural appropriation (headdresses at festivals, under section 0's dress ways); credit and attribution at work; artists' campaigns against AI trained on their work.

### C38. Valuation and price (split from values)
- *Working definition:* how worth is put on things (by price, by use, by the labor in them, by sentiment, by rarity), and what must never be priced.
- *Why here:* Values now holds valuation as a facet, and the questions at the top of this file keep the two together for now; the research notes give valuation ("what things are worth, and to whom") apart from values. *Also fits:* Libra (fairness of price), Sagittarius (the sacred).
- *What it would add:* "value" in its economic and sentimental senses, kept apart from values as moral priorities.
- *Avenues:* sentimental worth against market worth (what people won't sell at any price); the just price; appraisal shows and the surprise of learning what a thing is "worth"; "priceless"; sacred values and taboo trade-offs (Tetlock, under Values); how people price their own time and labor; the endowment effect (under Possession's Measures).

### C39. Providing (split from self-worth)
- *Working definition:* supporting one's family materially, and the worth and standing that come from doing it.
- *Why here:* Self-worth's Variation asks "whether men's worth is tied to providing"; the research notes give support (the steady anchor others lean on), earning and reliability. The author writes for men, so the provider ideal gets an entry of its own here, framed as questions for each group, and for women who provide. *Also fits:* Cancer (family), Virgo (work), Capricorn (duty).
- *What it would add:* the provider role as a source of worth, and of pain when a man cannot provide, which no current concept holds whole.
- *Avenues:* surveys on whether a man should be the main earner (Pew; the WVS item on a wife who earns more than her husband *check*); men's worth after a job loss (under Self-worth's Conflict and crisis); migrants who provide from afar through remittances; *el proveedor* in Mexican family talk; couples in which the wife is the main earner, and how they speak of it; fathers' advice to sons on providing.

### C40. Display of wealth (split from wealth)
- *Working definition:* showing what one has, or hiding it, and the judgments that follow.
- *Why here:* Wealth's facets include wealth shown or hidden; section 0's "Thrift and display" bullet; the research notes give luxury (silk, satin, fur, velvet) and conspicuous jewelry worn to show substance. *Also fits:* Leo (being seen), Aries (C2, appearance).
- *What it would add:* the line between pride and vulgarity in showing wealth, and the reasons people hide it (envy, modesty, fear).
- *Avenues:* conspicuous consumption (Veblen, under section 0); stealth wealth and "quiet luxury"; *buchón* style and *corridos tumbados*; *quinceañera* and wedding spending; luxury brands and their counterfeits; the rich who live plainly (Stanley and Danko, under Wealth); giveaways and the potlatch, as the nations describe them.

### C41. Territoriality and localism (split from possessiveness)
- *Working definition:* claiming a space and defending it against outsiders: a seat, a street, a surf break, a neighborhood, a town.
- *Why here:* Possessiveness now holds territory as a facet; the research notes give territory (in the sign's material, the first to fence its ground against intruders). *Also fits:* Cancer (home), Scorpio (power), Aquarius (community).
- *What it would add:* the group's claim to space, apart from jealousy in love.
- *Avenues:* surf and fishing localism; "locals only" signs and stickers; neighbors who fight new housing or shelters near them ("not in my backyard"); gang turf and its boundaries; resentment of newcomers (the "Don't Californicate Oregon" stickers, under Aries, beginning); studies of territoriality in people and in places.

### C42. Stinginess (split from possessiveness)
- *Working definition:* refusing to give, share or spend more than one must.
- *Why here:* Possessiveness's facets include refusing to share or lend, and Wealth's Words give *codo* (stingy); generosity, its opposite, is in Leo's ten. *Also fits:* Leo (generosity, its opposite).
- *What it would add:* the vice opposite generosity, with its own jokes, gestures, insults and tales (Scrooge).
- *Avenues:* jokes about the stingy; the elbow tapped to call someone *codo* *check*; who pays the check (Aries, self-assertion); stinginess with tips; stinginess as a claim aimed at groups (recorded as a claim, never adopted); the miser in folktales; the line between thrifty (C5) and cheap.

### C43. Steadfastness (split from stubbornness)
- *Working definition:* holding firm to a person, a principle or a course, as a virtue; constancy.
- *Why here:* Stubbornness now holds both the vice and the virtue (moral firmness, tenacity); the research notes give determination, commitment and constancy; persistence / perseverance / endurance is a near-synonym group. *Also fits:* Sagittarius (conviction), Aries (C18, endurance), Capricorn (discipline).
- *What it would add:* the praised side of holding firm, with heroes and words of its own ("steadfast", "faithful"; *firme*, *constante*), kept apart from the blamed side.
- *Avenues:* hymns and sermons on steadfastness; constancy in love (songs of fidelity); keeping one's word; long service honored; holdouts praised after the fact; "stay the course" in politics and sport.

### C44. Admitting mistakes (split from stubbornness)
- *Working definition:* saying one was wrong, and apologizing.
- *Why here:* Stubbornness's facets include refusing to admit error or to apologize; the research notes give mistakes and blame (refusing to admit a mistake and blaming subordinates instead); blame is a Tier 2 concept, under six signs. *Also fits:* Libra (diplomacy), Capricorn (responsibility), Leo (face).
- *What it would add:* the cultural scripts of apology, and of losing or saving face when one is wrong.
- *Avenues:* studies of apology across cultures; public apologies (under Stubbornness's Ritual); "sorry" in Canadian English; *perdón*, *disculpe* and *lo siento*, and when each is used; whether leaders may admit mistakes and keep their standing; blame-shifting at work; how children are taught to apologize.

### C45. Touch (split from sensuality)
- *Working definition:* who may touch whom, how and where, and what touch means.
- *Why here:* Sensuality's facets include touch, warmth and texture, and its Feeling line gives touch norms (Jourard; Sorokowska et al.); Mexican Spanish *apapacho* (under Sensuality's Words) names a warm, soothing touch. *Also fits:* Cancer (care), Scorpio (intimacy), Gemini (greeting).
- *What it would add:* touch as a whole topic: in greeting, comfort, child-rearing, care for the old and the sick, and among strangers.
- *Avenues:* greeting with a kiss, a hug or a handshake; touch between men, and how it has changed; touch and children ("give Grandma a kiss", under Aries, autonomy); touch in care work; touch deprivation, and "skin hunger" in the 2020 lockdowns; massage; touch in worship (the sign of peace; the laying on of hands).

### C46. Indulgence and restraint (split from sensuality)
- *Working definition:* how freely people may satisfy their wants for pleasure, food, drink and fun, and how much they are expected to hold back.
- *Why here:* Sensuality's excess (gluttony, hedonism) and deficiency (joylessness, puritanism) frame it; the research notes give self-indulgence (generosity toward oneself tipping into excess); Hofstede's indulgence-restraint dimension measures it (under Sensuality's Measures). *Also fits:* Capricorn (discipline, self-control), Leo (play).
- *What it would add:* the moral line on pleasure as a concept of its own, apart from the pleasures themselves.
- *Avenues:* indulgence scores by country; "treat yourself" against "everything in moderation"; Lent and Ramadan; diet culture; the history of temperance movements; "guilty pleasures"; treats and sweets for children, and who rations them.
