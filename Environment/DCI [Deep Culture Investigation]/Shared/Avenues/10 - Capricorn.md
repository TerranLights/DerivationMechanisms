# Avenues of investigation: 10 Capricorn (authority and duty)

**What this file is.** For each Capricorn concept, every avenue through which a culture's understanding of it can be learned. `Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md` gives the families of angles that apply to every concept; this file makes them specific to each Capricorn concept. It is written for any culture in the world, with examples drawn mostly from the pilot (Cascadia) and from Spanish and English, because those are the pilot's languages. Examples are prompts for where to look, not findings.

**Marks.** *(check)*: a source, dataset, term or fact given from memory, to be confirmed before use. Indigenous ceremonies, stories and practices are researched only as the nations themselves share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold. **Law** is treated here as a cultural concept: what law means to people and how they relate to it. The formal legal system is one of its avenues; the system itself is profiled in Stage 1 (`Templates/T1_Lineage_Profiles.md`, profiles 01 and 02).

**The concept list** (**not yet settled**; it is settled with the author just before this sign's pass): authority, duty, responsibility, discipline, ambition, hierarchy, merit, law, order. Duty was settled under Capricorn by the author (2026-11-02). Questions for the author:
- **Recognition** is in Capricorn's ten too; its proposed home is Leo (cross-reference at the end).
- **Order** is in Virgo's ten too; its proposed home is Capricorn, with Virgo keeping a cross-reference. Order is also one of this sign's folkways (order ways); section 0 covers the folkway's own angle and points to section 9.
- **Discipline** is in a near-synonym group with self-discipline and self-control. This file researches all three under discipline: discipline imposed by others and discipline of oneself (as Aries researches self-reliance under independence).
- **Duty and responsibility** overlap, and Spanish splits the ground differently (*deber*, *obligación*, *cumplir*, *responsabilidad*). This file treats duty as what is owed, to whom and by what tie, and responsibility as answering for outcomes: taking charge, being accountable, bearing blame. Should they stay two entries?
- **Law** also sits in Sagittarius's traditional areas (`Shared/Investigation_Sheet.md`, Part C), and it borders justice (Libra) and crime (Pisces).
- **Hierarchy** overlaps rank ways, and **authority** overlaps power ways (both folkways here) and power and control (Scorpio).
- **Possibly missing from the list:** status, reputation and career (the sign's traditional areas, covered in section 0); obedience and deference; tradition (the Aries file points to Capricorn for it); legitimacy; loyalty; patience and the deferred reward; seniority.

## 0. The domain: authority and duty

**Traditional areas:** career, status, reputation, authority, responsibility. **The domain question:** who commands, who obeys, and what is owed?

- **Where authority comes from.** Weber's three kinds of legitimate rule: traditional, charismatic and legal-rational ("Politics as a Vocation", 1919; *Economy and Society*); Arendt's "What Is Authority?" (in *Between Past and Future*, 1961), on authority as neither force nor persuasion; the Roman split between *auctoritas* and *potestas*. For each culture: which sources people accept (age, birth, God, election, expertise, wealth, office, the land, the ancestors) and which they suspect.
- **Distance between ranks.** Hofstede's power distance; GLOBE's power distance, asked twice, as things are and as they should be (the gap is evidence too); Trompenaars's achieved against ascribed status, and universal rules against personal ties; tightness and looseness (Gelfand et al., 2011, countries; Harrington and Gelfand, 2014, U.S. states); the authority foundation of Moral Foundations Theory (Graham, Haidt and Nosek).
- **Political cultures.** Elazar's moralistic, individualistic and traditionalistic cultures (by state and within states); Woodard's Left Coast, Far West and El Norte (*American Nations*, 2011); Lipset's claim that Canadians defer to authority more than Americans (*Continental Divide*, 1990), against Michael Adams's Environics data arguing the reverse (*Fire and Ice*, 2003): a disagreement to record, not settle; Canada's "peace, order and good government" (Constitution Act, 1867, s. 91) set beside "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness".
- **The culture's own interpreters.** Mexican writing on authority, the family and the state: Octavio Paz (*El laberinto de la soledad*, 1950); Rogelio Díaz-Guerrero's "historic-sociocultural premises" of the Mexican family, such as the father's unquestioned authority and the mother's self-sacrifice, measured with scales from the 1950s on and contested since (*Psicología del mexicano*, *check* edition); Roger Bartra's critique of national-character writing (*La jaula de la melancolía*, 1987); Larissa Adler de Lomnitz on favors, *compadrazgo* and connections inside formal institutions (*check* titles). Western historians on authority in the U.S. West: Patricia Limerick, Richard White, Donald Worster (*Rivers of Empire*, 1985, on power through water). Native nations' own statements of governance, law and responsibility, as they publish them.
- **The culture's own map of the domain.** Which words it joins or splits: Spanish *derecho* is both "law" and "a right", where English splits them; *deber* is both "duty" and "must"; *cumplir* (to fulfill what is owed) has no single English match. Which of this sign's concepts the culture treats as central, and which it hardly names.
- **Outsiders' guides.** Business-culture guides on hierarchy, deciding and scheduling (Erin Meyer, *The Culture Map*, 2014); immigrant orientation guides; military cultural handbooks; guides for Americans working in Mexico and Mexicans working in the U.S. and Canada (*check* titles). Tag them practitioner; they record what newcomers are told to expect.

## 1. Authority

**Working definition:** the recognized right to direct others, decide for them or speak for them, and others' acceptance of that right.
**Facets:** parental, religious, political, legal, workplace, military and police, professional and expert (doctors, scientists, teachers), traditional (elders, hereditary chiefs) and charismatic authority; office against informal standing; authority over the body, the household and the land; obedience and its limits; questioning and resisting authority.
**Neighbors and opposites:** legitimacy, command, power, leadership, respect, deference, obedience; anarchy, rebellion, defiance, insubordination. **Excess:** authoritarianism, tyranny, abuse of office, blind obedience. **Deficiency:** a vacuum of authority, permissiveness.


**The authoritarian-libertarian axis lives here** (author, 2026-11-10): how far authority reaches into daily life, and how hard it enforces its rules.
- **The permission share** (the headline): of a fixed list of everyday activities, the share that is forbidden or needs permission. 0% is the far libertarian end, 100% the far authoritarian end (`Shared/Permission_Share_List.md`). It is scored by reading the law in force, as Libra's letter-of-the-law research does.
- **The correctional-control share** (its companion): adults in prison, in jail, on probation or on parole, as a share of all adults. It also rises with crime, so it stands beside the headline rather than replacing it.
- **Two figures,** as for the left-right measure: the government's own level, and all levels combined.
- **The numbers** are collected at Stage 2 (`Shared/Sources_and_Datasets.md`, section H). Here they are read beside everything else the culture shows about authority: whom people obey, how much permission they expect to need, and how they feel about being watched and ruled.
- **Words:** "authority", "the authorities", "the powers that be", "the Man", "the brass", "higher-ups", "boss", "bossy", "chain of command", "pull rank", "go over someone's head", "because I said so", "who died and made you king?", "let me speak to your manager", "Question Authority" (a 1970s bumper sticker), "sir" and "ma'am"; "tyee", Pacific Northwest English from Chinuk Wawa for a chief or boss, and also a big Chinook salmon (the Grand Ronde Tribes' Chinuk Wawa dictionary, 2012); Spanish *autoridad*, *las autoridades*, *el que manda*, *mandón*, *el mero mero*, *el patrón*, *el jefe* and *la jefa* (in Mexican slang also one's father and mother), *¿mande?* (the Mexican polite answer when called, literally "command [me]"; its colonial origin is a popular claim *check*), *a sus órdenes*, *cacique*, *caudillo*, *tlatoani* (a Nahuatl word for ruler, used ironically of Mexican presidents *check*), *respeto*, *lambiscón* and *barbero* (a flatterer of superiors), *igualado* (Aries, Self-assertion).

## 2. Duty

**Working definition** (settled under Capricorn by the author, 2026-11-02; wording proposed): what a person owes and must do because of a role, a tie or a promise, whether or not they wish to.

## 4. Discipline

**Working definition:** training and keeping behavior to a standard, whether imposed by others (parents, teachers, armies, employers, churches, the state) or by oneself (self-discipline, self-control, willpower).
**Facets:** discipline imposed (in the home, the school, the army, the workplace, the church, the prison); self-discipline (habits, routines, diet, exercise, study, saving); self-control of impulses and emotions; punishment as a means of discipline; the discipline of a craft, sport or art; spiritual disciplines (fasting, prayer, meditation).
**Neighbors and opposites:** self-control, willpower, grit, perseverance, restraint, rigor, training; indulgence, laxity, sloth, impulsiveness, spontaneity. **Excess:** rigidity, harshness and abuse, asceticism, perfectionism. **Deficiency:** indiscipline, laziness, being spoiled.

## 5. Ambition

**Working definition:** the wish and drive to rise, achieve or become more than one is: in work, wealth, standing, skill or service.
**Facets:** career; education; wealth; status and social climbing; political office; art and sport; ambition for one's children; collective ambition (for a family, town, nation or people); aspiration against contentment.

## 6. Hierarchy

**Working definition:** the ordering of people, groups, places or beings into higher and lower ranks, with differences of power, honor and access.
**Facets:** social class; occupational rank; organizational hierarchy (the org chart, the chain of command); age and generation; gender; race, color and ethnicity; religious hierarchy; hereditary rank; academic rank; the ranking of places; the ranking of beings in nature; the etiquette and forms of address that mark rank; flat structures and the wish for them.
**Neighbors and opposites:** rank, status, class, precedence, pecking order, chain of command; equality, egalitarianism, flatness. **Excess:** rigid inherited ranks, snobbery, domination and servility. **Deficiency:** no clear lines of responsibility; leaderless drift.

## 7. Merit

**Working definition:** deserving reward, rank or praise because of one's own effort, ability and results, rather than birth, connections or luck.
**Facets:** academic, professional, athletic and artistic merit; effort against talent; meritocracy as a system; desert in justice; merit against need, equality, seniority, loyalty, connections, inheritance, luck and grace; who defines merit (tests, judges, markets, elders).
**Neighbors and opposites:** desert, earning, achievement, worth, credentials, fair competition; privilege, nepotism, favoritism, patronage, luck, entitlement, grace. **Excess:** meritocratic arrogance, contempt for those who fail, credentialism. **Deficiency:** nepotism, cronyism, unearned privilege.

- **Words:** "merit", "deserve", "earned it", "earn your stripes", "pay your dues", "self-made", "meritocracy" (coined as satire by Michael Young, 1958), "a level playing field", "born on third base", "nepotism", "nepo baby" (2022), "the old boys' network", "it's not what you know, it's who you know", "participation trophy", "entitlement", "diversity hire" (a taunt in the fights over affirmative action; quoted, not used); Spanish *mérito*, *merecer*, *ganárselo a pulso* (to earn it by one's own effort), *el que es buen gallo donde quiera canta*, *palancas* (levers: connections), *recomendado*, *amiguismo*, *compadrazgo*, *nepotismo*, *influyentismo*, *dedazo*, *hueso* (a patronage job), *plaza* (a permanent post), *concurso de oposición* (an open competitive exam for a post), *meritocracia*, *esfuerzo*.
- **Beliefs and standing:** is the system fair enough that rewards track merit? Is merit itself earned, or a kind of luck (talent, upbringing)? ## 8. Law

**Working definition:** the binding rules of a community, and how people relate to them: what law is for, whose it is, whether it is just, and whether to obey it, use it, bend it or avoid it. The formal legal system is one avenue among many.
**Facets:** the state's statutes and courts; custom and a community's own rules; religious law; Indigenous legal orders; natural or higher law; law as protection, threat, tool or game; legal consciousness; the rule of law against the rule of persons; obeying and evading; going to court; self-help and vigilantism; how far law reaches into private life; the gap between law on paper and law in practice.

- **Change:** post-2020 arguments over policing and disorder; encampment policy; surveillance technology; militarized security in Mexico; climate disasters and the breakdown of order.
- **The senses:** noise and quiet as order (quiet hours; noise complaints about parties, *banda* from a passing car, roosters, car alarms and leaf blowers, which many California cities have restricted *check*; church bells welcomed by some neighbors and fought by others); the sounds of order breaking (glass, sirens, helicopters, a crowd's roar); smells of disorder (garbage, urine in doorways, smoke) and of order kept (cleaning products, cut grass, the scrubbed floor); the look of order (straight rows, mowed lawns, clean sidewalks, graffiti painted over) and of its absence; the noise of *desmadre* heard as joy by some and as threat by others; the feel of a crowd: safe and festive, or about to turn.
- **Space, place and things:** the order built into towns (the Spanish plaza and grid; the township survey's straight lines; cul-de-sacs; zones that keep uses apart); lawns, fences and hedges kept to neighbors' standards; sidewalks and who may use them (vendors, skateboarders, people living outside); design that keeps people from lingering (benches with dividers, spikes, fences around underpasses); barricades and police tape; litter bins, queue barriers and ticket machines; the tidy house and its hidden junk drawer (household order is Virgo's, C4); squares and streets as places of protest, and how authorities reshape them afterward.
- **Time and rhythm:** order in time (schedules, routines, quiet hours, curfews, closing times such as the 2 a.m. last call in California, garbage days, street-sweeping days); festivals of permitted disorder at set times (carnival before Lent; Halloween night; New Year's Eve) and the morning after; the order of a ceremony or a procession, and what happens when it slips; the swing between disorder and crackdown in a city's history; spring cleaning; the night as the time when order loosens.
- **The economy of it:** what order costs and who pays (police, private security, sanitation, street cleaning, code enforcement); private guards against public police; how order shows up in property values ("a nice neighborhood"); the industry of services for people living outside, and the fights over its spending; the cleaning and organizing industry; business improvement districts that buy extra order for a few blocks; who bears the costs of order (fines, towed cars and tents, sweeps).
- **Conflict and crisis:** riots and their aftermath (Watts, 1965; Los Angeles, 1992; Vancouver, 1994 and 2011); protests that became contests over order (Seattle's WTO protests, 1999; Portland, 2020); earthquakes that broke order and showed how people restore it (San Francisco, 1906; Mexico City, 1985; Loma Prieta, 1989); wildfires and evacuations; disaster research finding that looting is rarer than people expect, set against what people fear *check*; COVID-19 lockdowns as order imposed on daily life; the 2022 convoy in Ottawa and at border crossings; Tijuana's years of violence and how the city speaks of recovering from them.
- **Margins and extremes:** total order (strict homeowners' associations; gated enclaves; zero-tolerance policing at its most rigid); places where order has broken down (large encampments and the streets around them, described by their residents and their neighbors); hoarding and compulsive tidiness as illness at the two ends of household order; people who seek disorder (punks; anarchists; desert festivals that build a temporary city with their own rules); drifters who live outside every routine; the line a culture draws between harmless mess and dangerous disorder.

### C1. Career
- *Working definition:* a working life seen as a whole and as a path: how it is chosen, climbed, changed and ended, and how far a person is their job.
- *Why here:* career is one of the sign's traditional areas and has no concept of its own; section 0 holds it as a bullet (how careers are chosen, ladders and titles, one career or many, measures); the research notes list career (profession and public standing, set against home); the file's opening names it among the possibly missing. *Also fits:* Virgo (work, in its ten; C42, vocation and calling; C43, retirement), Aries (identity: "what do you do?"), Leo (C44, the dignity of work), Sagittarius (C51, schooling as the way up).
- *What it would add:* the career as a story people tell about themselves (the ladder, the calling, the job that "pays the bills"); who chooses it (the person, the parents, the family trade, a test); one career for life against many moves (Sennett, *The Corrosion of Character*, 1998); the company man and his decline (Whyte, *The Organization Man*, 1956); the careers of women and of mothers; the Mexican sense of *carrera* as the degree one studied; what happens to men whose careers stall or end, framed as a question for each group, with women's experience alongside.
- *Avenues:* section 0's career bullet and its measures (ACS occupations; BLS job tenure; INEGI's ENOE; Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey); career guidance in schools (*orientación vocacional*) and career days; professional-network profiles as public career records; obituaries as career summaries; Wrzesniewski and colleagues (1997) on work as a job, a career or a calling; Kanter, *Men and Women of the Corporation* (1977); oral histories of working lives.

### C2. Status and standing (position in a ranked order)
- *Working definition:* the position a person, family or group holds in a ranked order, with the rights, duties, manners and way of life that go with it, whether given at birth or won.
- *Why here:* status is one of the sign's traditional areas; section 0 holds it as a bullet (what confers standing, Mexican status words, the MacArthur ladder, occupational prestige, skin color and attainment); the research notes list status and status obsession; the near-synonym group status / rank / reputation / public life points here. - *What it would add:* ascribed against achieved status, as a question for each group; status groups with their own ways of life (old families, the professions, the clergy, the military); what a status obliges (C27, *noblesse oblige*); status that does not add up (the rich newcomer without standing; the educated poor); losing status (demotion, retirement, divorce, migration); status anxiety; how openly status may be talked about.

### C3. Reputation (the record that follows a person)
- *Working definition:* the standing a person, family, firm or place has earned over time, and the records (of credit, work, conduct and crime) that carry it from one setting to the next.
- *Why here:* reputation is one of the sign's traditional areas; section 0 holds it as a bullet (*el qué dirán*, credit scores, online reviews, professional-discipline records, background checks, the reputation of places, how a damaged name is repaired); the research notes list reputation and disgrace (a lasting stain on one's name); Gemini's closing line leaves reputation to "Capricorn or Leo".
