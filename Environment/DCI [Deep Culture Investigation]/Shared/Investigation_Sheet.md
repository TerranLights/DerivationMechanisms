# How to investigate a meta-category and its concepts

STATUS: draft v0.1 (2026-11-10), for the author to review. v0 was checked by an independent review for missing angles the same day; its findings are folded in. Once settled, this sheet replaces the eight questions in `Templates/T4_Sign_Pass.md`.

**What this is.** The step-by-step sheet for Stage 4: how to approach each meta-category (sign) and each of its concepts, in each unit. DCI is a full, in-depth cultural investigation (author, 2026-11-10). The aim is **every approach, angle and topic that could possibly bear on how a culture understands a concept.**

**The companion files.**
- `Shared/Avenues/NN - <Sign>.md`: every avenue for each concept of each sign, concept by concept.
- `Shared/Regional_Appendix_North_America.md`: the pilot region's languages, dictionaries, archives and datasets. Other regions get their own appendix when their first target begins. This sheet stays the same for every culture.

**How to use it.**
- **Every family is asked for every concept.** An **honest null** (one line saying what was looked for and not found) is always a valid answer, in any family. Breadth never becomes padding.
- **The angles are prompts, not limits.** An angle the culture shows that this sheet lacks is followed, and noted for the next version.
- **One home per item.** Each kind of evidence has one family (Part B's notes say where shared items live). Facts already in earlier stages are cited, not repeated: the lineage profiles (Stage 1), measured data (Stage 2). New measures found here go into the unit's Stage 2 file, so each fact lives in one place.
- **Founders first, then later layers,** in the families that carry history: B2, B5, B9, B10, B14, B15, B16.
- **Every claim is tagged** (measured, official, scholarly, synthesis, classic, practitioner, claim; and *artifact*, proposed in Part F) with its read mark.

---

## Part A: before the sign

**A0. The unit's source pack** (once per unit, before its first sign, reused for every concept). The regional dictionaries and corpora; proverb collections; the literary, musical and film canon; the pool of exemplars (heroes, award lists); the festival and commemoration calendar; the key ethnographies and community studies; the nations' own sources; the Stage 1-3 files. Building it once saves searching for the same works in every concept.

**A1. Settle the concept list** with the author (`Shared/Concept_Lists_by_Sign.md`).

**A2. Map the meta-category's whole domain,** not only its concepts. Each sign has traditional areas of life (from the Robot Fundamental Concepts meta-category files; Aries: the self, the body, appearance, beginnings, initiative). These are investigated too, in Part C.

**A3. Build the concept map,** for each concept:
- **A neutral working definition,** written in plain words that any language can carry (Wierzbicka and Goddard's Natural Semantic Metalanguage is the model), so it doesn't smuggle in English assumptions.
- **Facets, near-neighbors and opposites.** Every family researches the facets and opposites, not only the word.
- **Excess and deficiency,** where the culture treats the concept as a mean between two faults; "not seen as a mean" is a valid answer.
- **Links to concepts in other signs.**
- **Search terms in each of the unit's languages:** the word, its rivals, its opposites, its slang.
- **Standard situations:** a fixed set of everyday situations in which the concept is at stake, asked in every unit (for courage: facing physical danger; standing up to a boss; speaking against the crowd; leaving home for good). Searching by word misses the cases nobody names; the same situations everywhere also give Stage 5 and the comparison across nations a common footing.
- **A depth flag for each family:** full, brief, or null expected (several families are known to come back thin; see Part F).

**A4. Gather the inputs:** the source pack (A0); the sign's folkways (`Shared/Folkways_Crosswalk.md`); the unit's seams (the target's scope, section 4). Project canon is read so that disagreements can be recorded, never used as evidence.

**A5. List each unit's languages and voices:** its languages and varieties, trade languages, immigrant languages; whose voices the sources carry and whose they might miss.

## Part B: each concept, in each unit

Twenty-three families of angles, then the synthesis (B24).

### B1. Words and meaning
- **The words:** the core term in each language, rivals, near-synonyms, words with no English equivalent, words that cross concepts (Mexican Spanish *coraje* means anger more often than courage).
- **Etymology and history** of the words; **keywords** (Williams, *Keywords*) and conceptual history (Koselleck).
- **Connotation:** praise, blame or neutral; formal or everyday; old-fashioned, religious, political, corporate.
- **Collocations** ("rugged individualism", "self-made man").
- **Slang, dialect, euphemism;** regional dictionaries (the appendix lists them per region).
- **Metaphors:** how people picture it.
- **What kind of thing it is:** a quality of persons, of acts, of groups or of places; a lasting trait or a passing state (Spanish *ser* and *estar*).
- **How grammar marks agency and blame:** "I broke it" against "it broke" (*se me cayó*); studies of agentive language and blame (Fausey and Boroditsky, 2011).
- **Politeness grammar:** formal and informal "you" (*tú* and *usted*); honorifics.
- **Loans from trade and contact languages** into the regional language.
- **Cultural scripts:** what a speaker assumes when using the word.
- **"I" against "we":** pronoun counts in books and song lyrics (Twenge et al., 2012; DeWall et al., 2011).
- **Which speech is heard as confident, rude or weak** (perceptual dialectology, Preston); attitudes to the languages themselves (official languages, English-only campaigns, mixed speech such as Spanglish).
- **Frequency and change:** corpora with a regional field, search data (Google Trends figures are relative, comparable only within one request).
- **Names:** places, people, businesses, teams, streets.

### B2. Ideas, beliefs and moral standing
- **Standing:** virtue, vice, neutral, or not a category at all.
- **Rank:** when it conflicts with other values, which wins?
- **Conditions and limits:** when it is right, when it goes too far.
- **Agency and attribution:** is an outcome put down to character, effort, luck, fate, God or "the system"? (Locus of control; Morris and Peng, 1994; *si Dios quiere*.)
- **Kinds of worth people appeal to** (Lamont; Boltanski and Thévenot, *On Justification*).
- **Culture as a toolkit** (Swidler): record which understanding people use in which situation, not one single view.
- **Risk worldviews** (Douglas and Wildavsky; Kahan's cultural cognition).
- **Folk theory:** where people think the concept comes from.
- **Religious teaching:** scriptures, doctrines, moral teaching (sermons live in B14).
- **Philosophy and political thought:** the unit's own thinkers and traditions; the nations' philosophies as they state them.
- **The national classics as a baseline, not the unit's reading.** A country's classic accounts of itself usually describe its core (Paz's *El laberinto de la soledad* describes central Mexico, not the north), so they serve as the source culture or the national baseline.
- **Ideologies and movements** that claim the concept.
- **The ideal against the real.**

### B3. Feeling and the body
- **Emotions** that go with it; how it feels from inside (often thin in published sources).
- **Ideal affect:** whether excited or calm states are the ideal (Tsai).
- **Display:** face, voice, posture, gesture, silence; who may show it, where (display rules; Matsumoto's data are country-level).
- **Pain and endurance norms** (Zborowski, 1952, and later work).
- **The body:** training, ideals, toughness, grooming, fitness, injury.
- **Ideals of manhood and womanhood** as they bear on the concept ("precarious manhood", Vandello and Bosson, 2008; *machismo* against *caballerismo*, Arciniega et al., 2008; the masculinities of particular trades). The author writes for men, so this gets its own line.
- **Medicine and health:** patient autonomy and who decides; vaccine exemptions; folk medicine and healers (*curanderismo*); cultural concepts of distress (*susto*, *ataque de nervios*; DSM-5's glossary); public reports such as Oregon's annual Death with Dignity data, whose most common end-of-life concern is "losing autonomy".
- **Reading it in others.**

### B4. The senses
- **Sound and silence:** soundscape and noise (Schafer's World Soundscape Project, begun at Simon Fraser University, *The Vancouver Soundscape*, 1973); silence as meaning (Basso, "To Give Up on Words", 1970); noise complaints.
- **Smell, taste, touch** (Howes, *Sensual Relations*; Classen, *Worlds of Sense*).
- **Food and drink as ways of acting the concept out:** eating chili as bravado (Rozin and Schiller, 1980); drinking cultures; first foods; comfort foods; what is eaten to celebrate, mourn or prove oneself.

### B5. Everyday practice
- **Home and family life; friendship and neighbors; courtship and marriage.**
- **Work:** norms, careers, self-employment, the folklore of occupations, dangerous work.
- **Commerce:** bargaining, customer service, advertising, brands.
- **Driving** as a stage for the concept: road rage, speeding, seatbelts.
- **Recreation:** sport, hunting, fishing, the outdoors, hobbies, games, gambling.
- **Online life** (online expression lives in B14; online measures in B17).
- **Religion in practice; civic life; personal finance.**
- **Etiquette:** greetings, forms of address, requests, refusals, apologies, gifts, hospitality.

### B6. Space, place and things
- **Personal space** (Hall, *The Hidden Dimension*).
- **Settlement:** lot sizes, fences, cul-de-sacs, gated communities, density, car dependence.
- **The house:** plans, a bedroom per child, porches, kitchens; vernacular house types (Glassie).
- **Territory:** trespass and "no trespassing", open range and fences, public lands, beach access.
- **Sense of place** (Tuan, *Topophilia*; Basso, *Wisdom Sits in Places*); the ordinary landscape read as evidence (J. B. Jackson; Meinig, ed., *The Interpretation of Ordinary Landscapes*).
- **Which languages appear on public signs** (Landry and Bourhis, 1997).
- **Objects that carry the concept:** the pickup, the gun, the knife, the canoe, tools, heirlooms (Miller, *Stuff*); what museums and historical societies choose to keep.
- **Repair and making:** do-it-yourself and maker culture; the right-to-repair movement.
- **Technology and its ideology:** the local tech industry's beliefs (Turner, *From Counterculture to Cyberculture*; Barbrook and Cameron, "The Californian Ideology").
- **Car cultures:** hot rods, lowriders, pickups.

### B7. Time and rhythm
- **Past, present or future orientation;** the classic model is Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck's *Variations in Value Orientations* (1961), which compared five neighboring cultures in one New Mexico valley.
- **One thing at a time against many at once** (Hall, *The Dance of Life*); punctuality; waiting and patience.
- **Time use:** alone, with kin, at work, in worship, volunteering (time-use surveys).
- **The seasonal round:** salmon runs, hunting seasons, harvests, the school year, fiesta calendars.

### B8. Life course and transmission
- **Child-rearing:** styles, the values parents want, praise and punishment, stories, games, toys, chores.
- **Children's own culture,** not only adults' view of it: dares, playground lore, fights, teasing (Bronner, *American Children's Folklore*; Knapp and Knapp, *One Potato, Two Potato*).
- **How children learn:** by instruction, or by watching and pitching in (Rogoff); teasing as moral training (Briggs, *Inuit Morality Play*).
- **Schooling:** curricula, textbooks, school rituals, sports, discipline, how history is taught.
- **Youth subcultures.**
- **Coming of age:** the milestones themselves (rites of passage as ceremony live in B9).
- **Adulthood; old age and elders.**
- **Total institutions as stages of life:** boot camp, boarding and residential school, the nursing home (Goffman, *Asylums*).
- **The end of life:** eulogies, obituaries, epitaphs; what a community chooses to praise.

### B9. Ritual, festival and commemoration
- **Holidays, festivals, parades, fairs, rodeos, pageants.**
- **Ceremonies,** religious and civic; Indigenous ceremonies only as the nations share them.
- **Rites of passage** as ceremony.
- **Dance:** square and contra dance, *folklórico*, *banda*, the powwow as a public intertribal event.
- **Public journeys:** pilgrimages, canoe journeys, processions.
- **Vows and their keeping:** vows to saints (*mandas*); roadside memorials (*descansos*).
- **Monuments and memorials:** what is remembered, and by whom it was put up.

### B10. Institutions and formal rules
- **Government:** programs, services, official rhetoric.
- **Schools and universities; religious bodies.**
- **Workplaces and professions:** codes of ethics, licensing, HR rules, mission statements, job ads.
- **Unions, guilds, associations, clubs, fraternal orders; co-operatives.**
- **Corporations and markets; media institutions; sports bodies.**
- **Military and emergency services.**
- **Honors systems:** medals, orders, halls of fame (who receives them lives in B15).
- **Tribal and First Nations governments** and their own institutions.
- **Informal institutions:** gangs, militias, mutual aid, communes, online communities.

### B11. The economy of the concept
- **Industries built on it:** the outdoor industry, self-help, security, insurance, wellness.
- **Insurance:** risk handed off or carried oneself.
- **How people fund needs:** savings, credit, crowdfunding, mutual aid, the state.
- **What the unit's jobs are:** its occupational mix and labor history.
- **What it sells about itself:** the concept in marketing and exports.

### B12. Rewards and sanctions
- **Reward:** praise, status, titles, money, fame, marriage prospects, leadership.
- **Breach:** gossip, ridicule, shame, guilt, ostracism, losing a job, formal punishment (violence as a sanction lives in B13).
- **Who enforces:** family, peers, elders, clergy, employers, the market, the state, online crowds.
- **Redemption:** apology, penance, restitution, starting over.

### B13. Conflict, violence and crisis
- **Fighting norms:** the "fair fight", school fights, feuds and duels in history, self-defense, martial arts.
- **Collective violence:** riots, uprisings, vigilantism.
- **War and the warrior:** warrior traditions as the nations describe them, enlistment, pacifism, draft resistance (Americans who went to British Columbia in the Vietnam era).
- **Crises as stress tests:** disasters (disaster research: Erikson, *Everything in Its Path*; Klinenberg, *Heat Wave*; Solnit, *A Paradise Built in Hell*, as synthesis); wildfire (stay and defend, or leave); earthquake preparedness; **the COVID-19 pandemic,** which struck every nation at once: masks, movement, compliance, vaccination (Bazzi, Fiszbein and Gebresilasse, 2021, link frontier individualism to COVID behavior).

### B14. Expressive culture
- **Sayings, proverbs, idioms** (proverb dictionaries per region).
- **Humor** and its targets; **insults** (described, quoted only briefly).
- **Folklore:** legends, tall tales, urban legends; folklore archives.
- **Myths and sacred stories,** as their owners share them; owned and restricted knowledge is not retold.
- **Literature, poetry, song** (genres, lyrics, radio formats, anthems, hymns, protest songs).
- **Film, television, theater, games, comics, children's books.**
- **Visual art, crafts, carving, murals.**
- **Mottos, flags, seals, slogans, bumper stickers, graffiti, tattoos.**
- **Sermons and speeches; letters to the editor; voter pamphlets** where citizens write arguments for and against measures.
- **Family photographs and selfies; social media and memes.**

### B15. Exemplars and counter-exemplars
- **Heroes, role models, saints, founders, rescuers, rebels, inventors.**
- **Villains, cautionary figures, fools.**
- **Archetypes** (the pioneer, the cowboy, the *vaquero*, the trickster).
- **Fictional characters.**
- **Contested figures,** heroes to some and villains to others.
- **Exemplars that can be counted:** award lists with places (civilian bravery awards, military decorations, heritage fellowships), polls of the "greatest" person, notable-people databases by birthplace (Laouenan et al., 2022; Schich et al., 2014), whom streets and schools are named after.
- **How the exemplars changed.**

### B16. History and memory
- **The founders' understanding** and each later layer's contribution.
- **Formative events** (cite Stage 1, profile 13; add what is specific to the concept).
- **Collective memory:** origin myths, what is celebrated, what is forgotten; memory fights.
- **History of emotions** (Stearns and Stearns, *Anger*; Rosenwein's "emotional communities").
- **Personal documents:** diaries, letters, wills, yearbooks; pioneer diaries; the 1930s Federal Writers' Project life histories and state guides.
- **Trauma and its legacy,** told in the affected peoples' own voices first.
- **Migration:** who came, who left (the unit's emigrants and diaspora; hometown associations); selective migration (Bishop, *The Big Sort*), which can make a region's values look inherited when they were chosen by movers.

### B17. Measured evidence
At the finest level available, each with year, sample and level. The appendix for each region lists its datasets; Part F lists the cautions.
- **Values and attitudes surveys,** national and regional.
- **Cultural-psychology measures:** individualism and collectivism, tightness and looseness, personality regions, frontier individualism, honor culture, display rules, self-construal.
- **Behavior in administrative data:** self-employment, moving, marriage and divorce, giving, volunteering, homicide by circumstance, suicide, crashes, church attendance, union membership.
- **Experiments and standard tasks by region:** field experiments (lost wallets, helping), economic games; few exist below the country.
- **Published computational studies of text:** word embeddings over time (Kozlowski, Taddy and Evans, 2019); county-level social-media language (Eichstaedt et al., 2015).
- **Systematic observation:** published studies that rate streetscapes or neighborhoods (Sampson and Raudenbush, 1999; street-view studies).
- **Naming data** (Varnum and Kitayama, 2011).
- **What people consume:** library checkouts, book and music sales, streaming charts, search.
- **The author's measures** where they bear on the concept.
- **Natural experiments:** children of immigrants, whose behavior separates culture from institutions (Fernández and Fogli, 2009); matched communities across a seam; frontiers settled by choice (Kitayama et al., 2006); regional selves in national samples (Plaut, Markus and Lachman, 2002).

### B18. Who varies, who dissents
- **Holders:** which groups hold which understanding: region and seam, urban and rural, class and education, occupation, generation, gender, ethnicity, immigrants and their children, religion, politics, veterans, parents and non-parents, disability, newcomers from other units, subcultures, elites and ordinary people, each Indigenous nation as its own voice.
- **The live debates** over the concept.
- (Whom the concept is extended to lives in B21; how it is taught, in B8.)

### B19. Margins and extremes
Where the culture's tolerance ends, seen from its edges.
- **Outlaws and social bandits** (Hobsbawm, *Bandits*); street codes (Anderson, *Code of the Street*).
- **Extremes of the concept:** movements and episodes that push it to its limit (armed occupations, autonomous zones, sabotage campaigns, secession movements, narco culture and its saints).
- **What the culture treats as illness and what as wrongdoing** (Conrad and Schneider, *Deviance and Medicalization*).

### B20. Relations, contrasts and self-image
- **Links to other concepts;** trade-offs and tensions; contradictions the culture lives with.
- **Self against other:** how the unit defines itself against its neighbors.
- **Self-image and self-marketing:** tourism and license-plate slogans, official state and provincial symbols, booster pitches, staged authenticity (MacCannell, *The Tourist*); governors' and premiers' addresses and Mexican governors' *informes*; citizenship study guides and tests; brands the unit exports.
- **How outsiders see it:** stereotypes from outside, recorded as outsiders' claims, never adopted; classic outsider accounts read critically.
- **Against the national baseline:** what is national and what is the unit's own.
- **Across an international border inside one region** (the classic case: Canada against the U.S., Lipset, *Continental Divide*; Adams, *Fire and Ice*).
- **Against the source cultures** abroad, and what changed in the move.
- (The seams themselves live in the cross-unit comparison file, Part G.)

### B21. Who and what the concept applies to
- **Insiders and outsiders;** men, women, children, elders (who is entitled to it).
- **Groups as subjects of the concept:** tribal sovereignty, home rule, secession.
- **Land, climate and the nonhuman world:** the wilderness ideal (Nash; Cronon, "The Trouble with Wilderness"; Stegner's "Wilderness Letter"); nature religion (Albanese); animals as symbols, partners and tests (hunting ethics, rodeo and *charrería*, pets, predators); how resources are shared (water, fishing); climate and threat theories of culture, recorded as hypotheses (Van de Vliert; Fincher and Thornhill).
- **Spirits, ancestors and the dead.**
- **Corporations, institutions, the state.**
- **Machines, robots and AI:** present-day attitudes only (era E0): robotaxis, automation, the right to repair, the unit's tech industry and its fiction about AI.

### B22. Silences, taboos and the unmarked default
- **What can't be said** (often thin in published sources).
- **Taboo topics and forbidden displays.**
- **The unmarked default:** what is so assumed it is never named.
- **What communities try to keep from children** (challenges to library and school books).
- **What is conspicuously missing** compared with neighbors or source cultures. A silence is a finding and needs evidence, such as that comparison; it is not the same as a research null.

### B23. Change and direction
- **What is shifting now,** and since when; the drivers (technology, economy, migration, climate, politics, generations); movements and backlashes; measured trends.
- Research stops at the present (era E0): no projection; that is Stage 6, the author's.

### B24. Synthesis for the concept
- **Reading:** one paragraph, then one line.
- **Coded fields,** so that 80 nations can be compared: standing (virtue, vice, neutral, not a category); rank against family and against the group; who is entitled to it (by gender, age); seen as a mean (yes or no); direction of change; confidence (high, medium, low).
- **Where sources disagree,** recorded, not resolved. **Canon check.** **Questions for the author.**

## Part C: the meta-category as a whole, per unit

**C1. The domain question** (table below), answered for the unit, with at least a paragraph on each traditional area the concepts don't already cover.
**C2. The sign's folkways** (Fischer's 24, by `Shared/Folkways_Crosswalk.md`), each through the families that fit it. Fischer built the folkways for four British settler cultures: for Indigenous and Mexican-founded units, each folkway is read through the culture's own categories, and Indigenous practice goes under religious ways or wherever each nation places it, never under "magic ways".
**C3. The culture's own structure:** how it groups and ranks this domain's concepts.
**C4. What the list misses:** concepts this culture treats as central to the domain that aren't on the list, flagged for the author.

## Part E: approaches and source types

| Approach | Examples | Usual tag |
|---|---|---|
| Ethnography and community studies | published fieldwork; matched-community studies | scholarly |
| History and conceptual history | monographs, regional histories, archives, old newspapers; keywords; history of emotions | scholarly, classic |
| Personal documents | diaries, letters, wills, yearbooks, life histories | artifact |
| The peoples' own voices | Indigenous nations' publications, newspapers, dictionaries, cultural centers, oral histories; community histories; memoirs | official (a nation's government), artifact, practitioner |
| Linguistics | dictionaries, corpora, dialect atlases, cultural scripts, metaphor and pronoun studies | scholarly, measured |
| Religion and philosophy | scriptures, sermons, catechisms, the unit's thinkers | classic, artifact, official |
| Literature, the arts, media | novels, songs, films, art, ads, memes, read as evidence of values | artifact (an agent's own reading of them is [synthesis: this file]; a published content analysis is scholarly) |

## Part F: rules and cautions
**The standing rules** (as in the RUNBOOK):
- Every family is asked; honest nulls, not padding. Founders first; variation required; disagreements recorded, not resolved.
- No national stereotypes: a people is never one voice.
- Native, Indigenous or Aboriginal, never "Indian", in our own words.
- No South Asian works as sources; name-only cases go to `Shared/held_for_review_Sources.md`. **No Subcontinentals,** with the cases known in advance: in the pilot, British Columbia's South Asian communities and their history (including the *Komagata Maru*, 1914) and Oregon's Rajneeshpuram commune are each given one exclusion line, flagged, and researched no further.
- Short quotations only; research only, up to the present (era E0); the signs are research classifiers.

**Indigenous research:**
- Owned and restricted knowledge is not retold; the nations' own voices come first.
- **Data governance:** First Nations data under OCAP (ownership, control, access, possession) and Indigenous data generally under the CARE principles. A survey such as the First Nations Regional Health Survey is used only as its governing body allows.
- **Old ethnography** (Boas, Curtis and their generation) is read critically, as outsiders' records of their time, checked against the nations' own accounts.

**Measurement cautions:**
- **Self-report and the reference-group effect** (Heine et al., 2002): people rate themselves against those around them, so cross-group comparisons of rating scales mislead. Weigh behavior beside self-report.
- **The ecological fallacy:** a county value describes a county, not its people.
- **From counties to a unit:** a unit's value is the population-weighted average over its counties in `Data/Unit_Territories.md`, unless stated otherwise.
- **Selective migration** confounds regional and frontier effects (B16).
- **Google Trends** figures are relative and comparable only within one request.

**Circularity.** Woodard, Fischer, Garreau and the other framework authors define the units, so their portraits of a unit's values can't be its main evidence. Every reading they suggest needs independent corroboration.

**The *artifact* tag** (proposed; the author decides): a primary cultural object (a song, an ad, a sermon, a diary, a monument) whose question is how representative it is, not whether its claims are true. It would join the tags in `Shared/Sources_and_Datasets.md`.

**Known thin spots** (flag "null expected" in A3 unless the unit shows otherwise): how a concept feels from inside; display rules below the country; regional experiments (few below the country; old ones, such as Levine's pace-of-life cities); homeschooling counts (state reporting varies); what can't be said.

## Part G: running it (proposed; the author decides)
**The scale.** At full depth, this sheet is very wide. One concept across Cascadia's eleven units is about 250 family-by-unit cells; at one or two searches a cell, that is roughly 3,000 searches per sign for one target, about three sessions' worth. Two things keep it in bounds:
1. **The source pack** (A0), built once per unit and reused for every concept.
2. **The depth flags** (A3): full, brief or null expected, set per family per concept before the agents start.

**Job size, three options:**
1. **One concept, all of a target's units** (recommended): one job per concept, plus one for the domain question and folkways. Each agent writes one file per unit and a short comparison across the seams that feeds Stage 5. This changes the RUNBOOK's "one job = one unit × one stage" and the T4 template.
2. **One unit, one concept:** the deepest; about 120 jobs per sign for Cascadia.
3. **One unit, one sign** (the current rule): too wide for this sheet.

With option 1, each unit's sign folder becomes `Units/<unit>/04 - Signs/NN - <Sign>/`, with `00 - Domain and folkways.md` and one file per concept, plus `Targets/<target>/04 - Seams/NN - <Sign>/<concept>.md` for the comparisons.
