# Plot Constraint Mapping — Method

**Status: proposed.** Drafted 2026-11-26 alongside one of the author's works in progress; generalized to every linear medium and moved here with the author, 2026-11-27. The author's rulings in it are dated and bind; the rest stays proposed until the author adopts it.

**What this is:** a method for writing, for any plot architecture, the **conditions** each beat must meet and the ones it must not, before (or instead of) deciding what happens in it. It works on any story unit in any linear medium, written, audio or visual:
- **Written:** a stand-alone novel, a novel series, or one book of a series.
- **Audio:** an audio series, or one of its episodes.
- **Linear visual media:** a feature film, a short film, a TV series, one season of it or one episode, a miniseries, a web series.
- **In any of these:** one character's arc.

**Not for video games** (author, 2026-11-27). Video game plot design is a completely separate architecture with its own structural process, and it gets its own dedicated guide files, made in a separate job. Don't apply this method to a game's plot, questlines or quests.

**Where it comes from.** The method was distilled from the author's own works in progress (a stand-alone novel and a novel series) and has since been applied to a third (an audio series). Those works are unpublished, so they are not named here, and **every example in this file is made up** (author, 2026-11-27). Keep it that way: when adding an example, invent one; never draw it from the author's real works.

---

## The one-sentence rule

**A constraint says what must or must not be true of a beat; it never says what happens in it.** Whatever event eventually fills the slot is then guaranteed to fit the structure, the character, the work's laws, and its continuity.

## Why work this way

- **It keeps the events open.** The author can find the best event later without the structure having been decided by accident.
- **It's checkable.** Any proposed event can be tested against the list in minutes.
- **It catches structural faults early**: a Doorway nobody chooses, a rescue by coincidence, a beat placed in two units.
- **It separates what's binding from what's suggested**, so suggestions never harden into canon unnoticed.

## The tests

- **The event test:** if a constraint names a specific event, character action or outcome that hasn't been decided, it isn't a constraint yet. Rewrite it as the condition that event was meeting.
- **The pasting test** (from the Enneagram design law): if a character-level constraint could be pasted onto any other character and still read as true, it belongs in the shared file, not the character's.
- **The trace test:** every constraint can name where it comes from (see Tags).

## Inputs

Gather these before writing a single constraint:
1. **The beat framework:** the composite master (`../Story_Structure/story-structure-definitions.md`) and the per-source extractions (Snyder, Truby, Harmon, Bell, Weiland, Campbell, Fox) in `../Story_Structure/`.
2. **The author's laws:** Universal Design Laws (Tepenian Universe Timeline repo) and any work-level rulings (for example, "only the Catalyst may be a coincidence").
3. **The protagonist's data:** for a character, the Stage 1 derived sheet (Enneagram type, defense, flaw, greatest fear, goal, ghost) from the Character Development Methodology. For an idea, an investigation of the idea (see Step 1).
4. **The work's givens:** premise, theme, antagonist(s), arc stage (KWML or Weiland), setting and era, legal status of the characters (for example, whether a character is free to leave, or bound by law or contract).
5. **Established content:** whatever the outline already contains, so continuity limits can be drawn.
6. **The work's format and delivery:**
   - its **measure**: pages, minutes of runtime, episodes, or chapters;
   - its **length budget**: page count, runtime, or episode count and length;
   - its **delivery breaks**: volume splits, season breaks, episode endings, ad-break act breaks, or release drops.
7. **The medium's strengths and limits:** what the medium can and can't carry. A novel can show inner thought; film and TV show images, but carry little inner thought without voice-over; audio is heard and never seen; a short film has room for very few beats. This is where [Medium] constraints come from.
8. **The original, if the work is an adaptation:** what the original establishes, and how close the author wants to stay to it. This is where [Source] constraints come from.

**Not an input: the audience guides** (author, 2026-11-27). The "writing stories for men" guides and the notes on traditional masculine values, principles and virtues come in only after the characters' arcs, the world, and a full plot-architecture outline are established. Then whatever from them fits most naturally into the story already built is what gets incorporated into the plot. They never shape the constraints up front. *(This is Step 12, the audience pass.)*

## Step 0 — Map the layers

1. **Name every layer** the work has: series, season, book, film, episode, part, volume, character arc, subplot. For each, decide whether it is a **story unit** (has its own full beat structure) or a **delivery division** (a split for printing, broadcast or release, with no beats of its own).
   - **A delivery division may carry a small requirement of its own** where that makes sense in context, without becoming a beat: an episode or a volume ending on a hook, a TV act ending before an ad break.
   - **In TV and audio series, decide how the episodes work:** each a full story unit (episodic), each carrying only part of a larger unit's beats (serialized), or both, which is common.
   - *Example:* a trilogy whose books are each printed in two volumes. The trilogy and each book are story units; the volumes are a delivery division, and each book's Midpoint falls shortly before its volume split.
2. **Declare each layer's constraints separately; satisfy them together where beats meet** (author, 2026-11-27). A series beat's constraints come from the series; a book or episode beat's from its own unit. Each set is declared on its own, almost as if the other didn't exist, and neither is written in terms of the other. Where two layers' beats land on the same point, exactly or reasonably close, note it ("same point, two layers"): the one event that fills that point must satisfy **both** sets. See writing rule 6.
3. **Write a positions table** for each story unit: the beat, its percentage, its position in the unit's own measure (page, minute or episode), and the delivery division it falls in.
4. **Treat positions as approximate.** Reasonably close is good enough; don't chase exact position math.

## Step 1 — Name the protagonist and fix the frame

1. **Name who makes the choices at each layer.** The Doorways and the post-Midpoint turn need a character who chooses. If the protagonist is an idea, name the characters who carry it, and record the laws that govern who they can be (for example, "the main character is always a robot").
2. **Fix the frame** (Truby step 1): self-revelation, psychological need, moral need, desire, initial error. Whatever isn't decided yet, **record as deliberately deferred**, and never write a constraint that forces it.
3. **If the protagonist is an idea, investigate the idea before constraining it.** Survey what the work already says, what its universe already defines, and the real-world thinking on it. Expect **more than one conception** to be in play; constraints built on one conception apply only where that conception is being dramatized. (*Example:* a series whose protagonist is the idea of justice first surveys what justice means in the work, in its world, and in real-world thought, and finds that retribution and fairness are both in play.)
4. **State the core structural difficulty up front** when the protagonist has one. *Example:* a lead whose characteristic failure is avoiding choice will find both Doorways, which are commitment beats, the hardest beats in the work.

## Step 2 — Choose the scope

Start with the **load-bearing beats**: the Doorway to Act 2, the Midpoint, and the Doorway to Act 3. Add the rest later. A small, finished set is worth more than a large, thin one.

**If a unit is too short for the full beat sheet** (a short film or a short episode may be), first decide which beats it has. That is the author's call, recorded with the work. *(The composite has no beat set for short forms yet; see Open items.)*

## Step 3 — Gather each beat's definitions

For each beat in scope:
1. **Read every framework's version of it**, not only the composite's.
2. **Note where the composite differs from a source.** The composite wins; the difference is recorded, not silently resolved.
3. **Note each source's placement.** Sources often disagree by a few percent; the composite's percentages are the working ones.

## Step 4 — Write the shared constraints once

Write the constraints that hold for **every unit** at that layer (every book, every episode, every character of a kind) in one shared file. Each unit's file then holds only what it adds. This prevents multiple copies drifting apart. **Say so at the top of every unit file,** because a beat that shows one added line there still carries every shared constraint as well.

## Step 5 — Specialize to each unit

Derive each unit's own constraints from:
- **Character data:** type, defense, flaw, fear, goal, ghost. *Example:* "He cannot talk his way out of the decision"; "His fear cannot be faced and overcome here; that belongs later."
- **The unit's theme, antagonist and arc stage.** *Example:* in a series where each book has its own antagonist, each book's Midpoint turns on that book's antagonist.
- **Setting, era and legal status.** *Example:* in an era when servants are bound by contract, a servant's door closes through the contract, not by simply quitting.
- **The author's laws.**
- **The medium** (Input 7): what this unit can't rely on, given what its medium can and can't carry. Tagged [Medium]. *Example:* in an audio drama nothing is seen, so no turn can rest on a silent visual reveal.
- **The original, if the work is an adaptation** (Input 8): what fidelity to it requires. Tagged [Source].

## Step 6 — Draw the continuity limits

For each unit, list what it **cannot depend on** (things the work hasn't established yet) and what it **cannot resolve** (payoffs that belong later). Keep these separate from structural constraints; they come from the work's timeline, not from a framework.
- *Example:* in a five-book series where a stolen crown is recovered in Book 5, nothing in Book 1 can return the crown; and nothing before the heir learns of her claim can rely on her knowing it.

## Step 7 — Handle the Midpoint in two parts

The Midpoint is genuinely open: false victory or false defeat; the three Mirror Moment questions or one of four kinds of "death." So:
1. **Constraints that hold whatever shape is chosen.**
2. **A menu of candidate shapes**, each with the **additional requirement** it would bring. The menu offers options; it does not recommend.

## Step 8 — Add the archetype layer

**Required for every foreground robot character** (the composite's standing rule: the full composite, Weiland included, always); **optional for everyone else**, for whom a light treatment is enough (author, 2026-11-27).

1. **Check the length budget.** A character has one character arc, but may pass through more than one *archetype* arc. Decide the number of archetype arcs per character explicitly for each work, from its length budget. The one reference point set so far: a novel of about 300 pages supports one archetype arc per foreground character, with no transition from one archetype arc to the next, no shadow detour, and the archetype arc's Beginning state on page one. *(Budgets for other media: see Open items.)*
2. **The archetype is a lens on the same beats, never a second beat sheet.**
3. **Assign the arc with reasons**, and say why the runner-up lost.
4. **Adapt where the default flavor would break the character**, keeping the structural function and flipping the polarity (*Example:* for a character who withdraws under pressure, the Hero Arc's grandiosity becomes withdrawal).
5. **Add the arc's own MUST / CANNOT.**

## Step 9 — Check the existing outline against the constraints

In a **separate analysis file**, not in the constraint files, mark each beat: ✓ met · ◐ partly met or not yet built · ✗ conflicts · ? a question for the author. Record every conflict found.

## Step 10 — Turn candidates into conditions

Good candidate events will turn up during the check. **Keep the candidates in the analysis file.** In the constraint files, write only the **conditions** a candidate was meeting, tagged [Proposed], or phrase them conditionally ("If an assassination fills this slot, it costs more than the last one"). Strike them if the candidate is dropped.

## Step 11 — Record open threads and questions

End every constraint file with **Open Threads**: unresolved clashes (for example, one event proposed for two slots), deferred decisions, and anything waiting on other work. Questions only the author can answer go here, worded so they can be answered without context.

## Step 12 — The audience pass

**Only once a full plot-architecture outline stands** (author, 2026-11-27), never before:
1. **Examine the full story and the full character arcs,** profiles and backgrounds.
2. **Then look over the audience material:** the "writing stories for men" guides, and the notes on traditional masculine values, virtues and principles.
3. **Find which one, or which ones, fit most naturally** within the context of that story and those characters. List them, with the reason each fits, in the analysis file, as with the candidates in Step 10.
4. **What is incorporated must still pass every existing constraint.** It never overrides one, and never adds a constraint that reshapes a character, an arc or the structure. What the author adopts becomes established content, tagged [Adopted].

## Step 13 — Author review

Everything below [Law] and [Adopted] is proposed. **A constraint that turns out to be wrong for the work is struck, not worked around.** The author's rulings during review are recorded with their date, and they bind from then on.

## Writing rules

0. **Every beat carries multiple requirements and multiple prohibitions** (author, 2026-11-26). Expect several MUSTs and several CANNOTs per beat, drawn from every source that bears on it: the frameworks, the character, the theme, the laws, continuity, the medium, and the original if the work is an adaptation. A beat with only one of either is almost always under-specified. Go back through Steps 3–6 before accepting it.
1. **One condition per numbered item**, with a **bold lead sentence** that states it; any explanation follows in plain text. (This means each line holds one condition, not that a beat holds one line.)
2. **Testable.** Someone holding a draft of the scene, in whatever form (prose, script page, episode outline), can say yes or no.
3. **No events.** See the event test.
4. **Every item tagged** with its source and weight.
5. **A CANNOT is not just a MUST negated.** Write a CANNOT only when it rules out a specific, tempting failure (for example "The choice cannot be made for him by someone else's order," "A third party's act standing as the crossing").
6. **Declared separately, satisfied together** (author, 2026-11-27). Each layer's beat has its own constraints and requirements, declared independently of every other layer's, almost as if unrelated. A constraint never imports another layer's beat into its own list. But where a local beat and a larger-scale beat land on the same point, exactly or reasonably close, the event that fills that point must satisfy **both** sets of parameters. That event is a character-driven one: it comes from a character's decision that is internally consistent and in line with her or his own profile.
   - *Example:* in a five-book series, an event in Book 2 lines up with a beat of the series-wide plot. Book 2's beat and the series beat each keep their own declared constraints. The event chosen for that point meets Book 2's and the series' constraints alike.
7. **No forced decisions.** Nothing may require the author to settle something they've deferred.
8. **Open each file with "Working from (established)":** the givens it builds on, so the reader knows what's already decided.
9. **Write against roles, not names, where names may change.** If a faction's, place's or character's name is a placeholder or likely to change, the constraint binds the role ("the ruling council," not the placeholder name it has for now), so renaming it changes no constraint.

## Tags

| Tag | Weight |
|---|---|
| [Law] | Binding: the author's own rule (the Universal Design Laws, or a ruling recorded in a repo) |
| [Adopted] | Binding: a structural decision the author adopted |
| [Composite] | The author's composite structure (Derivation Mechanisms, `Stories/Story_Structure/story-structure-definitions.md`) |
| [Snyder] / [Truby n] / [Harmon] / [Bell] / [Weiland] / [Fox] / [CDM] / [RU] | Guidance from a source framework; not binding until adopted (CDM = Character Development Methodology; RU = Robot Universals). [CDM] and [RU] apply in every work, since all of the author's stories share one universe (author, 2026-11-27) |
| [Medium] | Follows from what the medium can and can't carry |
| [Source] | Follows from the original the work adapts |
| [Continuity] | Follows from what the work has or hasn't established |
| [Proposed] | Follows from an undecided suggestion; strike if the suggestion goes |

## File layout

The set lives in the work's **`structure/constraints/`** folder, beside `character arcs/` and `main story/`, as the Stories template lays out a work. (A repo laid out differently may use its own path, such as `plot/constraints/`.)

```
constraints/
├── README.md          ← what the set is, the tags, the rules every file follows, the positions table
├── 00-<layer>.md      ← one per higher layer (e.g. 00-series.md)
├── 00-every-<unit>.md ← the shared constraints for every unit at a layer (e.g. 00-every-episode.md)
├── characters.md      ← optional: character-arc constraints that hold across units (or one file per character)
└── <unit>.md          ← one per unit: only what it adds, by delivery division where there are any
```
Keep the **analysis** (reasoning, status checks, candidates) in separate files beside the constraint set, and link to them from the README.

## Common faults this method catches

Each was found in at least one of the three worked cases, except those marked *(anticipated)*, which follow from the general method and haven't yet turned up in a work.

| Fault | What it looks like | The constraint that catches it |
|---|---|---|
| **A Doorway nobody chooses** | A third party's act (a message sent, a killing committed) written as the crossing | "The protagonist chooses to cross"; "A third party's act standing in for the choice" (CANNOT) |
| **A Break into Three with no answer** | The opponent's attack written as the beat, with no solution from the protagonist | "An opponent's move with no protagonist's answer attached" (CANNOT) |
| **A rescue by coincidence** | A lucky find that gets the protagonist out of trouble | The one-coincidence law; "a coincidence may create trouble, never get the lead out" |
| **One event in two places** | The same founding, war or banishment written into two different beats or units | "One event placed at two points" (CANNOT); Open Threads |
| **Contradictory accounts** | Two files disagree on who did something | "One consistent account across files" (MUST) |
| **A carrier who can't last** | A B-Story or long thread carried by someone whose lifespan or timeline can't span it | "Its carrier is alive and present from its start to its convergence" |
| **No protagonist at a choice beat** | Turns happen to an idea or a faction | "Each unit has a protagonist whose choices the Doorways are" |
| **Layer mixing** | One layer's constraints written into another layer's list, or an event at a shared point checked against only one layer's set | Declared separately, satisfied together (Step 0.2, writing rule 6) |
| **Position-exact math** | Treating a 100-page gap, or a few-minute one, as a defect | Approximate positions (Step 0) |
| **A delivery break in the wrong place** | A volume, episode or season break that cuts a unit before a beat that has to fall inside it (for example, a volume ending before its book's Midpoint) | The delivery-division rule (Step 0.1); the beat's position constraint |
| **Forcing a deferred decision** | A constraint that only works if the author picks up or down now | "No forced decisions" (writing rule 7) |
| **One conception imposed** | Constraints that assume an idea has a single definition when the work holds several | Step 1.3 |
| **Two lowest points** | A local low point written heavier than the work's single "end" | Truby's "only one" rule at All Is Lost |
| **Relying on what the medium can't carry** *(anticipated)* | A turn that hangs on a character's unspoken thought in a film, or on a silent visual reveal in an audio series | [Medium] constraints (Input 7, Step 5) |
| **Breaking faith with the original** *(anticipated)* | In an adaptation, a beat that contradicts something the original establishes | [Source] constraints (Input 8, Step 5) |
| **The audience guides shaping the story** | A character, arc or constraint built from the audience guides before the story stands | Step 12, the audience pass (author, 2026-11-27) |
