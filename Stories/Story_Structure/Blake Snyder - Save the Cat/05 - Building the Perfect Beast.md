# Chapter 5: Building the Perfect Beast

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast." The local text has no page numbers; cited by chapter title, shortened below to (Snyder, ch. 5). The text's two Board diagrams and its sample index cards did not survive conversion; they are described here from the prose.

## 1. The guru and the portable Board
Snyder says lucky writers find a guru: someone wiser who offers to share. His was **Mike Cheda**, a development executive (at Barry & Enright, Disney, HBO and others) and a credited writer. Snyder credits him with solving the story of *Next of Kin*, a spec bought for about a million dollars that needed fixing before it could be made. Snyder says Cheda showed him the spot on a Century City sidewalk where the fix came to him: "Cowboys and Indians!" That framing guided the rewrite (Snyder, ch. 5).

When they met again recently, Cheda opened an artist's sketchbook across both pages. Three lines divided it into four horizontal rows, and paper squares, one per story beat, sat in the rows. He called it portable. Snyder recognized it at once as **The Board** (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 2. The Board: what it is and why to use it

### A universal, undiscussed tool
Snyder calls The Board perhaps the most important piece of equipment a screenwriter has after paper, pen and laptop. One on an office wall tells him a headache is in progress. It comes in many forms: chalkboards, corkboards with index cards and pins, legal-pad sheets taped to hotel walls during a rewrite on location. Yet he has never heard a screenwriting course discuss it (Snyder, ch. 5).

### How he learned it
He first saw one on Cheda's wall twenty years earlier. Although he had already been paid to write scripts, he had always just started writing and let the scenes land where they fell. Cheda taught him to plan with The Board and to get much more out of it. Snyder uses a large wall corkboard, index cards and pushpins. He keeps rubber-banded decks of cards for every project, finished or not, and can revive any of them by laying the deck back out to see where he stopped (Snyder, ch. 5).

### What it does
The Board lets you "see" the film before writing it. You can test scenes, story arcs, ideas, lines of dialogue and story rhythms cheaply, and find out whether they work. It is not writing, and the plan may be thrown out once drafting starts, but it is where you work out a story's problems in advance. He calls it the one tool he knows for building "the perfect beast," a well-plotted film (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Its two great benefits
Half joking, he names two (Snyder, ch. 5):
1. **It is tactile.** You work with pens, cards and pins, things you can touch, see and rearrange, instead of only typing.
2. **It eats up time, gloriously.** Choosing a board at an office-supply store, deciding where to hang it, shuffling a deck of cards in a café to lay out sequences, set pieces and "whammies." The serious point: while you do this apparently idle work, the story sinks into your subconscious in a different way. A good line or an idea for a chase can go on a card and be stuck where it might belong.

**No blank pages.** Everything is card-sized, and anyone can fill an index card, so The Board is a pressure-free zone (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 3. Setting it up: first cards first

### Four rows
Put up the board, then divide it with three strips of tape (or marker lines) into **four equal rows** (Snyder, ch. 5):

| Row | Covers | Pages |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Act One | 1–25 |
| 2 | Act Two, first half (to the midpoint) | 25–55 |
| 3 | Midpoint to the Break into Three | 55–85 |
| 4 | Act Three to the final image | 85–110 |

After a few uses the layout becomes familiar, like a well-known track.

### The ends of the rows are the hinges
Each of the first three rows ends on a turn: row 1 on the **Break into Two**, row 2 on the **Midpoint**, row 3 on the **Break into Three**. These are the story's turns. Snyder says this matches his mental map of a script, and fits Syd Field's view that each turn sends the plot off on a new heading (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Tools and the title card
His kit is index cards, felt-tip pens and colored pushpins. Partly to waste time, he writes the film's title on a card and pins it at the top. In weeks or months the board will be covered with cards, arrows, color codes and cryptic notes (Snyder, ch. 5).

### What goes on a card
Cards mainly stand for **scenes**. In the rough stage you may use as many as you like; the finished board will have **40, and no more** (Snyder, ch. 5). Each finished card holds:
- **Where:** interior or exterior and location, written like a scene heading (e.g., an apartment, by day). A sequence that moves through several locations, such as a chase, can be one card.
- **What happens:** the scene's basic action in one plain declarative sentence (his example is one character telling another she wants a divorce).
- More detail comes later (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Burning ideas go up first
Start with the scenes you are sure of, the ones that made you want to write the film. For Snyder these are usually comic set pieces, the hero's introduction, and maybe the finale. Pin each where you think it belongs; it may move or be cut later (Snyder, ch. 5).

**What you then see** is mostly empty space. The ideas that felt huge in your head look small once pinned up, and it is clear the story will not write itself. Each great scene turns out to be a fragment of the whole, and the hard work starts here (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 4. The major turns
Next, nail the **hinge points**. Snyder always tries to settle the major turns first (Snyder, ch. 5):
1. **Midpoint first.** Decide whether it is an "up" (a dizzying false victory on page 55) or a "down" (an equally false defeat). Settling it steers everything else; no other choice has to come first. Most writers can find the Break into Two, because the set-up and the start of the adventure are the film they already imagine. The midpoint tells you where it goes from there.
2. **All Is Lost next.** It is the midpoint flipped: whatever was up or down at the midpoint, reverse it into its false opposite. Both may need adjusting together.
3. **Then the Break into Three,** which is usually easy once the first two are set.

Note ⚠️: the text lists the hinge cards in the order midpoint, then the "Act Two" break, then the "Act One" break. From context, "Act Two break" seems to mean the turn out of Act Two (the Break into Three), but the wording is ambiguous. The Glossary's "Major Turns" entry has a similar slip.

## 5. Overloaded acts and black holes

### Cards that are not scenes
Snyder's own common mistake is writing cards that are not actual scenes, especially in Act One. He budgets three or four cards (three or four scenes) to reach the catalyst, but finds seven or eight cards of **backstory**, such as facts about the hero's past or his hobby. These are not scenes; they will be folded into one "Meet the Hero" card, the moment we first see him walk in. At this stage, though, let the set-up ideas pile up. Get everything out, try anything; it will be pared down later (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Sequences
A chase or similar sequence may span many scenes and places, but it is **one beat**. Five to seven cards for one sequence will eventually fold into one (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Black holes
A **black hole** is a gap where you cannot see how to get from one block of story to the next. The Board's advantage is that the gap is visible and stays visible, taunting you, so at least you know where the problem is and what must be filled. Each row needs roughly nine or ten cards (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 6. The eternally light Act Three
Early boards almost always have a thin Act Three, often just two cards: the hero works out what to do, and the showdown. Writers keep putting off fixing it. Usually ideas flood in eventually. If not, Snyder's checklist for filling it is (Snyder, ch. 5):
- **Set-ups and the Six Things That Need Fixing:** does Act Three pay every one of them off? It should.
- **The B story:** whether it is the love story or the theme's center, it must pay off too.
- **Loose ends:** minor (C, D, E) storylines, repeated images, themes. All this "bookkeeping" has to be settled in Act Three; there is nowhere else to do it.
- **The villains:** have all the lieutenants been dealt with on the way to the chief villain? Has every detractor received his comeuppance?
- **The world:** has the hero's action changed it?

Done honestly, Act Three fills up to the nine or ten cards it needs (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 7. Color-coding
Use different colored pens for each character's storyline (one color for one lead's cards, another for the other lead's) so the weave of the storylines, or its flaws, is visible in one look. Snyder says you will wonder how you ever worked without it. Scripts are structure, precision clocks of emotion, and seeing the colored threads shows how essential the planning is (Snyder, ch. 5). Color can also track:
- story points that serve the theme, and repeated imagery;
- minor characters' arcs;
- C, D and E stories.

**Why it saves time:** moving cards is far easier than moving chunks of finished prose you have grown attached to. Cutting "darlings" is harder once they are written. Organizing first makes the writing more enjoyable (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 8. Stripping it down to 40
The finished board gets **forty cards**, about ten per row. Fifty or twenty means trouble (Snyder, ch. 5).
- **Usually you have too many.** Test each beat: can its action or purpose fold into another scene, or be dropped? Snyder's own problem area is the set-up, where he sometimes has twenty cards because he fears not getting enough across. Honest cutting gets him to nine, which he considers ideal.
- **Sequences:** label the whole stretch with a single word such as CHASE and count it as one beat, since that is usually all it contributes to the plot.
- **Light areas:** in thin stretches, such as Bad Guys Close In, he allows himself some slack, occasionally even leaving a gap and hoping the writing produces a miracle. He knows these spots must be dealt with eventually; the point is knowing where they are (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 9. Two symbols on every card: +/- and ><
With forty cards in place, you are still not ready. Each card needs two marks, written at the bottom in a color not used elsewhere (Snyder, ch. 5).

### +/- : the emotional change
Treat every scene as a **mini-movie** with a beginning, middle and end. Its emotional tone must shift sharply, from plus to minus or minus to plus, the way a whole film's first and last images are opposites. His example: a lawyer starts a scene elated at winning a case (plus); his wife arrives and, with the trial behind them, asks for a divorce (minus) (Snyder, ch. 5).
- **Every scene must have one.** Without it, you have not yet found the scene's point.
- **Don't start writing** until all forty cards have a +/-. If a card resists, discard it; it is probably wrong.
- **Contrast with McKee:** some, such as Robert McKee, want these charges to alternate scene to scene in a steady wave of highs and lows. Snyder thinks that goes too far; it is enough that each scene changes something and shows it (Snyder, ch. 5).

### >< : the conflict
His mental picture: as a scene opens, two people come in through opposite doors, collide in the center, and struggle to get past each other to the far door. Each arrives with a goal, and the other is the obstacle. That is conflict. It may be physical, verbal, or as small as someone desperate to reach a bathroom. The classic categories from school (man against man, against nature, against society) all apply (Snyder, ch. 5).
- When a scene opens, know its main conflict, who is pushing against whom, each side's agenda, and how they collide.
- On the card, the >< records the players, the issue, and the winner.
- **One conflict per scene.** Extra opponents or extra issues make the conflict muddy, and the scene will be muddy too.
- **Every scene, every time.** If none exists, create one.

**Why conflict is required:** it is primal, and it holds attention. Snyder points to wrestling as television's longest-running drama (a contest to the death, at its root) and to romance in most films (a contest over sex). Every scene must be that basic at its core (Snyder, ch. 5).

**The rule:** find the conflict, rethink the scene, or throw out the card, and do not mourn it, since it is only a card (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 10. Ready to write

### The final count
Nine cards per row gives thirty-six. Snyder grants four extra for scenes you cannot bear to lose, placed anywhere, for forty total (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Don't over-perfect the Board
There are no prizes for a perfect board. Ask whether you are a board-builder or a screenwriter. Too much polishing means you have left preparation for the **Procrastination Zone**. Snyder starts writing just *before* the board is finished, before it sets hard (he compares it to a gelatin mold), when his obsession with pins and cards tells him to stop. Speed is the key (Snyder, ch. 5).

### The Board is a trick, not a contract
Once the forty beats and their symbols are done, he has prepared as much as possible, and typing suddenly feels great. The Board is a way of storing moments, rhythms, scenes and sequences in his head, of playing with them without committing. He must always be ready to discard it. He has often dropped his whole plan once writing began, or watched a minor character not even on the board grow into a lead. What The Board does is prepare the ground: test theories, fix some notions firmly, and shrink others (Snyder, ch. 5).

### What to keep in mind once writing starts
When you begin the draft, The Board itself stops mattering, but three things should stay with you (Snyder, ch. 5):
- hit the act break on page 25;
- hit the midpoint and the All Is Lost hard;
- have conflict in every scene.

These are the islands to keep in sight. The aim of everything is to reach THE END. If you get lost, go back to The Board. The worst outcome is not finishing, because half-finished scripts never sell, and planning on The Board is the best insurance against it (Snyder, ch. 5).

### The secret weapon
When truly stuck, Snyder sends his notes to Mike Cheda (for example, asking what should happen in Bad Guys Close In). Cheda actually reads the material, knows how to fix it, gives detailed notes, and is blunt; Snyder jokes about returning from a long lunch to hear Cheda ask if he is kidding. The book gives Cheda's website and his then fee of $500, which Snyder thinks far too low, and notes a magazine dubbed him a screenwriting Dr. Phil. To Snyder he is the man who taught him everything, for free (Snyder, ch. 5).

## 11. Summary: the deep-sea dive
Snyder recaps what the writer should have before typing FADE IN:, as a four-point checklist he names for a deep-sea dive (Snyder, ch. 5):
1. **The idea:** a killer title and logline, tested on friends and strangers who all want to see the film.
2. **Genre homework:** you know what your film is most like, have screened the relevant films of the past two decades, know what they did right and wrong, and know how yours advances the type.
3. **The hero:** the one with the most conflict, the longest emotional road and the widest appeal, with a primal goal and a genuine antagonist blocking it.
4. **The beats:** the story beaten out with the BS2 and on The Board, reduced to forty real scenes, each marked with its emotional change (+/-) and its conflict (><).

### The diver and the dock
He pictures the writer as a deep-sea diver going down into the subconscious, with Snyder and fellow writers on the dock feeding the air line. He urges writers to have real-life supporters too. Down there you will see strange and wonderful things and surprise yourself, but doubt and anxiety, like the bends, can make you see dangers that are not there. You need someone trusted on land backing the effort. He ends by wishing the writer good hunting and promising "fun things" in the next chapter (Snyder, ch. 5).

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 5; described, not copied)
1. **Board a film you screened:** take the beat sheet of a genre film you have studied, put its beats on index cards, and lay them on a board, or on the portable sketchbook version.
2. **Chart +/- in favorite scenes:** pick scenes from favorite films and identify how each starts and ends emotionally, and whether the two are opposites.
3. **Chart >< in the same scenes:** identify the opposing forces and who wins. Consider whether your favorite scenes are the ones with the most conflict, and whether bigger conflict is better.

## Terms introduced
- **Guru:** an experienced mentor who shares hard-won knowledge (for Snyder, Mike Cheda).
- **The Board:** a wall board (cork, chalk, or a sketchbook) divided into four rows, on which the story is laid out with index cards.
- **Four rows:** Act One; Act Two to the midpoint; midpoint to Break into Three; Act Three.
- **Hinges / major turns:** the Break into Two, Midpoint and Break into Three, at the ends of rows 1–3.
- **Index card (beat card):** one scene (or one sequence), with location and a one-sentence action.
- **Forty cards:** the target count for a finished board, roughly ten per row (nine per row plus four spares).
- **Meet the Hero card:** the single introduction scene into which backstory cards get folded.
- **Sequence / CHASE card:** several scenes that count as one beat.
- **Black hole:** a visible gap where you do not know how one stretch connects to the next.
- **Light Act Three:** the thin, two-card third act typical of early boards.
- **Bookkeeping (of Act Three):** paying off set-ups, the B story, minor threads and every villain and detractor.
- **Color-coding:** tracking each storyline, theme, image or minor arc in its own color.
- **Darlings:** material a writer is attached to; easier to cut as cards than as prose.
- **+/- :** the emotional charge change that every scene must show.
- **>< :** the single conflict of each scene: who opposes whom, over what, and who wins.
- **Mini-movie:** Snyder's view of each scene as having its own beginning, middle and end.
- **Procrastination Zone:** over-perfecting The Board instead of writing.
- **Whammy:** a big set piece or action jolt (from a producer's rule about reels; see Glossary).
- **Deep-sea dive checklist:** the four readiness conditions before writing.
