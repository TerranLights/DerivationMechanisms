## The Foreword (Sheila Hanahan Taylor)

### Who writes it and what she claims
A producer at Zide/Perry and a teacher in UCLA's producers' program recommends the book. Her points, in brief (Snyder, Foreword):
- She used the book's tools to re-check her own projects in development, so it serves beginners and working producers alike.
- It explains well-known commercial hits (*Miss Congeniality*, *Die Hard*, *Legally Blonde*, *Signs*) from every angle a studio cares about: genre, plot, structure, marketing and casting.
- She compares it to a phrasebook for the private language that studio executives and producers use. Learning to think like the people who pay for films brings a writer closer to selling.
- Her company already gives writers the same advice: watch films in the right genre and break them down; ask what the poster and the cast would be; use similar films as a yardstick.
- She values that it starts from the business side and balances art against commerce, and she shelves it next to Syd Field as a quick refresher on commercial structure.

## The Introduction (Snyder)

### Why another screenwriting book
Snyder names the books he respects: Syd Field as the founder of the modern how-to; Viki King's *How to Write a Movie in 21 Days* (he says he did it and sold the result); Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, which he calls the best book on storytelling; and Robert McKee, mainly as a seminar performance worth seeing once (Snyder, Introduction).

He then gives four reasons for his own book (Snyder, Introduction):
1. **It talks the way the industry talks.** He finds the other books academic and too reverent about film. He wants to use the working shorthand of writers and executives.
2. **The author has sold scripts.** He cites about twenty years as a working screenwriter, many high-concept spec sales, some bidding wars, a couple of produced films, and notes from major producers and executives.
3. **The method has been tested by teaching.** He has taught it to other writers who went on to sell.
4. **It tells the truth about your chances.** He objects to courses that encourage ideas that cannot sell. "Follow your heart" is fine for therapy, he says; a writer who wants better odds needs to hear when a cherished personal story has no market.

He adds that he does not want a teaching career; he wants to pass on what he knows.

### The problem he sees in current films
He is frustrated that well-funded productions ignore the basics. He describes a trend of spending heavily, opening very wide and recouping on the first weekend, regardless of a steep drop the next week. His view: the money spent on stars, effects and marketing would do more good if the filmmakers simply followed the rules of good storytelling first (Snyder, Introduction).

**Example: *Lara Croft 2*.** He argues it failed to draw its target male audience because the heroine is cold and humorless. Making her "cool" (a cool car, a cool suit) is not character development. A character who is cool but not likable will not get people out of the house (Snyder, Introduction).

### The core claim: we must like the person we travel with
Snyder states that nothing pulls an audience into a story more than liking the hero it travels with; he ranks it first among all elements (Snyder, Introduction). Everything about the book's title follows from this.

### The "Save the Cat" scene (first definition)
He names a scene that, in his view, modern films have stopped including: our first encounter with the hero, in which he performs some act (the emblem is rescuing a cat) that reveals his character and wins the audience over (Snyder, Introduction).

**Example: *Sea of Love*.** Al Pacino's cop runs a clever sting, which makes him "cool." On the way out he spots a late-arriving parole violator who has brought his boy along, and he quietly waves the father off rather than arrest him in front of the boy, with a tough parting line so he does not seem soft. That two-second kindness makes Snyder like him, follow him anywhere and root for him to win (Snyder, Introduction).

He contrasts the tiny cost of writing such a scene with the millions spent on Angelina Jolie's costume in *Lara Croft 2*.

### What the title stands for
The title is shorthand for the commonsense basics he wants to teach, which he calls the laws of physics governing good storytelling, learned the hard way in Hollywood (Snyder, Introduction).

### Who the book is for
It is aimed at writers who want to sell to the major studios, reach the widest audience, and get a hit (and ideally a sequel). He likes independent film, but this book is about mastering the mainstream market (Snyder, Introduction).

### Debts and attitude to the rules
He credits his writing partners (Howard Burkons, Jim Haggin, Colby Carr, Mike Cheda, Tracey Jackson, Sheldon Bull), his agents and manager, and his students, who pushed back on him. He presents the rules as ones that work reliably, and invites readers to learn them, use them, and even break them, then pass their own rules on (Snyder, Introduction).

## Terms introduced
- **Save the Cat scene:** the early scene where the hero does something that defines him and makes the audience like him. Fuller rule in ch. 6.
- **Make-Sure-It-Opens-Or-Else trend:** Snyder's label for big-spend, wide-release, front-loaded openings that ignore weak word of mouth.
- **Laws of physics (of storytelling):** his recurring name for the basic rules the book teaches. Ch. 6 calls them the Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics.
