# Chapter 3: It's About a Guy Who...

## 1. From "what" to "who"
After the concept, the next step is deciding whom the film is about. Snyder recalls his father's standing request for a story about "a guy who...", and says any pitch he hears must contain some version of that phrase (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Why the "who" matters
The "who" is the audience's entry point. Viewers fix on a person and project themselves onto them, whether the work is an epic or a detergent ad. That person need not be human: corporate mascots draw us into a product's "story" for the same reason. An idea is easier to communicate when someone on screen is living it for us. His paired examples are Lawrence planning to take Aqaba overland in *Lawrence of Arabia* and a busy mother waiting for a headache pill to work in a commercial (Snyder, ch. 3).

The screenwriter's task is to build audience stand-ins that appeal to the target market *and* serve the story's needs (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 2. Adjectives in the logline
This begins in the logline, which should hook us with *someone* as well as *something*. That is why good loglines carry adjectives for the hero (a risk-averse teacher, an agoraphobic stenographer, a timid banker) and for the antagonist (a protective cop, a power-mad terrorist, a murderous baker). Snyder adds three items to ch. 1's list of what a logline needs (Snyder, ch. 3):
- an adjective describing the hero;
- an adjective describing the bad guy;
- a compelling goal that people recognize as human.

These quick sketches of hero, opponent and goal give the listener something to latch onto and follow.

## 3. Every film needs a lead
Even ensemble films have a lead; Snyder's examples are *Pulp Fiction*, led by John Travolta, and *Crimes and Misdemeanors*, led by Woody Allen. A film must be about one or two people we can focus on, identify with and root for, and who can **carry the theme** (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Snyder's own order: idea first, then hero
He admits he rarely starts with the hero. The idea usually comes first, and a hero built into the idea is a bonus. Others work differently, he says, but in his approach the hero is there to serve the concept, not the reverse. Once the idea is solid, you improve it by finding the right characters, above all the hero (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 4. The logline often contains the hero

### *Poker Night*
In a comedy he and Colby Carr sold to Disney, the pitch was the characters: a henpecked husband gets the house to himself for a weekend and loses it to a crooked gambler at poker. Its shorthand was *Risky Business* with a father. All they had to do was balance hero against villain and make the film about the father growing from henpecked to empowered (Snyder, ch. 3).

### *Third Grade*
In a comedy sold to Universal, a man caught speeding outside his old school is sentenced to repeat third grade to learn manners. The question was which hero would squeeze the most comedy, the greatest distance traveled and the largest lesson out of that premise. Development showed the answer: a man who has never grown up. Outwardly he is a successful businessman in line for promotion, designing violent video games for children (an irony Snyder points out), but inwardly he has never learned basic decency. The poster comes straight from the premise: a grown man in an expensive suit, crammed into a child's desk among unruly eight-year-olds. The gimmick only means something once you find the right hero to send through it (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 5. Amping up the logline
Often the idea only hints at the right hero. Adjust the characters until the hero faces maximum conflict, travels furthest, and wants something as primal as possible. Snyder demonstrates by trying different "whos" on ch. 1's loglines (Snyder, ch. 3).

- ***4 Christmases*.** All he knows is that the leads are a young couple from divorced and remarried families. He would make them newlyweds who reject their parents' failures but whose own marriage is already shaky, so the day becomes a test: repeat their parents' path, or commit for good? That deep wish to stay together gives the couple something worth rooting for and a complete journey from start to finish. In his words, the story then *is* the characters (Snyder, ch. 3). He notes he has not read the script; this is how he would do it.
- ***Ride Along*.** The adjectives (risk-averse teacher, overprotective cop) and the primal goal (the woman both men love) tell him the shape: an ordeal that tests whether the teacher is brave enough to win his fiancée. Swap in an ex-Green Beret suitor and a timid cop, and it becomes a different film. The point: you can ruin a good idea with the wrong characters. He prefers the original (Snyder, ch. 3).
- ***The Retreat*.** As written, it is a green young employee's first corporate retreat, where someone tries to kill him. Make him a 65-year-old about to retire and it becomes a company "downsizing" staff before their pensions vest. The premise stays, but the journey, the moral and the audience all change, and he jokes that no one would show up (Snyder, ch. 3).

### The three tests for the right hero
Snyder's rule: pick the hero who (Snyder, ch. 3)
1. generates the **most conflict** given the premise;
2. has the **longest emotional distance** to travel;
3. is the most **demographically pleasing**.

### Demographics and the writer's blind spot
On the third point he describes his own bias. Past forty, he catches himself imagining every hero as a weary, wise forty-year-old, a film nobody would attend (though, he jokes, the French would love him for it). Stars of his generation suit ensemble family films but rarely lead. His fix is to keep the existential dilemma but give it to a teenager, or to make the couple in crisis twenty-somethings, because that is who goes to the movies. He sees no point fighting it (Snyder, ch. 3).

Everyone has a blind spot. The job is mass-market, high-concept films for everyone, worldwide, so do not assume your own tastes, or your friends', are everyone's. He recalls being pitched a film as a vehicle for a singer, and repeats his advice to pitch to real people. His father's advertising story makes the point: a wealthy client refused to buy Sunday TV time because, in his circle, everyone spent Sunday playing polo (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 6. The primal urge
Snyder repeats his key word three times: primal. Once you have the hero, his motive must be basic. A promotion should matter because it wins the woman he loves or pays for his daughter's operation. A clash with an enemy should build to life or death, not a minor squabble (Snyder, ch. 3).

**Why:** primal drives grab attention. His list of them recurs through the book: survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones, fear of death. The strongest ideas and leads run on basic wants and needs (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Test: remove the primal element
- *4 Christmases* with the leads as childhood friends instead of a married couple: same premise, still funny, but no stakes and no reason to root.
- *Ride Along* without the fiancée: survival remains, since the teacher must live through the night, but the fiancée as prize is what makes it resonate. He compares it to a knight-errant tale where the princess is the reward, a structure that works in any era.
- *The Retreat* with pranks instead of murders: no stakes; it becomes a training video or an "existential metaphor."

The lesson: give the hero real, simple, understandable stakes (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Primal relationships
Audiences respond most to stories about spouses, parents and children, and exes, because everyone has those people in their lives. Say "father" and each listener pictures his own. When in doubt, anchor characters in the deepest, most universal images available, ones any caveman would grasp (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 7. Casting the hero: don't

### The pitfall
Savvy writers think they know which actor wants which role next. Snyder insists they do not. His three rules (Snyder, ch. 3):
- Do not cast the film before you sell the script.
- Do not write parts for particular actors.
- Do not fixate on one actor; you will be let down.

### *Chickenhawk Down*
In 2004 he and Sheldon Bull wrote a comedy in which a U.S. President, stranded in hostile territory after a helicopter crash, has to capture Osama bin Laden single-handed, finding his "inner leader." Its shorthand crossed *Galaxy Quest* with the then-current President. It did not sell because almost no actor could play the lead *and* carry the opening; Tim Allen was their first choice and there was hardly a second. They had boxed themselves in on casting (Snyder, ch. 3).

### The lesson
Leave room for casting. Many actors should be able to play your leads and "open" the film; this is one reason young roles are in demand, since there are so many young actors. And no one knows what an actor wants next, not even the actor, and not you (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 8. Actor archetypes

### Why stars repeat roles
Major stars play one kind of part again and again because audiences need to see certain **archetypes** on screen. Today's stars stand in for earlier ones. Snyder's pairings: Russell Crowe for Errol Flynn, Jim Carrey for Jerry Lewis, Tom Hanks for Jimmy Stewart, Sandra Bullock for Rosalind Russell. These figures answer an inner need. **Write for the archetype, not the star,** and casting will follow (Snyder, ch. 3).

### "Snyderian" archetypes
He offers his own, loosely Jungian set. Each is named here with its gist (examples abbreviated) (Snyder, ch. 3):
- **Young man on the rise:** plucky, a bit naive, Horatio Alger-like; the type we want to see succeed (Harold Lloyd to Adam Sandler).
- **Good girl tempted:** pure-hearted and appealing; his female equivalent (Doris Day to Reese Witherspoon).
- **The imp:** a clever, resourceful child (Jackie Coogan, Macaulay Culkin), with an evil twin, the **bad seed**.
- **Sex goddess:** from Mae West to Halle Berry.
- **The hunk:** the male counterpart, from Valentino to Vin Diesel.
- **Wounded soldier on a last redemptive mission:** Paul Newman, Clint Eastwood.
- **Troubled sexpot:** Veronica Lake, Angelina Jolie.
- **Lovable fop:** e.g., Hugh Grant.
- **Court jester:** e.g., Danny Kaye.
- **Wise grandfather:** Alec Guinness, then Ian McKellen.

He adds a wider cast of recurring figures (magic dwarves, tricksters, sidekicks, talking animals, spinsters, wizards, Falstaff types, misers) that keep returning with the same story function (Snyder, ch. 3).

### What archetypes do
Each archetype carries a story arc audiences want to see replayed: who earns victory, who earns comeuppance. Whatever the fashion, audiences still want justice for characters they hate and triumph for those they admire, and Snyder suggests these patterns are built into us. Like knowing the history of story types, knowing your characters' ancestry is essential. The task: forget the stars, focus on the archetypes, and make them new (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 9. Special circumstances
Ensembles, biographies and animated adaptations look like exceptions, but the hero is found the same way as in any original spec (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Biography
- **The unlikable subject.** In *Kinsey*, writer-director Bill Condon faced a subject whose behavior many would find objectionable. Finding the hero meant also finding a "bad guy," the formula *The People vs. Larry Flynt* used to make a pornographer heroic (Snyder, ch. 3).
- **Fudging the facts.** *A Beautiful Mind* omitted unflattering episodes from John Nash's romantic history and combined two real marriages into a single wife. Snyder says this is common, done with legal advice (Snyder, ch. 3).
- **Changing the point of view.** Snyder's own DeLorean project hit a wall when research suggested John DeLorean may have been a swindler rather than a wronged maverick. He made the hero an insider who wrote one of the books he read: a man who believed in DeLorean and grew disillusioned. Showing how he was fooled gave the audience a way in. He titled it, ironically, *Dream Car* (Snyder, ch. 3).
- **The rule:** a biography's hero must still be someone the audience roots for, or at minimum comprehends (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Ensemble
The hero need not have the most scenes (*Pulp Fiction*, *Crimes and Misdemeanors*), but a dozen characters with equal screen time make it hard to find a way in. Robert Altman's ensembles (*Nashville*, *Welcome to L.A.*, *Short Cuts*) have no central lead, but he treated the city as the star: a different kind of hero that served his moral. In an ensemble, the hero is usually **the character who carries the theme**. To find him, ask who clashes hardest with the others and who grows most; then ask the usual questions about conflict, emotional distance and likability (Snyder, ch. 3).

### Animated adaptations of old material
Adapting across time and culture can require changing the hero. Disney's Aladdin went from an unlikable street thief in the source to an easygoing modern figure (developed in ch. 6). Disney's *Mulan*, *Pocahontas* and *Hunchback* faced similar problems with mixed results. Whether the cast is prehistoric animals or insects (*Ice Age*, *Antz*, *A Bug's Life*), the method for finding a winning logline and its hero is the same (Snyder, ch. 3).

### The five-point prescription
In every case, stick to basics. The hero should be someone the audience (Snyder, ch. 3):
1. can identify with;
2. can learn from;
3. has a compelling reason to follow;
4. believes deserves to win;
5. sees facing primal stakes that ring true.

Whatever the assignment, the hero is located by locating the story's heart (Snyder, ch. 3).

## 10. Slave to the logline
Once the hero and his primal goal are settled, go back and rewrite the logline with what you learned. Snyder openly asks writers to be "slaves" to it (Snyder, ch. 3):
- The logline is the story's code or DNA, the one thing that must stay true. A good one guides the whole writing.
- It is the touchstone for both writer and audience. Stay true to it and you deliver the best story; stray from it mid-draft only for a good reason.
- It matters most for the hero, because it states who he is, whom he faces and what is at stake.
- The best logline is the one with the sharpest conflict, the clearest hero and villain, and the plainest primal goal. Lock it in and use it to check your work as you write.
- If the writing reveals a better version, restate the logline to match. Holding to the "guy who..." frame keeps you on course from concept to final page.

## 11. Chapter summary points not covered above
- Finding the hero ranks **second** in building a winning concept, where "winning" means "will sell." Concept and cast are where every film starts: the first two questions anyone asks are what it is about and who is in it.
- Family relationships (parent and child, siblings, spouses) make better characters than strangers in the same situation.
- The logline needs an adjective for the hero, an adjective for the antagonist, and a clear, primal goal or setting (Snyder, ch. 3).

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 3; described, not copied)
1. **Genre loglines:** write a logline for each film on your genre list, with strong adjectives for hero and villain and a clear primal goal.
2. **Archetype spotting:** identify the actor archetypes in your genre's films, and which earlier actors could have played the same parts.
3. **Ensemble hero:** name an ensemble film and find its hero. Consider whether every film needs a hero, and name films that have no main one.
4. **Talking car:** write an amped-up logline for the bare idea of a man who acquires a talking car, with a sharpened hero, villain and primal goal, and adjectives that grab.

## Terms introduced
- **"It's about a guy who...":** the phrase that must appear in every pitch; shorthand for a story's hero.
- **Audience stand-in:** the hero as the viewer's point of identification.
- **Logline adjectives:** descriptive words for hero and antagonist that make a logline vivid.
- **Amping up the logline:** reworking the characters to maximize conflict, emotional journey and primal stakes.
- **Most conflict / longest way to go / most demographically pleasing:** the three tests for choosing a hero.
- **Primal:** rooted in basic drives (survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones, fear of death). His central yardstick.
- **Actor archetypes ("Snyderian archetypes"):** recurring character types that stars embody across generations.
- **Write for the archetype, not the star:** his casting rule.
- **Open (a movie):** a star's ability to draw an opening-weekend audience.
- **Slave to the logline:** treating the logline as the story's fixed DNA and touchstone.
