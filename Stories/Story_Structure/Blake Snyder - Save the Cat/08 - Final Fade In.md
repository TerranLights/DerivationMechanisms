# Chapter 8: Final Fade In

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 8 "Final Fade In." The local text has no page numbers; cited by chapter title, shortened below to (Snyder, ch. 8). This chapter is about selling and career, not story craft; it is extracted in full because the brief asks for the whole book.

## 1. The state of the business while he wrote
Snyder lists what happened in Hollywood during the writing of the book (Snyder, ch. 8):
- Sequels had mixed results.
- Many pre-sold franchises succeeded, but some failed badly.
- The strategy of opening very wide and recouping most of the budget in the first week, despite steep second-week drops, kept working, so it will continue.
- Family films beat all other kinds at the box office, which fashionable executives ignored, since PG films are not glamorous.

He calls it a boom, a gold rush: a profitable business with good reasons to invest in new talent (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Good news and bad news for spec writers
- **Good:** studios have money, and the uneven record of pre-sold franchises means they need original ideas, so buying specs makes sense.
- **Bad:** executives credit their successes to their own marketing, accounting and control, like someone who prays for sunrise and then thanks the prayer (Snyder, ch. 8).

### The book's thesis, restated
Selling a script now depends more than ever on treating the screenplay as a **business plan**. With a creative approach you can sell to Hollywood (Snyder, ch. 8):
- a catchy logline and a strong title earn attention;
- a well-structured script keeps you in the game;
- knowing how to fix your own script, and anyone else's, builds a career.

Master those demands and you will win. The open question is how to get in the door (Snyder, ch. 8).

## 2. Ambition vs. fate: getting an agent

### The question every class asks
Students always ask how to get an agent. Snyder half-suggests it is all luck and will happen in its own time, but admits he finds self-promotion easy. He enjoys marketing himself and his scripts, follows up the day after meeting someone, and asks friends for introductions. The worst anyone can say is no (Snyder, ch. 8).

He tells two stories: one of ambition and one of fate.

### Ambition: *The Blank Show*
He and friends made a low-budget TV pilot parodying the then-new world of cable. Snyder took it to Los Angeles, got it scheduled on public-access television, and covered the city's west side with fliers announcing the airtime. The morning after it aired, a producer working with Budd Friedman, who owned the Improv comedy club, called to offer representation, and Friedman offered to manage the group. The troupe later broke up, but the friendship lasted. The lesson: always be "working it" (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Fate: Hilary Wayne
While working as a production assistant on an NBC sitcom, he went home to Santa Barbara for a weekend, went to a club, and met a woman who became his girlfriend. Her closest friend was aiming to become an agent; when she was promoted at Writers & Artists, she asked Snyder to be one of her first clients (Snyder, ch. 8).
- She was his best agent. She made strong deals, understood his talent for concepts, and built his career from nothing.
- They worked in the era when spec sales dominated, and she was expert at setting studios against each other in bidding wars that made the trade-paper front pages.
- They were partners on the same wavelength, both hungry and both respectful of the market: they read where it was going, he wrote the product, she sold it.
- She died in 1998. The market has changed since and the spec frenzy is over, but the industry still depends on strong ideas and writers.

**The lesson:** be bold, and find an ally like Hilary Wayne, since no one succeeds alone (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Selling yourself, in a healthy sense
Writers tend to be inward and private, but to sell a script you must sell yourself. Snyder means this positively: there is nothing crass about it when your interest in the subject is real, and when you seek partners you can benefit as much as they benefit you, both sides gain. Talent and a perfect script are only part of what is needed; you have to leave the workroom, mix with people, dress neatly and smile (Snyder, ch. 8).

## 3. Preparing the field
Treat your career like a well-plotted story: draw up a plan and carry it out step by step (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Inventory
- **You:** a writer with some number of scripts, some sales or none, and a passion for movies.
- **Your product:** your best script and several pitches, with strong loglines and titles per ch. 1.
- **What you need:** an agent to sell your work, and producers to buy it or partner with you to get it set up and made.

### Build a target list
- The **Hollywood Creative Directory** (online, and worth buying in print): production companies, contacts, other writers, and producers working in your genre.
- The same publisher's **talent agency directory**: agents at large and small agencies whose taste matches yours (Snyder, ch. 8).

## 4. First contact

### Lead with yourself
However you approach people (letters, persistence, a stunt like his public-access show), you must introduce yourself and your work. Snyder thinks making it personal, letting them know *you* first, is the best introduction to the work. Hilary Wayne's maxim was "Every Sale Has a Story!", and the story is you (Snyder, ch. 8).

### The contact ladder
In person beats a phone call; a call beats a query letter; a letter beats an email. An email from a stranger usually gets deleted unless he was warned it was coming or it offers something useful immediately (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Think long term
You need an agent now, but you also need a reputation, because you will meet the same people for years. Don't burn bridges, or at least don't burn them completely. Be pleasant, considerate, helpful and upbeat, and keep showing up. Put yourself in each person's place: what they want, how to make dealing with you easy, and what they get from meeting you (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Get someone to go first
Agents come more easily when there is a deal to close, and you are easier to pitch once someone has bought something from you. So if a legitimate company offers to option your script, for a small sum, and nobody else has made an offer, accept. Having someone go first is invaluable; projects in progress show you are not a risk and give you something to talk about (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Keep turning the crank
How to stand out from everyone else pitching? Persist. Every letter, call or coffee meeting is progress, even without an agent or sale. Signs that you are moving (Snyder, ch. 8):
- An agent or producer passes on this project but invites future submissions.
- You meet someone you like. Chemistry has to run both ways, and you now have someone to approach again.
- You have narrowed fifty possibilities to three maybes. Every no gets you nearer a yes.
- You get a **referral**. Ask everyone who turns you down whether there is someone else you should contact. People like to help. A footnote tells how Snyder became an NBC page: a producer he cold-called had no job but recommended him to a friend at NBC, having been a page himself.

## 5. Networking
It really is about whom you know. Ways to build contacts without an agent (Snyder, ch. 8):
- **Film festivals:** go, collect cards, pitch, hear others' pitches. Everyone knows many other people; follow up afterward, ask for referrals, and offer to help on their projects.
- **Classes:** attend where writers gather, but also where aspiring producers go (he mentions a UCLA producers' seminar). Local universities likely have similar courses.
- **Writing groups:** online or local. He belongs to one called The Screenplayers. A good group mixes specialties (comedy, horror) and shares contacts; if none exists, start one.
- **Become an expert:** review films locally or online. Writer-director Rod Lurie and François Truffaut began as critics, which gave them a platform and readiness when the chance came.
- **Move to (or visit) Los Angeles:** for a week or for good, since that is where the business is. Starting over, he would take any job there, ideally as a script reader, and read and network while writing on the side. On a one-week trip, take as many meetings as possible, attend guild events, and bring a bio, cards, sample scripts, treatments and a photo.
- **A personal website:** an idea he had not yet tried: photo, bio, scripts in progress, treatments, sample pages, and current deals (with partners' permission), with the address on your business card.

## 6. What not to do
Because the key is meeting people face to face, he regards some popular advice as busywork (Snyder, ch. 8):
- **Screenplay contests:** he calls most of them a huge waste of time, admitting this will draw criticism. Writers obsess over the results, which mean almost nothing to agents and producers with real business; they pay little and get no film made. *Exception:* contests whose panels include professionals who sell to or work for major studios can offer real learning and networking. If you enter, research carefully and choose only those with high-level professionals at their panels or seminars.
- **Stunts:** attention-seeking gimmicks fail. He lists examples to avoid, such as shipping yourself in a crate to an agency, buying a trade ad begging for work, sending a star a doctored photo of the two of you, or threatening to jump off the Hollywood sign.

## 7. Marketing hits and misses
Snyder shares his own and others' "specialty pitches," to be tried at the reader's own risk (Snyder, ch. 8):
- **A pitch that traveled:** a meeting with co-writer Tracey Jackson went so well that producer David Permut flew them overnight to New York to pitch to Howard Stern.
- **David Kirschner's theatrics:** pitching a *Leave It to Beaver* film to Universal, he had the original TV mother arrive to serve milk and cookies; it sold at the first studio. For *Hocus Pocus* at Disney, he staged a spooky set with actresses dressed as witches, and that sold too.
- **Bob Kosberg:** a producer Snyder calls the king of the logline, who sells a project or two a month, develops ideas with writers, and quickly separates weak concepts from strong ones.
- **A flop:** for *Big Girl on Campus* (pitched as *Tootsie* at college), his partner Howard Burkons pitched dressed as a woman. It did not sell, though Burkons was asked on a date.
- **Specialty send-outs in the early-'90s spec frenzy:** scripts were **tracked** by executives before going out, and packaging became a fad. One script, *Ticking Man*, went out with alarm clocks set to ring when it arrived. Snyder and Jim Haggin sent *Nuclear Family* in twenty homemade, bomb-like "radioactive containment units," cleared in advance with studio security and coordinated by Hilary Wayne; by day's end Jeffrey Katzenberg and Steven Spielberg were bidding. For *Blank Check*, he and Colby Carr sent backpacks stuffed with a million in play money. Each led to a million-dollar sale and trade headlines (Snyder, ch. 8).

**Why it worked, and why it is over:** these campaigns came from genuine excitement, to set the reader's mood the way a film campaign sets the public's. Salesmen are easy to sell, and executives enjoy a show. But the practice is dated: too many bought scripts went unmade, the spec craze ended, and studios now avoid bidding wars. The next attention-getting tactic may come through the internet; the business still loves surprise and showmanship (Snyder, ch. 8).

## 8. "It is what it is"

### Perseverance
Snyder closes on a personal note. He has had great adventures in the business since childhood, and also deep self-doubt. If the work is in your blood, you learn to persist, and failure teaches as much as success. Keep working, keep a good attitude, and know that today could be the day (Snyder, ch. 8).

### The phrase
Before sending out any script, he and Colby Carr would say to each other, "It is what it is." For Snyder it means that a creative project that also has to make money is bounded by demands it must meet. If you have met them, covered every angle and done your best creatively, the rest is fate (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Accepting the business
The industry, likewise, is what it is. The people in charge chase whoever is "hot" and overlook more talented unknowns, often skim scripts, and care more about the headline than your growth. That is the nature of business (Snyder, ch. 8).

### Stay a bullhead, and have fun
You have to build a life inside those limits, and your stubbornness is what will protect your sanity. Though he has teased bullheaded writers throughout, he now urges them to stay that way: the powers that be can buy your script, fire you or rewrite it beyond recognition, but they cannot remove your capacity to rise again and come back stronger. Above all, enjoy every script you write, because enjoyment signals you are on track, and you want the hundredth FADE IN: to excite you as much as the first (Snyder, ch. 8).

## Terms introduced
- **Script as business plan:** treating a screenplay as a product designed for a market.
- **Working it:** active self-promotion (as with *The Blank Show*).
- **Every Sale Has a Story:** Hilary Wayne's maxim that the writer's own story sells the script.
- **Contact ladder:** in person, then phone, then query letter, then email.
- **Going first:** the value of any first option or sale in attracting others.
- **Turning the crank:** steady, repeated outreach until something happens.
- **Referral:** a recommendation to another contact; ask for one with every "no."
- **Specialty pitch / specialty marketing:** theatrical pitches and packaged send-outs.
- **Tracking:** executives' monitoring of a hot script before it goes out (see Glossary).
- **Spec sale frenzy:** the early-'90s period of bidding wars for unsold scripts.
- **Stupid screenwriter tricks:** gimmicks that backfire.
- **"It is what it is":** acceptance that after doing your best, the outcome is fate.
