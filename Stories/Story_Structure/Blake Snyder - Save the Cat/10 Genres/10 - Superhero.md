## What it is
Snyder defines it as the mirror image of Dude with a Problem: an extraordinary person stuck in an ordinary world (Snyder, ch. 2). He uses the picture of Gulliver pinned down on the beach by the Lilliputians. The story asks the audience to give a super-being human feelings and sympathy, and to imagine putting up with ordinary people like us.

The category reaches well past caped crusaders and the big comic-book universes. A "superhero" here is anyone whose exceptional nature sets him apart: a monster, a genius, a gladiator. The common thread is being *different* (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
- **The extraordinary hero.** He has powers, gifts, or a mind beyond everyone around him (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The small-minded world is the real problem.** Often the true obstacle is the petty, mediocre people around the hero, who don't understand him. Being special is hard (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **He didn't choose his world.** Born into a world he didn't make, the hero must cope with people who resent his unusual view and superior mind (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Earn sympathy through pain.** The challenge is how to make us care about a rich man or a genius. Snyder's answer is to stress the suffering that comes with the gift. The Batman figure is tormented, and he is admirable because he gives up his own comfort to serve the community (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Re-earn sympathy in every sequel.** The first film of a superhero series usually works because its origin story builds sympathy for the hero's plight. Sequels often fail because the makers assume that sympathy carries over, and they forget to draw us back into the hero's human side (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **We identify through sympathy, not understanding.** We will never fully understand the superhero. Our bond comes from sympathizing with how it feels to be misunderstood (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
- Everyone sometimes feels misunderstood or punished for being different. Snyder's examples are being shot down at a school meeting or sneered at for a bold idea at work, which lets "us Lilliputians" feel for Frankenstein's monster fleeing the torch-carrying mob (Snyder, ch. 2).
- He jokes that this is why bright, awkward teenagers love comic books: they already know how it feels to be misread (Snyder, ch. 2).
- The genre has lasted because it lets our biggest fantasies about our own potential take off, while grounding them in some reality (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *Superman*, *Batman*, *Dracula*, *Frankenstein*, *Gladiator*, *A Beautiful Mind*: the roster entry.
- *Gladiator* and *A Beautiful Mind*: human superheroes held back by a mediocre world. Snyder notes, as with other genres, that one star (Russell Crowe) keeps landing in the same archetype.
- *Frankenstein*, *Dracula*, *X-Men*: alike in that the hero suffers for being different.
- Bruce Wayne / *Batman*: the tortured rich hero whose sacrifice earns our sympathy.
- *Robocop 2*: a sequel that failed to rebuild sympathy.
- *Spider-Man 2*: a sequel that did rebuild it and became a smash.

Cross-chapter:
- *Zoolander*: Snyder calls it one of his favorite bad movies and a fine example of the Superhero genre (Snyder, ch. 2 summary).
- *Superman*: in the chapter summary he calls Superman a modern Hercules, part of his case that genres retell ancient tales (Snyder, ch. 2).
- *Spider-Man*: in Fun and Games, Tobey Maguire's character tries out his new powers (Snyder, ch. 4). The same film is Snyder's main case of **Double Mumbo Jumbo**: a spider bite and a separate lab accident each grant powers, two sources of magic in one movie. He partly excuses comic-book adaptations (Snyder, ch. 6).
- *Batman* (Michael Keaton and Jack Nicholson): an example of hero and villain as light and dark halves of one person (Snyder, ch. 7, "Make the Bad Guy Badder").
- *Gladiator*: Snyder's example of the primal drive for revenge (Snyder, ch. 7, "Is It Primal?").

## Terms introduced
- **Superhero (genre):** an extraordinary being living in an ordinary world, including human "superheroes" such as geniuses.
- **Lilliputians:** Snyder's name for the ordinary people, including the audience, who surround and misunderstand the superhero.
- **Creation myth:** the origin story that opens a superhero series and builds sympathy for the hero's plight.
