# Institutionalized (Save the Cat, genre 9 of 10)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (genre list, the Institutionalized section, and the summary); also ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (Opening Image). Cited below by chapter number, e.g. (Snyder, ch. 2).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
Stories about groups: institutions, organizations, and "families" of every kind, from the army to the Mafia to a suburban household. When people band together for a shared cause, the story shows what is gained and lost when the individual's goals are sacrificed to the group's. What makes these films distinctive is that they do two things at once. They honor the institution, and they expose the cost of losing yourself inside it (Snyder, ch. 2).

Snyder chose the name because the group dynamic in these stories is so often crazy, even self-destructive (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
- **A group with a shared goal.** The subject is the institution or "family" and its hold on its members (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **A breakout character.** Each of these films has one member whose job is to show that the group's goal is a sham (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The cost of the uniform.** Whether the uniform is a soldier's or a fashionable polo shirt, putting it on means giving up part of who you are. The films weigh the benefits and costs of ranking the group above the self (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Often a newcomer's point of view.** The story is frequently told through someone new to the group, who stands in for the audience and is brought in by a veteran. In any world whose technology, jargon, or rules are unfamiliar, this character is very useful for exposition, because he can simply ask how things work and let the story explain (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The core question.** Every story in the category comes down to asking who is crazier, the individual or the group (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
Snyder calls it another caveman-level story. Group loyalty can defies common sense and even survival, yet humans have always shown it. Watching characters fight that same battle, the one we face every day, is what makes the genre popular and primal (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- The roster entry: *M\*A\*S\*H*, *Animal House*, *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*, plus two "family" sagas, *The Godfather* and *American Beauty*.
- *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*: a group of mental patients. Jack Nicholson's character is the breakout who exposes the group.
- *American Beauty*: modern suburbanites, with Kevin Spacey's character as the breakout.
- *M\*A\*S\*H*: the American military, with Donald Sutherland's character as the breakout. Snyder reads its theme song, "Suicide Is Painless," as being about the madness of the herd rather than of war.
- *The Godfather*: a Mafia family, with Al Pacino's character as the breakout. The final image of Pacino's face in *The Godfather Part II* shows a man who has effectively killed himself for the family and its traditions.
- Snyder pairs that ending with Spacey's late realization in *American Beauty* and Nicholson's blank face after the operation in *Cuckoo's Nest*. The three are, he says, the same film carrying the same message in very different ways.
- *9 to 5* (Jane Fonda's character) and *Animal House* (Tom Hulce's character): newcomers who serve as the audience's way in.

Cross-chapter:
- *The Breakfast Club*: when a reader asks whether it is Rites of Passage or Institutionalized, Snyder says Institutionalized (Snyder, ch. 2 summary).
- *Animal House*'s opening image, the college motto under the founder's statue, is one of his examples of an opening image that sets the tone (Snyder, ch. 4).

## Terms introduced
- **Institutionalized:** a story about a group or "family" that both honors it and exposes the cost of belonging.
- **Breakout character:** the group member who reveals that the group's goal is a fraud.
- **Newcomer (the "virgin"):** the character new to the group who stands in for the audience and draws out the exposition.
- **"Who's crazier, me or them?":** the genre's core question.
