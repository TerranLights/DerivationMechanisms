# Out of the Bottle (Save the Cat, genre 3 of 10)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (genre list and the Out of the Bottle section); also ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (Midpoint), ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics" (Save the Cat, Double Mumbo Jumbo, Laying Pipe, Keep the Press Out). Cited below by chapter number, e.g. (Snyder, ch. 2).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The wish story. A character gets a wish granted, or has one forced on him, and his life starts to change. The name is meant to call up a genie released from a bottle to serve its master, but Snyder says magic is optional. Divine intervention, a magic object, a magical being, or plain dumb luck all count as the same device (Snyder, ch. 2). Usually the gift arrives because we like the character and feel he has earned a break.

Snyder opens the section with his own film *Blank Check*, which he wrote with Colby Carr. The boy hero says aloud that he wishes he had his own money, then gets a million dollars and runs wild with it. There is no magic in that film; the writers worked hard to make the windfall feel grounded, and it still belongs here (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
The genre has two faces (Snyder, ch. 2):

**1. Wish fulfillment**
- **The hero is a put-upon Cinderella.** He is so held down by the people around him that the audience will cheer for anyone or anything that gives him a little joy.
- **The good fortune can't last.** Human nature means we don't want even an underdog to stay on top too long.
- **The hero learns the limits of magic.** In the end he discovers it is better to be an ordinary person like the audience, which suits us, since we know the magic will never come to us.
- **A moral is required.** The ending has to carry a lesson.

**2. Comeuppance (the curse version)**
- **The setup is reversed.** The hero is someone who badly needs to be knocked down a peg.
- **There must still be something redeemable in him.** Snyder calls this trickier to pull off.
- **It needs an early Save the Cat moment.** Early on we must see that this jerk has something worth saving.
- **The curse turns out to be a benefit.** The magic works on him like a curse, he grows through it, and he wins in the end.

## Why it works
Wishing is a deep part of the human psyche. Snyder jokes that some version of "I wish I had..." may be humanity's most common prayer, so "what if" stories that act out wish fantasies are primal and simple enough to "pitch to a caveman." That explains both how common and how successful they are (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *Liar, Liar*: comeuppance. A child's wish forces his lying lawyer father to tell only the truth, on the very day lying is his best courtroom weapon. To survive, the father has to change, and by changing he wins what he really wanted all along: his wife's and son's respect.
- *Bruce Almighty*: wish fulfillment, with God as the source of the magic.
- *The Mask*: another Jim Carrey example, with a magic object as the source. Snyder wonders aloud whether some stars fit certain Jungian archetypes.
- *The Love Bug* (Herbie the magic car), *Love Potion* (an invented formula that makes people fall in love), *Flubber* (a magic substance that rescues a teacher's career): the bestower can be a thing.
- *Freaky Friday* (the Jodie Foster original and the Lindsay Lohan remake), *All of Me* (Steve Martin), *Groundhog Day* (Bill Murray): more comeuppance tales.
- *Blank Check*: wish fulfillment with no magic at all.

Cross-chapter:
- **Midpoint false victory.** Snyder's example of a false-victory midpoint is the hero who has been handed some Out of the Bottle magic and seems to have everything he thinks he wants. The lesson he really needs is still ahead (Snyder, ch. 4, Midpoint).
- *Nuclear Family* (Snyder and Jim Haggin, sold to Spielberg's Amblin): Snyder files it as wish-fulfillment comedy: every member of the family gains a power that fills a personal need, and in the end they all give the powers up, having learned that family matters more than success. That ending is the genre's required moral (Snyder, ch. 6, "Keep the Press Out"; logline in ch. 1).
- *Blank Check* and pipe: Snyder admits the film spends a bit too much setup before the boy reaches the bank with his check (Snyder, ch. 6, "Laying Pipe"). In ch. 2 he also concedes it closely resembles *Home Alone*.

## Terms introduced
- **Out of the Bottle:** a story in which a wish, or a curse, is granted and turns the hero's life over.
- **Wish fulfillment:** the version where a put-upon hero gets the thing he longed for.
- **Comeuppance tale:** the curse version, where a flawed but redeemable hero is forced to change.
- **Put-upon Cinderella:** Snyder's label for the downtrodden wish-fulfillment hero.
