# Buddy Love (Save the Cat, genre 6 of 10)

## What it is
The genre covers two kinds of story that Snyder treats as one: the buddy picture and the love story. His central claim is that a buddy movie is secretly a love story, and every love story is a buddy movie in which sex is possible (Snyder, ch. 2). So the roster runs from cop-buddy films and *Dumb & Dumber* to *Rain Man* and every romance ever made.

**Snyder's origin theory.** He sees the buddy story as largely a product of cinema; there are few earlier examples, *Don Quixote* among them. His guess is that some screenwriter noticed his hero had nobody to react to. A novel can fill that gap with inner monologue and description; a film cannot. Giving the hero a partner to argue things out with solved the problem, and the form became a staple (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
The rules are the same for drama and comedy, with or without sex (Snyder, ch. 2):
- **They start out hating each other.** If they didn't, Snyder asks, where would the story have to go?
- **The adventure shows they need each other.** Each turns out to be an incomplete half of one whole.
- **Needing each other causes more conflict.** Nobody likes depending on someone else, so the discovery makes things worse before it makes them better.
- **The All Is Lost is a breakup.** Near the end comes a separation, a fight, or a "good riddance" farewell, which is none of those things at heart. It is two people who can't bear that they do worse apart. To win, they must give up their egos, and by the final curtain they have (Snyder points ahead to ch. 4 for the beat).

**Sub-variant: the catalyst buddy.**
- Often only one buddy is the hero and does most of the changing, while the other acts as the catalyst for that change and changes little or not at all. Snyder notes that story meetings often come down to arguing over whose story it is (Snyder, ch. 2).
- A subset he calls important is the tale where some "being" enters a person's life, changes it, and leaves. Many boy-and-his-dog stories work this way (Snyder, ch. 2).

**Writing one.** Screen a dozen films from the genre and you'll be struck by how alike they are. That isn't theft; the beats repeat because they keep working (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
Stories about "me and my best friend" are human and universal, so they always connect. Snyder jokes that a caveman would understand the pitch, and so would his friend (Snyder, ch. 2). Elsewhere he gives the primal basis for romance. One drive is finding a mate (*Sleepless in Seattle*), and audiences everywhere understand a love story (Snyder, ch. 7, "Is It Primal?"). Conflict between two people trying to get each other into bed is, he says, why most movies include a romance (Snyder, ch. 5, +/- and ><).

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- Cop-buddy pictures, *Dumb & Dumber*, *Rain Man*, and every love story: the roster entry.
- *Don Quixote*: a rare buddy tale from before film.
- Laurel and Hardy, Bob Hope and Bing Crosby, *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid*, *Wayne's World* 1 and 2: the line of film buddy pairs.
- *48 Hours* (two men), *Thelma & Louise* (two women), *Finding Nemo* (two fish): the dynamic works with any pairing.
- *Bringing Up Baby*, *Pat and Mike*, *Woman of the Year*, *Two Weeks Notice*, *How to Lose a Guy in 10 Days*: romantic comedies that are, genre-wise, refined Laurel and Hardy films with one buddy in a skirt.
- *Rain Man*: Tom Cruise's character changes, while Dustin Hoffman's is the catalyst who barely changes.
- *Lethal Weapon*: the story belongs to Danny Glover's character, with Mel Gibson's character as the agent of change.
- *E.T.*: the "being who comes, changes a life, and leaves" subset.
- Sandra Bullock and Katharine Hepburn, Hugh Grant and Cary Grant: a joke that modern romantic leads are "copying" classic ones, made to show that shared beats are simply good storytelling.

Cross-chapter:
- *Rain Man* is Buddy Love, not Golden Fleece (Snyder, ch. 2 summary).
- In Fun and Games, buddies in every buddy movie clash the most (Snyder, ch. 4).

## Terms introduced
- **Buddy Love:** the buddy picture and the love story treated as one genre with one set of rules.
- **Incomplete halves of a whole:** the buddies' discovery that each lacks what the other has.
- **Catalyst buddy:** the partner who triggers the hero's change while changing little himself.
- **"Whose story is it?":** the development question of which buddy is the real hero.
