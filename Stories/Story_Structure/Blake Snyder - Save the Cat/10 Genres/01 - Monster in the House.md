# Monster in the House (Save the Cat, genre 1 of 10)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (genre list and the Monster in the House section); also ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics" ("Watch Out for That Glacier!"). Cited below by chapter number, e.g. (Snyder, ch. 2).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
Snyder's first genre, and the one he suspects is the oldest story people have told. Strip it down and it has just two components: a monster and a house. Put people inside the house who must destroy the monster (or escape it) and you have a story type so basic that anyone anywhere grasps it at once. Its single rule of survival, in his words, is "Don't... Get... Eaten!" (Snyder, ch. 2).

He stresses that "primal" here is not a polite word for "dumb." The genre works because it needs no cultural translation; he claims you could follow most of these films with the sound off (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
Snyder calls the rules simple and gives them as follows (Snyder, ch. 2):
- **The house is a confined space.** It can be literal or figurative: a seaside town, a spacecraft, a theme park, or even a family. What matters is that the people inside cannot simply leave.
- **A sin has been committed.** Usually the sin is greed, either for money or for sex. That transgression is what brings the monster into being or calls it in.
- **The monster is a kind of avenger.** It is typically supernatural, or at least larger than life. It hunts down those guilty of the sin and lets those who come to recognize the sin survive.
- **Everything else is running and hiding.** Once the setup is in place, the body of the story is the chase through the confined space.
- **The writer's job is the twist.** To add something new to the genre, invent a fresh monster, fresh monster powers, and a fresh way of delivering the scare.

**The genre is not exhausted.** Snyder insists, as he does for every genre, that a new version is always possible, but only if it breaks from cliché and gives the audience the familiar thing with a new angle (Snyder, ch. 2).

**Breaking the rules produces a mess.** When a film ignores the parts above, the tone goes muddled: the audience can't tell whether to laugh or be frightened (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
- It is primal: fear of being killed and eaten needs no explanation, so it travels to every audience, which is why the genre has spawned a long line of global hits and film series (Snyder, ch. 2).
- Snyder's shorthand is that you can pitch it "to a caveman" (Snyder, ch. 2). In ch. 7 he returns to the point: audiences in any country follow films like *Jaws* and *Alien*, since avoiding being eaten is a basic drive (Snyder, ch. 7, "Is It Primal?").

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *Jaws*, *The Exorcist*, *Alien*: his opening trio of textbook cases.
- *Tremors* (and sequels), *Panic Room*: listed in the roster as members.
- *Jurassic Park*: the "house" is a futuristic dinosaur park.
- The *Nightmare on Elm Street*, *Friday the 13th* and *Scream* series, plus haunted-house and ghost stories in general: all members.
- *Fatal Attraction*: proof that the monster need not be supernatural; a person (Glenn Close's character) fills the role, and the house is the family.
- *Arachnophobia*: his main failure case. The monster is a small spider you can step on, so it is neither supernatural nor frightening, and there is no house at all, since the townspeople could just leave. The result is a tonal muddle.
- *Lake Placid*, *Deep Blue Sea*: cited alongside *Arachnophobia* as films that fail because their makers ignored the genre's rules ⚠️ (he groups them without detailing each failure).
- The myth of the Minotaur: an ancient model with a great monster (half man, half bull) and a great house (the labyrinth). Snyder uses it to argue that no genre is ever finished, since nobody back then could have foreseen *Fatal Attraction*.
- In the chapter summary he also calls *Jaws* a retelling of the Minotaur myth and of medieval dragon-slayer tales (Snyder, ch. 2).

Cross-chapter:
- *Outbreak*: Snyder calls it a Monster in the House at heart, but a dull one. The film has to build its "house" by quarantining a town, and it introduces the townspeople far too late for us to care about them. Meanwhile the threat (a virus) creeps toward us too slowly to frighten (Snyder, ch. 6, "Watch Out for That Glacier!").

## Related beats and tools
- **Screen your genre.** Snyder advises a home festival of the genre's films if you are writing one (Snyder, ch. 2). See the ch. 2 chapter file for the general method.
- **"Watch Out for That Glacier!"** A threat that approaches too slowly, or is too remote, kills this genre in particular. The danger must be immediate, and it must threaten people we care about (Snyder, ch. 6). See `../06 - The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics.md`.
- **"Is It Primal?"** The genre is his standard example of a primal drive (Snyder, ch. 7). See `../07 - What's Wrong with This Picture.md`.

## Equivalents in other frameworks
Snyder's genres are story types, not beats, so there is no matching step in Bell, Campbell, Harmon or Weiland. Truby's *The Anatomy of Story* is not organized as a genre system ⚠️ (his separate genre material is not a source for this pass). No clear one-to-one equivalent established.

## Terms introduced
- **Monster in the House (MITH):** a story of people trapped in a confined space with a monster that hunts them.
- **The house:** any confined space the characters can't easily leave, whether a place, a vessel, or a family.
- **The monster:** a usually supernatural avenger that punishes a sin, most often greed.
- **"Run and hide":** Snyder's label for the body of the story once the setup is in place.
- **"Pitch to a caveman":** his test for a story so primal it needs no explanation.
