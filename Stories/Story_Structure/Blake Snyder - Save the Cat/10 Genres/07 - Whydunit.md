# Whydunit (Save the Cat, genre 7 of 10)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (genre list, the Whydunit section, and "Hollywood's Dirty Little Secret"). Cited below by chapter number, e.g. (Snyder, ch. 2).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The investigation story: detective tales, mysteries, conspiracy and exposé dramas, social dramas built around uncovering a wrong. Snyder renames the "whodunit" because he finds the culprit's identity much less interesting than the motive. The roster line says it plainly: who did it hardly matters; why is what counts (Snyder, ch. 2).

What separates it from the Golden Fleece is where the discovery lands. A good Whydunit is not mainly about the hero changing. It is about the *audience* learning something about human nature that it would not have believed before the crime and the case. The story digs into the hidden core of a person and finds something unexpected and usually dark or ugly, which answers the question "Why?" (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
- **A crime or wrong committed by hidden evildoers.** Greed and murder happen, and someone unseen is behind them (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The audience is the real detective.** There is an onscreen stand-in (or several) doing the legwork, but it is the viewers who must sort the evidence and be shocked by what they find (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **It walks on the dark side.** These stories take the audience into the shadowy side of life (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The investigation turns back on us.** Probing humanity's dark side usually becomes a probe of the viewer, like a snake eating its own tail. A good Whydunit, Snyder says, points the x-ray at the audience and asks whether we are this evil ourselves (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Study the masters.** If your idea is this kind of discovery, watch the great Whydunits, noting how the onscreen surrogate stands in for the viewer (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
Snyder grounds it in a near-universal belief: that evil hides in people's hearts. The pleasure is in the shock of discovery and in the uncomfortable recognition that the darkness uncovered may be our own (Snyder, ch. 2). He gives no separate "primal" argument for this genre.

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *Chinatown*: in Snyder's view possibly the finest example of the genre, and a model of great screenwriting. Each viewing takes you deeper into ever smaller chambers, like a nautilus shell.
- *Citizen Kane*: a classic Whydunit about reaching the hidden center of a person's heart.
- *The China Syndrome*, *All the President's Men*, *JFK*, *Mystic River*, *The Insider*: detective stories and social dramas that share the form.

Cross-section:
- *Who Framed Roger Rabbit?* is, in Snyder's view, the same movie as *Chinatown*. He cites it as one of many "same story, new skin" pairs in "Hollywood's Dirty Little Secret" (Snyder, ch. 2).
