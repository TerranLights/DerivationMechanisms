# Golden Fleece (Save the Cat, genre 2 of 10)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (genre list and the Golden Fleece section); also ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (Debate), ch. 7 "What's Wrong with This Picture?" ("Take a Step Back"). Cited below by chapter number, e.g. (Snyder, ch. 2).

## What it is
Snyder's name for the quest story, borrowed from the Greek tale of Jason's voyage for the fleece. Any script that could be called a road movie belongs here. Its core is always the same: the hero sets out to get one thing and ends up finding something else, namely himself (Snyder, ch. 2). On that basis he says, half joking, that *The Wizard of Oz*, *Star Wars*, *Back to the Future* and a road comedy are essentially one movie.

## Working parts and rules
- **Milestones on the road.** The story's turning points are the people and events the hero meets along the way (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The episodes must connect.** Because the form is episodic, it can look like a string of unrelated incidents, but it must not be. The writer's job is to make every stop matter to the hero (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The theme is internal growth.** What each incident does to the hero is the real plot. Progress is measured by how the hero changes, not by distance covered (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The incidents themselves are secondary.** Snyder goes as far as calling the specific adventures nearly irrelevant: whatever set pieces you choose must be bent so that each one marks a step in the hero's growth. His truism is that the story works because of the self-knowledge the hero gains from the events, not because of the events (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The heist branch.** Every heist film lives in this genre too. Any mission, quest, or "treasure in a castle" approached by one person or a team follows the same rules. In these, the mission often becomes less important than the personal discoveries it produces, and the meaning of the job outweighs its twists (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
The quest has been a winning tale "since, well, forever," one of the oldest campfire stories (Snyder, ch. 2). It sits in a lineage that runs through *The Odyssey* and *Gulliver's Travels*; in the chapter summary Snyder also calls *Road Trip* a modern version of Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- The roster entry: *Star Wars*, *The Wizard of Oz*, *Back to the Future*, *Planes, Trains and Automobiles*, plus most heist films.
- *Road Trip*: grouped with the above as "basically the same movie," and later called a modern *Canterbury Tales*.
- *Rain Man*, *Road Trip* and, surprisingly, *Animal House*: films Snyder and Sheldon Bull studied for character dynamics while writing their own road comedy.
- *The Odyssey*, *Gulliver's Travels*: the ancient and classic road stories that carry the genre's truism.
- *Ocean's Eleven*, *The Dirty Dozen*, *The Magnificent Seven*: heist and mission films where personal meaning outweighs the job.
- Snyder and Sheldon Bull's road comedy: a boy expelled from military school returns home and discovers his parents have moved away without a word to him. While writing it they kept adjusting not the adventures but what each one means to the boy (Snyder, ch. 2).

Cross-chapter mentions of the same script:
- **Debate:** as Act One closes, a friendly cab driver drops the boy at the edge of town facing a spooky road; a passing heckler turns his fear into a joke, and he sets off by his own decision (Snyder, ch. 4, Debate).
- **"Take a Step Back":** the first drafts failed because the boy was already a kind kid who helped everyone he met, so he had nowhere to grow. It took seven drafts, over ten months, to push him back far enough emotionally for the journey to mean something (Snyder, ch. 7).
- **Genre boundary:** asked whether *Rain Man* belongs here or in Buddy Love, Snyder answers Buddy Love (Snyder, ch. 2 summary).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
Snyder's genres are story types, not beats, so there is no matching step in Bell, Harmon or Weiland. The closest link is Campbell's hero's journey, which is itself a quest pattern. Snyder praises Campbell in his introduction but does not map this genre onto him ⚠️. Truby's *The Anatomy of Story* is not a genre system ⚠️.

## Terms introduced
- **Golden Fleece:** a quest or road story in which the hero seeks one prize and finds himself instead.
- **Milestones:** the people and incidents on the road, each of which must mark a step in the hero's growth.
- **Heist (as Golden Fleece):** a team or individual going after guarded treasure; same rules, and the personal stakes usually matter more than the loot.
