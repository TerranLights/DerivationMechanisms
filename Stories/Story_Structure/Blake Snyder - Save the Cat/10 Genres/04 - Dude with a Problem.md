# Dude with a Problem (Save the Cat, genre 4 of 10)

## What it is
Snyder sums up the genre in one line: "An ordinary guy finds himself in extraordinary circumstances" (Snyder, ch. 2). The story starts on what seems a normal day, then something enormous and out of the ordinary breaks into it, and the ordinary person has to cope.

The genre's tone and weight vary enormously. Snyder deliberately puts a lean thriller like *Breakdown* in the same bin as *Schindler's List*, and he expects readers to be startled that *Die Hard* and *Schindler's List* share a category (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
- **Two parts, like Monster in the House.** First a "dude," meaning an average man or woman much like the viewer. Second a "problem," something the dude can only beat by reaching deep into himself. From these two parts you can build endless combinations (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The more ordinary the dude, the bigger the challenge feels.** Whether or not the hero has skills, what makes the story work is the size of the problem relative to the hero (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Always push the villain to his worst.** Snyder's rule of thumb is that a worse villain produces greater heroism, because the bigger the problem, the steeper the odds the dude must overcome (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The dude wins by being himself.** However powerful the opposition, he beats it by being willing to use his own individuality to outthink forces far stronger than he is (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
Almost everyone sees themselves as an ordinary person, so the audience lines up with this hero from the very first scene. The situation is primal: imagining how *you* would handle a huge, sudden crisis is one of the most basic fantasies there is (Snyder, ch. 2). In ch. 7 Snyder names the drives at work in two of its films: saving one's family (*Die Hard*) and surviving (*Titanic*) (Snyder, ch. 7, "Is It Primal?").

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *Die Hard*: an ordinary day turns extraordinary when terrorists seize the building where the hero's wife works.
- *Schindler's List*: the Nazis begin taking away the hero's Jewish neighbors and workers.
- *The Terminator*: a robot from the future arrives to kill the heroine and her unborn child.
- *Titanic*: the ship strikes an iceberg and there aren't enough lifeboats.
- *Breakdown* (Kurt Russell): the purest case of an average man with no special skills or training. Like the *Die Hard* hero, his goal is the most ordinary of domestic ones: save his wife.

Cross-chapter:
- *Die Hard*'s logline is one of Snyder's two models of irony in a logline (Snyder, ch. 1).
- In *Die Hard*'s Fun and Games, the hero first outwits the terrorists (Snyder, ch. 4, Fun and Games).
- *Die Hard* is also his example of hero and villain as two halves of one persona: Bruce Willis and Alan Rickman (Snyder, ch. 7, "Make the Bad Guy Badder").
- *Memento*: in the ch. 4 exercises Snyder grants that it falls into Dude with a Problem, then challenges the reader to test whether it fits his beat sheet or is just a gimmick (Snyder, ch. 4, exercises).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
Snyder's genres are story types, not beats, so there is no matching step in Bell, Campbell, Harmon or Weiland. Truby's *The Anatomy of Story* is not a genre system ⚠️. Snyder himself sets this genre up as the mirror image of his own Superhero genre (see `10 - Superhero.md`).

## Terms introduced
- **Dude with a Problem:** an ordinary person thrown into an extraordinary, usually life-threatening situation.
- **The dude:** the average man or woman the audience identifies with.
- **The problem:** the overwhelming situation the dude must dig deep to overcome.
- **"The badder the bad guy, the greater the heroics":** the genre's rule of thumb for villains (Snyder, ch. 2), restated in ch. 7 as "Make the Bad Guy Badder."
