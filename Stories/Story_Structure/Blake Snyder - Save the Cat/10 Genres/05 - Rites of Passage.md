# Rites of Passage (Save the Cat, genre 5 of 10)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (genre list, the Rites of Passage section, and the chapter summary). Cited below by chapter number, e.g. (Snyder, ch. 2).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
Stories of life's painful transitions: puberty and first crushes, the midlife crisis, a marriage ending, aging, addiction, grief. Snyder's roster entry takes in every "change-of-life" story (Snyder, ch. 2). These tales can be dramatic, touching, or very funny, but whatever the tone they share one shape and one set of rules.

He heads off a misreading. Since every movie is about change, calling these stories "about change" says nothing. What sets them apart is that the pain comes mostly from an outside force, which is simply life itself. The hero's own choices play a part, but the "monster" is usually invisible, vague, or impossible to name, and that is exactly why it is hard to fight (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Working parts and rules
- **An unnamed monster.** The antagonist is a life stage or condition (addiction, adolescence, loss) rather than a villain. It creeps up on the hero (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Everyone sees it but the hero.** In a well-made story of this type, everybody around the hero understands what's happening; only the person living through it doesn't (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Only the experience can solve it.** No shortcut or outside rescue helps; the hero has to go through it (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The plot is a slow realization.** The story traces the hero's gradual discovery of what the monster is (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Victory by surrender.** The ending is not a conquest but a yielding to forces bigger than oneself. The end point is accepting one's own humanity, and the moral is always a shrug of acceptance, which Snyder labels "That's Life!" (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Structure as stages of acceptance.** He compares the shape to the stages of acceptance in Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's *On Death and Dying*; the story charts the hero's reluctant acceptance of natural forces he can neither control nor understand, and the win is the hero's eventual ability to smile (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Why it works
Everyone has lived through some version of these transitions, so they resonate. They also hit the most sensitive periods of a life, which Snyder calls part of what makes us human and good material for both poignant and comic stories (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Examples
From the genre roster and the section (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *10*: Dudley Moore's midlife crisis, which Snyder calls perhaps the funniest ever filmed; proof the genre can be comedy.
- *Ordinary People*: a grief story about getting past a loved one's death.
- *Days of Wine and Roses*, *The Lost Weekend*, *28 Days* (Sandra Bullock), *When a Man Loves a Woman* (Meg Ryan): stories of coming to terms with alcohol or drugs.
- *That's Life!*: the Blake Edwards title Snyder borrows as the genre's moral. He notes that Edwards (*10*, *Days of Wine and Roses*, *That's Life!*) seems to thrive in this genre.

Snyder also lists the kinds of life passage the genre covers: puberty, midlife crisis, old age, romantic breakups, and grieving (Snyder, ch. 2).

Boundary case: in the chapter summary he rules that *The Breakfast Club*, which a reader might call a Rites of Passage story, is Institutionalized (Snyder, ch. 2 summary).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
Snyder's genres are story types, not beats, so there is no matching step in Bell, Campbell, Harmon or Weiland. Truby's *The Anatomy of Story* is not a genre system ⚠️. Snyder's own outside reference here is Kübler-Ross's stages of acceptance, not a story framework.

## Terms introduced
- **Rites of Passage:** a story of a painful life transition, where the antagonist is life itself.
- **The unseen monster:** the vague, hard-to-name force (addiction, aging, grief) that torments the hero.
- **"That's Life!":** Snyder's tag for the genre's moral of acceptance (borrowed from a Blake Edwards film title).
