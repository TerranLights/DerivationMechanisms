# Theme Stated (Save the Cat, beat 2)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the Theme Stated section and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also the Glossary ("Thematic Premise"), ch. 4's Break into Three section and ch. 3 "It's About a Guy Who..." (the hero carries the theme). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
Early in the script, someone poses a question or makes a remark that turns out to be what the film is about. Snyder calls this the film's **"thematic premise"** (Snyder, ch. 4; Glossary).

Three features define it in his account (Snyder, ch. 4):
- **Who says it.** Usually not the hero. Usually it is said *to* the hero, often by a minor character (Snyder, Glossary).
- **How it sounds.** Not a slogan. Snyder gives sample themes of the proverb kind (be careful what you wish for; pride before a fall; family over money) but stresses that the real line will be casual and conversational, an aside the hero does not grasp at the time.
- **What it does.** It has a delayed, far-reaching effect. The hero will understand it only later, through the story.

## How it works
- **The screenplay as an argument.** Snyder frames a good script as a case the writer makes for and against a way of living or a goal. Is this dream worth having, or a false hope? Which matters more, wealth or happiness, the individual or the group? The rest of the film lays out that argument and either proves or disproves the opening statement, testing it from every side (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Every film must be about something.** This applies to comedies, dramas and monster pictures alike. A writer who can't say what the film means has a problem. The answer may only surface after a full first draft; once it does, move it to the front (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Treat it as an opening bid.** Snyder's image is of a declaration: state the claim, promise to prove it, then do so (Snyder, ch. 4). The Glossary describes the same thing as a debate set in motion early and settled, one way or the other, by the end.
- **Where the argument continues.** The B story is the place where the theme is discussed openly (Snyder, ch. 4, B Story section). At the Break into Three, conversations about theme in the B story help the hero find the solution. In the Finale, the theme's question is answered.
- **The hero carries the theme.** In ensemble films, the hero is usually the character who carries the theme (Snyder, ch. 3).

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** the heroine claims she has no need to bother with being feminine because she is an FBI agent. The film then tests that claim, asking whether a woman can be both tough and feminine. The Finale answers yes.

## Placement
- Page 5 of a 110-page script; Snyder says page 5 is where he always puts it. He also says "somewhere in the first five minutes" (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 5%.

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Snyder's own pairing:** the B story (where theme is discussed), the Break into Three (where it helps the solution emerge) and the Finale (where its question is answered).
- **Truby:** no step of his own. His approach plans the story backward from the hero's self-revelation (step 1), so the theme is designed in from the start rather than spoken at a fixed spot ⚠️.
- **Weiland:** the Lie the character believes and the Truth that opposes it are often hinted at early ⚠️. The match is in function only.
- **Harmon:** step 2, Need, as the first sign of what the character lacks ⚠️.
- **Campbell:** no clear match.
- **Bell:** no clear match ⚠️.
