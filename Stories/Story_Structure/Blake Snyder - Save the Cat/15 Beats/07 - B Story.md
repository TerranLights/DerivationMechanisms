# B Story (Save the Cat, beat 7)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the B Story section; also the All Is Lost, Break into Three and Finale sections, and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" (color-coding, the light Act Three) and the Glossary ("Booster Rocket," "Thesis, Antithesis, Synthesis"). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The B story is the second storyline, starting just after the hero enters Act Two. In most screenplays it is "the love story," and it is also the storyline that carries the film's theme (Snyder, ch. 4).

"Love story" is meant broadly. In *Legally Blonde* the B story is the heroine's friendship with a manicurist she meets in Boston. Snyder counts it as the film's love story even though it isn't a romance, because it's where she is cared for (Snyder, ch. 4).

## How it works

### A breather after the act break
The leap into Act Two is abrupt and very visible. The opening of the B story works as a small "booster rocket" that carries the audience past that jolt by changing the subject. Snyder notes that this cutaway usually stays consistent with the A story while widening its scope (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **The booster rocket in general.** The Glossary uses the same term for any lively addition that carries a script past a likely dull patch. Such patches typically come right after a big moment like the first act break, or where the action sags late in Act Two.
- **Examples.** *Home Alone*'s polka-band musician livens up the mother's slow trip home late in Act Two. The *Legally Blonde* manicurist arrives just as the law-school story starts to tire (Snyder, Glossary).

### New characters from the upside-down world
B-story characters are often entirely new; we may not have met or even heard of them in the first ten pages. Because Act Two inverts Act One, they tend to be inverted versions of the Act One cast (Snyder, ch. 4).
- ***Legally Blonde*:** the working-class manicurist is a distorted-mirror version of the heroine's sorority sisters, which Snyder calls a classic antithesis character (Snyder, ch. 4).
- ***Elf*:** the hero's love interest, who plays a costumed elf at a store during the holidays, is described the same way (Snyder, ch. 4).

### The home of the theme
The B story is where the film's theme can be talked about openly. In *Legally Blonde*, the heroine confides in her friend what she is learning the hard way at law school. That friendship is where she draws the strength for her final push into Act Three (Snyder, ch. 4).

### Its payoff
- **At the Break into Three.** The B story's characters and its conversations about theme help the hero find the solution. Here the external A story and the internal B story meet and twine together. The classic version has the hero get the key clue from "the girl," solving both problems at once: defeating the villains and winning the beloved (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **In the Finale.** The A and B stories both end in the hero's triumph (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **On The Board.** The B story must be paid off in Act Three along with the C, D and E stories, which is part of why an early Act Three always fills up (Snyder, ch. 5). Snyder suggests color-coding each character's story in its own ink on The Board, so the weave of the strands is visible in one look (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Summary of its jobs
Snyder says you must have a B story. It supplies (Snyder, ch. 4):
- the love story;
- an open forum for the theme;
- the cutaways the A story needs.

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** the love story is between the heroine and her fellow contestants, not her romantic interest, because the theme is femininity and she doesn't know that world. Each contestant is a funhouse-mirror take on femininity, with a quirk and a talent, and each turns out to need and like her. Her bond with them carries the film's message and is its heart. She gets her kiss with the male lead in the end, but it is the contestants who make her grow.

## Placement
- Page 30 of a 110-page script (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 27%.
- **Numbering:** on the BS2 form the B Story is beat 7, listed after Break into Two (page 25) and before Fun and Games, which also starts at page 30 (Snyder, ch. 4). So it begins at the same time as Fun and Games rather than between two beats.
- It runs through Act Two and resolves in the Break into Three and the Finale.
- On The Board it begins early in row 2 (pages 25-55) (Snyder, ch. 5).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Truby:** step 6, Ally or allies ⚠️. Truby treats the ally as a character function, not a separate storyline.
- **Campbell:** Meeting with the Goddess ⚠️, a love or nurture encounter, though not a thematic subplot.
- **Bell:** no clear match.
- **Weiland:** no clear match ⚠️. Her framework handles relationships through the arc rather than a named subplot beat.
- **Harmon:** no clear match ⚠️.
- **Snyder's own pairing:** Theme Stated (the B story is where the theme is argued) and the Break into Three (where A and B fuse).
