# Finale (Save the Cat, beat 14)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the Finale section; also the Set-Up and Break into Three sections and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" ("The Eternally Light Act Three"), ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics" (The Covenant of the Arc), ch. 7 "What's Wrong with This Picture?" (Turn, Turn, Turn) and the Glossary ("Thesis, Antithesis, Synthesis," "Six Things That Need Fixing," "Callbacks," "Running Gags"). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N). The five-point finale is **not** in this book; see the separate section below.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The Finale is Act Three, where the story is wrapped up (Snyder, ch. 4). In it:
- the lessons the hero has learned are put to use;
- the character tics planted in the Set-Up are overcome;
- both the A story and the B story end in the hero's triumph.

The old world is overturned and a new order is built, led by the hero, who draws on what he learned in Act Two's inverted world (Snyder, ch. 4).

## How it works

### Dispatch the bad guys from the bottom up
The opposition is defeated in rising order: underlings and henchmen first, then the boss. The main source of the problem, whether a person or a thing, has to be removed completely before the new order can stand (Snyder, ch. 4).

### The hero must change the world
- **More than a win.** Winning isn't enough. This is where a new society comes into being, and it has to happen in an emotionally satisfying way (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Transform, don't just survive.** The Glossary entry on thesis, antithesis and synthesis makes the same point: surviving the journey is not enough, the hero must transform his world to be truly great. Act Three is the synthesis, where what was and its opposite are combined into something new (Snyder, Glossary).

### Pay off everything that was set up
- **Six Things That Need Fixing.** The flaws, bullies and wishes shown in the Set-Up get fixed and paid off here (Snyder, ch. 4; Glossary). Snyder says audiences love these payoffs, but they only work if the flaw was planted early.
- **Callbacks.** Early setups are recalled, often explaining what they were for (Snyder, Glossary).
- **Running gags.** A repeated bit gets its final twist once the character has changed, as when the coffee drinker finally orders tea (Snyder, Glossary).
- **Everybody arcs.** The Covenant of the Arc holds that every character except the villain changes over the story, and the Finale is where those arcs complete. *Pretty Woman* is his example: everyone is changed except the villain, who learns nothing (Snyder, ch. 6).
- **The climax accelerates.** Under Turn, Turn, Turn, pressure builds through the back half and bursts out in the Act Three climax in a surge of energy and emotion (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The eternally light Act Three (Board work)
On an early Board, Act Three is almost always thin: two cards, one where the hero works out what to do and one for the showdown. Writers keep putting off fixing it (Snyder, ch. 5). Snyder's remedy is a bookkeeping check that will fill the row:
- **Set-ups.** Has every early plant, including each of the Six Things, been cashed in by the end? Any that haven't need to be.
- **B story.** Is the B story, whether a true love story or the film's thematic center, paid off?
- **Loose ends.** Are the minor subplots (C, D, E and so on), the repeated imagery and the themes all tied up? Act Three is the only place left to do it.
- **Bad guys.** Were all the lieutenants killed off on the way to the chief villain? Did every one of the hero's detractors get what was coming?
- **The world.** Has the hero's action changed the world?

Answering these will crowd Act Three with nine or ten cards, which Snyder says is guaranteed (Snyder, ch. 5).

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):**
- **The fusion.** The Finale is the pageant itself. In a textbook synthesis, the heroine survives the talent round by turning her agent's training into a stage act, with her colleague's help. Her two worlds merge.
- **Theme answered.** This settles the Theme Stated question: toughness and femininity can coexist in her.
- **Villains caught.** She then catches the villains, the pageant's organizer and her son, whose warped idea of femininity drove the sabotage.
- **Both stories won.** She has proved herself a woman among women and brought the culprits to justice.

## The five-point finale (not in this book)
The 2005 text of *Save the Cat!* has **no five-point finale**. The Finale section describes only what is summarized above. The five-point structure comes from Snyder's later book, *Save the Cat! Strikes Back* (2009) ⚠️. That book is not on disk and was not checked for this extraction. From general knowledge, and unverified ⚠️:
1. **Gathering the Team** ⚠️: the hero rallies allies and resources and makes a plan.
2. **Executing the Plan** ⚠️: the plan is carried out against the opposition.
3. **The High Tower Surprise** ⚠️: a twist reveals that the plan was not enough, or that the opponent was ready for it.
4. **Dig, Deep Down** ⚠️: with the plan failed, the hero has to find a new, inner resource.
5. **The Execution of the New Plan** ⚠️: the hero acts on that discovery and wins.

The composite labels these points Gather the Team, Storm the Castle, High-Tower Surprise, Dig Down Deep and Executing the New Plan.
