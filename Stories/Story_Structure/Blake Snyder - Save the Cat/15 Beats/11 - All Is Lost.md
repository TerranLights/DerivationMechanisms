# All Is Lost (Save the Cat, beat 11)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the All Is Lost section; also the Midpoint and Bad Guys Close In sections and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 2 "Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!" (Buddy Love), ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" ("The Major Turns," "Final Words on the Subject") and the Glossary ("Whiff of Death"). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
All Is Lost is the low point at page 75. It is the inverse of the Midpoint: if the Midpoint was an "up," this is a "down," and vice versa. It is the beat most often described as the **"false defeat."** Everything looks black, but only for now (Snyder, ch. 4).

In the moment it feels like complete defeat (Snyder, ch. 4):
- every part of the hero's life lies in ruins;
- there is wreckage everywhere;
- there seems to be no hope.

## How it works

### The whiff of death
This is Snyder's "trade secret," added to every All Is Lost for extra flavor and found in many hit films (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Kill someone.** Great films often use this beat for a death. The best example is Obi-Wan's in *Star Wars*. All Is Lost is where mentors die, so that their pupils can find out the strength was theirs from the start; losing the teacher is what lets them prove it.
- **No mentor, no deaths?** It makes no difference. Snyder's advice is to put something involving death into the beat anyway. It can be central or symbolic: a houseplant, a pet fish, word that a favorite relative has passed away. He says it works every time.
- **Why it works.** Snyder likens it to the crucifixion, calling it the beat's "Christ on the cross" moment. The old world, the old self and the old way of thinking die here. That death clears the ground for the thesis (what was) and the antithesis (its upside-down version) to fuse into a **synthesis**, a new world and a new life. Whatever is shown dying, even a goldfish, echoes that and makes the moment more moving.
- ***Elf* as proof.** The film follows the beat sheet closely. At page 75, rejected by his real father and overwhelmed, the hero stops on a bridge and plainly considers suicide. Snyder says he nearly shouted "whiff of death" in the theater (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Check your genre films.** He tells writers to look for it in the films they've screened. He expects they'll find it, and says every good, primal story needs it (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **The Glossary entry.** Something dies here, literally or figuratively: the mentor, the sickly-looking ally, the family pet. The beat closes out the old order and opens the new one the hero will build out of this apparent ending (Snyder, Glossary).

### It flips the Midpoint
The halfway beat and this one are a matched, inverse pair. Whatever false state the Midpoint shows, All Is Lost shows its opposite (Snyder, ch. 4).
- In *What's Up, Doc?*, which has a "down" Midpoint, the hero actually wins the prize he wanted at All Is Lost.
- That victory is false and spoiled, as a gang of crooks storms the awards event and launches Act Three.

So in a story with a "down" Midpoint, this beat can look like a win that sours.

### It is the reverse of where the Midpoint stood
In Snyder's teen-comedy example, the heroes rule the school at the Midpoint. By All Is Lost the old mean clique has retaken its place and the heroes leave in shame; everything really is lost (Snyder, ch. 4).

### In a Buddy Love story
Here the low point is a split, a fight or an angry farewell. None of these is real. Underneath, the pair simply hate admitting they function worse apart, and each must drop their pride to win (Snyder, ch. 2).

### Building it on The Board
Once the Midpoint is set, All Is Lost is easy to find, because it is the Midpoint's flip. Ask what about the Midpoint's up or down can be reversed into its false opposite (Snyder, ch. 5). Landing these two beats firmly is one of the few things Snyder wants writers to remember while drafting (Snyder, ch. 5).

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):**
- **The setback.** Ordered off the case, the heroine refuses because she has a lead. Her boss gives her an ultimatum, quit or be fired, and she stays at the pageant.
- **Worse off than at the start.** Snyder calls this textbook: she is now in a worse position than when the film began.
- **The whiff of death.** What dies is her sense of self: stripped of her badge and role as an agent, she no longer knows who she is.
- **The parting gift.** Her mentor can't fix things, but he hands her one last weapon, a new dress.

## Placement
- Page 75 of a 110-page script (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 68%.
- On The Board it falls inside row 3 (pages 55-85), between that row's opening and its closing hinge. It is not one of the three row-end hinges (Snyder, ch. 5).
