# Debate (Save the Cat, beat 5)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the Debate section and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" (row 1 of The Board). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The Debate is the stretch between the Catalyst and the move into Act Two. In it, the hero weighs what the Catalyst demands (Snyder, ch. 4).

Snyder admits the section once puzzled him. If news arrives that the hero's sister has been kidnapped by pirates, the hero obviously knows what to do, so why stall until the act break? His answer is that this is the hero's last chance to say the idea is mad, and the audience needs to see the hero recognize that:
- Should I go?
- Do I dare?
- The outside world is dangerous, but is staying put any better?

## How it works
- **It must pose a question.** The moment of truth need not be clean or simple, but the Debate has to raise a question of some kind. The section then shows how the hero answers it (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **The question isn't always "should I?"** In *Legally Blonde*, the heroine settles at once on getting into Harvard Law to win back her boyfriend. The Debate's question is whether she *can* get in. The section shows her acing the entrance exam, making a risqué admissions video and being accepted. With the question answered, she can stride into Act Two (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Show how daunting the journey is.** Snyder's example is his own comedy in progress with Sheldon Bull, a road story (Snyder, ch. 4):
  - **The hero's bind:** a boy is expelled from military school and comes home to find his parents have moved away. He can neither go back nor stay, and has to decide whether to set off after them.
  - **What the Debate shows:** how frightening that trip would be.
  - **Comic treatment:** a friendly cab driver leaves him at the edge of town, facing an eerie road. His nerves are deflated when a passing motorist jeers at him. He sets off on a light note, his decision now firm.
- **End with a firm decision.** In both examples the section closes when the question is settled and the hero commits. That handoff sets up the Break into Two, which Snyder insists must be the hero's own choice (Snyder, ch. 4).

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** the Debate question is whether the heroine can pass as a pageant contestant. It's answered through comic scenes with a mentor who agrees to remake her.

## Placement
- Pages 12-25 of a 110-page script (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 11-23%.
- On The Board it fills the latter part of row 1 (Act One, pages 1-25), ending at the row's hinge, the Break into Two (Snyder, ch. 5).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** the Refusal of the Call. Supernatural Aid may also fall here, since a mentor's help often comes before the threshold ⚠️. In the *Miss Congeniality* example the mentor appears in exactly this section.
- **Bell:** the Argument Against Transformation.
- **Truby:** step 5, Desire, often firms up here as the hero settles on a goal ⚠️.
- **Weiland:** the Key Event, where the character becomes engaged with the conflict ⚠️.
- **Harmon:** the gap between step 2, Need, and step 3, Go ⚠️. His circle has no separate hesitation step.
