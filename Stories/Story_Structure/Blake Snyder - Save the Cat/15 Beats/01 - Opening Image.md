# Opening Image (Save the Cat, beat 1)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the Opening Image section and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" (+/- scenes as mini-movies), ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics" (Save the Cat), ch. 7 "What's Wrong with This Picture?" (Take a Step Back). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The Opening Image is the first thing the audience sees, and Snyder treats it as the film's first statement of what kind of film this is. In a single image, scene or short sequence it signals the tone, the mood, the type of story and its scale (Snyder, ch. 4).

It has a second job: it shows where the hero starts. Snyder calls it a "before" picture of the person, or group, the story will follow. Because he holds that a good screenplay is about change, this snapshot exists so it can be compared later with an "after" picture (Snyder, ch. 4).

Above all, he says, it should make the viewer settle in with the feeling that this is going to be good (Snyder, ch. 4).

## How it works
- **Set the tone and the stakes at once.** Snyder's examples all tell the viewer what they are in for before any plot happens (Snyder, ch. 4):
  - *Lawrence of Arabia*: the reckless motorcycle ride that ends in Lawrence's death sets a fatal, epic register.
  - *Citizen Kane*: the gated, looming castle hides a mysterious man and announces a mystery.
  - *Animal House*: the college founder's statue and its pompous motto announce a comic send-up.
- **Build it as a bookend.** The Opening Image has a partner beat, the Final Image. The two should be opposites, one a plus and one a minus, so that the distance between them records the emotional upheaval the film is about (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Make the change visible in the first and last ten pages.** Snyder warns that actors often read only the opening and closing pages of a script to see whether a striking change is there. If it is missing, the script may be thrown on the reject pile (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Introduce the hero when you can.** Very often this is where the main character first appears. Snyder's law of the "Save the Cat" scene, which asks the hero to do something likable when we first meet him, so we root for him, is tied to this first meeting (Snyder, Introduction; ch. 6). Its adjunct asks the writer to get the audience in sync with the hero's plight from the very start.
- **Start the hero as far back as possible.** The "Take a Step Back" rule says the hero's journey should begin at its emotional starting point, so the whole arc is shown. A hero who has already changed at the opening has nowhere to go (Snyder, ch. 7). The Opening Image is where that starting point is put on screen.
- **Study examples.** Snyder notes that once you have screened a dozen films like yours, you will be able to name several with memorable opening images; every good film has one (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **The same +/- logic runs at scene scale.** In ch. 5 Snyder asks every scene to swing from plus to minus or the reverse, "just like" the opening and final images of a film. The opening/final pairing is the large-scale model for that rule.

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** the film opens with the heroine as a playground tomboy, surrounded by boys and beating them up; in the present day she is still among men, now as an FBI agent.

## Placement
- Snyder's beat sheet (the BS2) puts it on page 1 of a 110-page script (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 1%.
- On The Board (Snyder, ch. 5), it is the first card of row 1, which covers Act One, pages 1-25.
