# Set-Up (Save the Cat, beat 3)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the Set-Up section and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" (row 1 of The Board), ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics" (the laws Save the Cat, Pope in the Pool, and Laying Pipe), ch. 7 "What's Wrong with This Picture?" (The Hero Leads), Introduction, and the Glossary ("First Reel," "Six Things That Need Fixing," "Callbacks," "Running Gags," "Thesis, Antithesis, Synthesis"). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The Set-Up is the first ten pages of the script, a dozen at most. Snyder treats it as the section that makes or breaks a reader's interest (Snyder, ch. 4). In the first reel, roughly the first ten minutes, it has to establish three things with energy:
- the hero;
- what is at stake;
- what the story's goal is.

It is also the opening stretch of what Snyder calls the **thesis**. He divides a film into three worlds instead of three acts: thesis, antithesis, synthesis. The Set-Up, and Act One as a whole, record the hero's pre-adventure world in full, a complete "before" picture (Snyder, ch. 4; Glossary, "Thesis, Antithesis, Synthesis").

## How it works

### Introduce the A-story cast
By page 10, the writer should have brought on, or at least pointed toward, each person who matters to the A story (Snyder, ch. 4). The Glossary's "First Reel" entry repeats the point. Silent-film reels ran about ten minutes, so "the first reel" still means the first ten minutes, and Snyder suggests spending it on bringing in the whole A-story cast. The same entry cites producer Joel Silver's advice to end each reel on a "whammy," a big action set piece.

### Plant what will pay off later
The Set-Up is where the writer plants:
- every character tic;
- every behavior that will need addressing later;
- why the hero must change before he can win, and in what way.

Snyder's thumbnails (Snyder, ch. 4):
- *Romancing the Stone*: a lonely writer living in a fantasy world.
- *Rain Man*: a slick, cold car importer.
- *Legally Blonde*: an apparently shallow airhead.

### Six Things That Need Fixing
Snyder coined the term and admits the number six is arbitrary (Snyder, ch. 4). It names a list of what is missing or wrong in the hero's life. The writer must *show* the list, not state it.

The Glossary widens the list to three kinds of item:
- the hero's small character flaws;
- the enemies and rivals who push him around;
- a wish list.

If we like the hero and think he deserves help, these get fixed later. How the list works:
- **Time bombs.** Snyder likens the items to time bombs: later in the script they go off, get turned upside down and get cured.
- **Callbacks.** They become running gags and callbacks, and those only land if the audience saw the original setup.
- **Example.** In *Big*, the ride's height requirement is the main setup. The boy also can't get the girl and has no privacy, and Act Two gives him all of these once he turns into an adult.
- **Building the list.** Snyder himself shuttles between the setup pages and the payoff pages, adding to the list as he goes. The more payoffs, the better (Snyder, Glossary).

### Callbacks and running gags
The Glossary separates the two:
- **Callbacks** are images, bits, traits or metaphors planted early and recalled later, often explaining what an obvious setup was for. His example is the flier in *Back to the Future* that later points the hero to the lightning strike he needs.
- **Running gags** are repeated bits or tics. The audience enjoys them more each time, and they should get a twist late in the film once the character is ready to change. His example is the coffee drinker who finally orders tea.

### Stasis = Death
The Set-Up world feels like the calm before a storm. Left alone, it would stay the same. But the reader should sense trouble coming, since a world that never changes is a dead one. Change has to arrive (Snyder, ch. 4). In the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown Snyder compresses this to "Stasis equals death" (Snyder, ch. 4).

### Meet the hero so we like him
The book's title law, Save the Cat, belongs to the moment we meet the hero. He should do something that wins our affection and makes us root for him (Snyder, Introduction; ch. 6). The adjunct rule asks the writer to get the audience in sync with the hero's plight from the start.
- **Introduction, *Sea of Love*:** the cop lets a father go in front of his son, a two-second act of kindness.
- **Ch. 6, *Aladdin*:** the thief gives his stolen food to hungry children.
- **Ch. 6, *Pulp Fiction*:** the hitmen are made funny and naive.
- **The fix for a flawed hero:** if the hero is damaged goods, make his antagonist worse (Snyder, ch. 6).

### State the goal
The first item on Snyder's "Hero Leads" checklist asks whether the opening pages make the hero's goal plain to the audience. It should be said aloud and restated in words and action throughout (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Don't drown it in exposition
- **Laying Pipe.** If the setup needs more than 25 pages, there is a problem, because audiences only tolerate so much "pipe" (Snyder, ch. 6):
  - *Minority Report* takes about 40 minutes to reach its hook.
  - *Along Came Polly* spends a long time getting to its central romance.
  - Snyder admits his own *Blank Check* ran about half a beat long in Act One.
- **The Pope in the Pool.** Exposition that must be given can be buried under something diverting to watch (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The Set-Up on The Board
Snyder budgets three or four cards, that is three or four scenes, for the first ten pages to reach the Catalyst (Snyder, ch. 5). He often starts with far more:
- **Backstory cards.** Many early cards are backstory, not scenes, such as a card noting the hero's musical hobby. They get folded into a single "Meet the Hero" scene.
- **Paring down.** He admits row 1 can swell to 20 cards before he pares it to about nine.

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** by page 10 we have met everyone in the A story:
- the colleague the heroine likes, who barely notices her;
- her boss;
- the macho FBI world where she fits in.

She is a scruffy loner with no social life but seems content: textbook Set-Up, with a storm on its way.

## Placement
- Pages 1-10 of a 110-page script, a dozen pages at most (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 1-9%.
- The thesis world (Act One) runs on to the Break into Two at page 25.
- On The Board it is the start of row 1, which covers pages 1-25 (Snyder, ch. 5).
