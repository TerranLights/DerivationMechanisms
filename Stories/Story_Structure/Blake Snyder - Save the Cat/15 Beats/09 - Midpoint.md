# Midpoint (Save the Cat, beat 9)

(Turn, Turn, Turn; the "Is It Broken?" Test) and the Glossary ("Stakes Are Raised," "Major Turns"). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

## What it is
A script has two halves, and the Midpoint is the threshold between them. Snyder rates it as important as the two act breaks, especially early on when laying out the beats (Snyder, ch. 4).

From reviewing hundreds of films, he finds the Midpoint takes one of two forms (Snyder, ch. 4):
- **An "up."** The hero seems to reach a peak. It is a false peak.
- **A "down."** The hero's world seems to cave in. The collapse is false too, and from here things can only get better.

Choosing which kind your script needs is, in his image, like driving a nail firmly into a wall: once it's in, the clothesline of the story can be hung securely.

## How it works

### How Snyder found it
In his early career he taped films on audiocassette to play while driving between Santa Barbara and Los Angeles. Each tape side ran 45 minutes, and a mountain pass sat exactly halfway along the drive. So he flipped each tape at the summit (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **The film:** *What's Up, Doc?* split cleanly into two halves.
- **Its Midpoint:** a "down." The first half ends with the hero's hotel room on fire. After a fade, he wakes the next day a broken man, to find the heroine, who caused the fire, waiting to help.

From then on, Snyder kept noticing how many films use the Midpoint to change their whole dynamic.

### Stakes are raised
"The stakes are raised at the midpoint" is a phrase heard often in script meetings, and Snyder says it is true. The Midpoint is where the fun and games end and the film goes back to the story (Snyder, ch. 4).
- The Glossary also calls this a "ticking clock" or "midpoint bump."
- **What it is:** a sudden new problem, bigger and less expected than anything before it and seemingly impossible to overcome.
- **What it does:** it gives the hero fresh challenges and leads toward the final win (Snyder, Glossary).

### False victory, false defeat
If the hero has, for instance, been granted some Out-of-the-Bottle magic, the Midpoint can hand him everything he thinks he wants. It's a false victory, because he hasn't yet learned the lesson he really needs (Snyder, ch. 4).

### The Midpoint-All Is Lost axis
The Midpoint's partner is the All Is Lost beat on page 75, usually labeled the "false defeat." The two are inverses (Snyder, ch. 4):
- Things are never as good as they look at the halfway point, nor as bad as they look at the low point, or the other way round.
- Whichever false state the Midpoint shows, All Is Lost shows the opposite.
- **Example:** *What's Up, Doc?* has a "down" Midpoint, so at All Is Lost the hero actually wins the prize he wanted. But that victory is tainted when a crowd of crooks descends on the ceremony, setting Act Three in motion.

Snyder invites readers to check the films they have screened in their genre; he claims this axis is in every one (Snyder, ch. 4).

### After the Midpoint
- **The "up" version.** The next section of Act Two starts from it: the opposition seems beaten and the hero's team seems in harmony, but the fight isn't over (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Turn, Turn, Turn.** The plot should spin and speed up as it rounds the Midpoint toward the finish. If you don't feel it intensifying there, something is wrong (Snyder, ch. 7). The "Is It Broken?" test asks the same thing: does the second half pick up speed and pressure once past the halfway mark? (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Build it first
When placing the major turns on The Board, Snyder starts with the Midpoint (Snyder, ch. 5):
- **Decide up or down.** Snyder treats this as the one choice that must be settled before anything else.
- **Why first.** Most writers can find the Break into Two, because the setup and early adventure are what they already picture. The Midpoint tells you where the story goes after that.
- **Then the rest.** All Is Lost follows as its flip, and then the Break into Three tends to come easily.
- **What to remember.** In his final words on The Board, landing these two beats firmly is one of the few things he wants writers to carry into the draft.

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** the fun stops when word of a fresh threat against the pageant arrives and the heroine's stakes rise. We've had the comic set pieces, met the suspects and watched the tomboy among the contestants; now the real trouble begins.
