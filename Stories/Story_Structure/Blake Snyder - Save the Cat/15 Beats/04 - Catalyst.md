# Catalyst (Save the Cat, beat 4)

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" (the Catalyst section and the *Miss Congeniality* breakdown); also ch. 5 "Building the Perfect Beast" (cards before the Catalyst) and ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics" (Laying Pipe). Cited below as (Snyder, ch. N).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
The Catalyst is the event that knocks over the world the Set-Up has just shown. The writer has spent the opening pages showing how things are; now something arrives and topples it (Snyder, ch. 4).

Snyder's stock forms are deliberately everyday (Snyder, ch. 4):
- a telegram or other message;
- being fired;
- discovering a spouse's affair;
- learning you have days to live;
- a knock at the door.

His film examples (Snyder, ch. 4):
- *Romancing the Stone*: a package arrives that sends the heroine to South America.
- *Rain Man*: a phone call brings news of the hero's father's death.
- *Legally Blonde*: at dinner, the heroine's boyfriend breaks up with her.

## How it works
- **It mirrors real life.** Snyder likes the beat because such moments happen to everyone, and life-changing events often arrive looking like bad news (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **It is not what it seems.** Like several beats on the sheet, the Catalyst is deceptive. It feels like the opposite of good news, yet once the adventure is over it proves to be the very thing that brought the hero to a happy outcome (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **It must be there.** Snyder misses it keenly when it's absent or weak, and ranks that lack next to his complaint about missing Save the Cat scenes (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **It is the first real event.** The Catalyst is the first point where something actually happens. If it's late or missing, the reader gets restless, and a studio reader's coverage will say the script has no plot (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Keep it on its page.** In Snyder's early drafts the Catalyst tends to drift to around page 20, because the setup is overlong and cluttered. His cure is to cut until the beat sits back on page 12. Trimming shows that the cut material was either redundant or not shown economically. This is why he values a structure map at all (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Few cards before it.** On The Board, Snyder allows three or four scene cards for the first ten pages to get to the Catalyst. Backstory cards get folded into a single "Meet the Hero" scene (Snyder, ch. 5).
- **Too much setup delays the hook.** The Laying Pipe law warns that when a premise needs heavy setup, the real starting event comes too late and tests the audience's patience. *Minority Report* takes about 40 minutes to reach its hook, where the detective learns he is the predicted killer (Snyder, ch. 6).
- **It leads straight into the Debate.** In *Legally Blonde*, the breakup quickly produces the heroine's plan to follow him to Harvard Law, which raises the Debate's question of whether she can get in (Snyder, ch. 4).

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** Snyder calls it a textbook call to adventure. A threat against the beauty pageant arrives, the pageant's organizers are introduced, and the FBI decides to plant a female agent as a contestant. A search of the available agents lands on the heroine.

## Placement
- Page 12 of a 110-page script (Snyder, ch. 4). Our conversion: about 11%.
- On The Board it falls in row 1 (Act One, pages 1-25), after the opening three or four cards (Snyder, ch. 5).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Truby:** step 4, Inciting event.
- **Campbell:** the Call to Adventure. Snyder himself calls the *Miss Congeniality* Catalyst a call to adventure (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Weiland:** the Inciting Event.
- **Bell:** the Disturbance ⚠️. The composite treats it as an alias, but Bell's Disturbance may come earlier, on the opening pages, rather than at about 10%.
- **Harmon:** the end of step 2, Need, leading into step 3, Go ⚠️.

## In the composite
Defined in the master as **Catalyst** (Beat-by-Beat Definitions; Summary Table placement ~10%), aliased to Bell's "Disturbance" and Campbell's "Call to Adventure." The master adds a craft rule that the Catalyst is the only event allowed to be random or coincidental. That rule is not in Snyder's 2005 book.
