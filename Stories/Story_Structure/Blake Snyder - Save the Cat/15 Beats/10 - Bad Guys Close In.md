# Bad Guys Close In (Save the Cat, beat 10)

## What it is
Bad Guys Close In runs from the halfway turn down to the low point at page 75. Snyder calls it the hardest part of a screenplay to write. He knows no technique for it other than forcing your way through, which is where a writer's stubbornness pays off (Snyder, ch. 4).

The name describes the hero's position after an "up" Midpoint (Snyder, ch. 4):
- **The surface:** everything looks fine. The opposition seems beaten for now and the hero's team is working in harmony. The bad guys may be people, a natural force or a thing.
- **The turn:** the bad guys regroup and come back harder.
- **The team:** dissent, doubt and jealousy start to break the hero's team apart from the inside.

## How it works

### Pressure from two directions
In the classic version, the forces against the hero, both external and internal, tighten their hold. The enemy won't quit, and the hero has nowhere to turn for help. He is alone, has to endure, and is heading for a big fall, which arrives at All Is Lost (Snyder, ch. 4).

### Snyder's own struggle with it
He calls this the weakest part of his *Blank Check* (Snyder, ch. 4). A teen comedy he co-wrote shows how he eventually solved it:
- **The setup.** Four underdog girls take on their school's dominant mean clique and, by the Midpoint, have beaten them and taken over the top spot. He and his partner were stuck on what came next.
- **The solution, by returning to basics.**
  - The defeated clique regroups, which they even wrote as its own comic scene.
  - The heroes fall out among themselves: success goes to their heads, each claims the credit, and rivalry over who is most popular splits them.
- **The result.** By All Is Lost the Midpoint has flipped: the old clique is back on top and the heroes leave in disgrace.

He says that simple pattern took weeks to find, and only looks obvious afterward.

### It is a known trouble spot on The Board
- **Leaving it light.** While paring the Board down to 40 cards, Snyder lets himself off lightly on this weak area. He may even leave gaps and hope the draft solves them, though he knows they must be dealt with eventually (Snyder, ch. 5).
- **Calling for help.** His comic aside about calling his story mentor for help is, specifically, about not knowing what happens in this section (Snyder, ch. 5).

### Escalate, don't drift
- **Turn, Turn, Turn.** As the antagonist's grip tightens, events should come faster, building pressure that bursts out in the Act Three climax (Snyder, ch. 7). The plot shouldn't just move forward but accelerate and keep revealing more:
  - the heroes' flaws, betrayals, doubts and fears, and the threats against them;
  - the villains' concealed strengths, reserves and darker motives, unknown to the hero.
- **Make the Bad Guy Badder.** A hero who does everything right can still seem small if the opposition is weak (Snyder, ch. 7):
  - **Match them.** Hero and villain should be close to equal, with the edge to the villain, who will go to any lengths.
  - **Mirror them.** They are often two halves of one persona.
  - **Genre echo.** The Dude with a Problem genre makes the same point: the worse the villain, the greater the heroism (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **Watch Out for That Glacier!** Opposition that "closes in" from far away and too slowly drains tension (Snyder, ch. 6):
  - **The rule.** Danger has to be immediate; the stakes have to fall on people we care about; and the consequences should be shown early.
  - **Glacier films.** *Dante's Peak* (a volcano that just waits to erupt), *Outbreak* (a virus creeping slowly closer) and *Open Range* (heroes who talk for most of the film about going after the villains).

**Example breakdown (Snyder, ch. 4, *Miss Congeniality*):** the heroine's doubts about her femininity grow and her friction with her mentor deepens. Meanwhile the real villains move closer, unseen, behind the scenes of the pageant. No one has been killed yet, but there is a list of suspects.
