# Chapter 1: What Is It?

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 1 "What Is It?" The local text has no page numbers; cited by chapter title.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## 1. The Saturday-night test: everyone is pitching
Snyder opens with an ordinary scene. A group of friends picks a film, and one of them reads the choices aloud from the newspaper. That reader is doing exactly what a professional pitcher does. Stars, effects and good reviews do not help if the reader cannot say what the film is *about*. When the poster and title do not make it clear, the reader ends up listing things the film is not, or repeating gossip, and the friends move on to another title (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

His conclusion: the question "What is it?" is the whole game. A clear answer is the industry's currency (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 2. Monday morning in Hollywood
The same question drives the business side. Producers pitching to executives, agents calling about a client's script, and marketing teams designing a poster all have to answer it. A project that cannot answer it is finished (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

### Why it is getting harder
The audience's attention is contested by film, television, radio, the internet, music, hundreds of cable channels, magazines and sports. Snyder estimates that even a keen filmgoer spends about half a minute choosing what to see on a weekend. A writer has to cut through all of that, including for people who are not keen at all (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 3. Pre-sold franchises
Studios reduce the risk by making sequels and remakes of properties the audience already knows, which Snyder calls pre-sold franchises. Because people half-know the film already, the "What is it?" problem shrinks. His examples are adaptations of a TV show (*Starsky and Hutch*), a comic (*The Hulk*) and a video game (*Resident Evil*), plus sequels such as *Shrek 2*, *Spider-Man 2*, *Mission: Impossible 3* and *Ocean's Twelve*. He does not call this creative bankruptcy. He thinks decision-makers simply doubt that audiences want something new, and admits they may be right: if a film cannot say what it is, why would anyone gamble on it (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?")?

### The spec writer's answer
Spec writers do not own franchises. Snyder's way around this is to set aside the script, the favorite scenes, the soundtrack and the dream cast, and to work on one sentence. A better, faster, more inventive answer to "What is it?" keeps a listener interested. Doing this before writing also improves the story itself (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 4. "The logline from hell": why he refuses to hear the story first
When writers launch into their plot, Snyder stops them and asks for the one-line. He finds writers often leave it until last, absorbed in scenes and clever motifs, and then cannot state the core of their film in under ten minutes. He treats that as proof the idea has not been thought through. A spec writer has to consider everyone down the chain (agent, producer, studio head, audience), and the writer will not be in the room to "set the mood" for any of them. So exciting strangers is the first job. If the pitch does not grab him, he does not want the story (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

### What a good logline does
The industry calls it a logline or one-line. Snyder's test of a good one is envy: when he reads a sold logline in the trades and wishes he had thought of it. He quotes three recent family-comedy spec sales (*4 Christmases*, *The Retreat*, *Ride Along*) and adds a joke rule that anything "from hell" helps a comedy. Besides answering "What is it?", he says, each one has the same four components (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 5. The four components of a logline

### 5.1 Irony (the most important)
Credited to his former partner Colby Carr: irony is what a logline needs above everything else, whether the film is a comedy or a drama. His examples are the loglines of *Die Hard* (a cop visiting his estranged wife finds her building seized by terrorists) and *Pretty Woman* (a businessman falls for the prostitute he hires as a weekend date). Irony is what writers call the **hook**, because it catches interest. In the spec examples, a family holiday turns cynical, and a new hire's welcome becomes a threat on his life. A logline should be emotionally intriguing, "like an itch you have to scratch" (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

He likens the logline to a book jacket: a good one makes you reach for the book at once. **Warning:** if you cannot find the irony when you try to write the logline, the problem may be the story, not the sentence. Rethink the story. Demanding irony works as a diagnostic for what is missing (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

### 5.2 A compelling mental picture
The second element: a whole film should be visible in the line. Like Proust's madeleine, it expands in the listener's mind and promises more. His example is producer David Permut's pitch for *Blind Date*, a perfect woman who changes once she drinks. Snyder can picture the date, the disaster and the man trying to save it (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").
- **Time frame:** the three spec loglines each mark out when the story happens (one Christmas Day, one retreat weekend, one night, the way *After Hours* does). You can see where the film starts and ends.
- **Built-in conflict:** *Ride Along* sets opposites against each other over a shared goal. This explains the appeal of fish-out-of-water premises: you can foresee the clash when someone is thrown into an unfamiliar world.
- **Test:** does the set-up make the listener's imagination race ahead? If not, there is no logline yet, and possibly no film (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

### 5.3 Audience and cost
A good logline also signals who the film is for and roughly what it will cost, which matters to studio buyers. Snyder reads *4 Christmases* this way: it chases the *Meet the Parents* / *Meet the Fockers* crowd; it is a medium-budget **four-quadrant** picture aimed at everyone; young leads bring in the core audience while famous older actors in the parents' roles draw older viewers. It is also cheap. He calls it a **block comedy**, one that stays in a neighborhood with few location moves. An executive who wants a mid-budget, broad-audience Christmas film can see all of that from the line (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

### 5.4 A killer title
The fourth element: the logline includes the title, and the two work as a combination punch. The title needs irony too, and must tell the tale (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").
- **Best example:** *Legally Blonde*. It captures the whole concept while avoiding the trap of being so literal it sounds silly. He lists weaker alternatives it could have had.
- **Worst example:** *For Love or Money*, used for at least four films; he cannot remember what happens in a single one. A generic title kills interest.
- **The rule:** the title must work as the headline of the story. In his words, "It says what it is!" (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?"). *4 Christmases* passes; a vague alternative such as *Yuletide* would signal Christmas but not this particular Christmas story. He calls this the **Say What It Is Test**.
- **Title-first development:** he sometimes starts from the title. *Nuclear Family* began as a pun ("nuclear" as radioactive rather than as parents and children). That gave the ironic logline: a troubled family camps on a nuclear dump and wakes with superpowers. Written with Jim Haggin, it sold to Steven Spielberg in a bidding war for $1 million and met all four criteria (four-quadrant, effects rather than stars) (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 6. The bullheaded writer and why to slow down
Snyder says all good screenwriters are "bullheads," torn between grand self-belief and deep insecurity. Once they can see their film, they charge ahead regardless of advice. He asks writers to stop before typing FADE IN: and give long thought to the logline, title and poster, then test-market them (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 7. Test marketing

### The case study
He describes an online student with a promising but vague idea. Snyder sent him back to "Page One," meaning a rewrite from nearly scratch. The writer put away his scenes and motifs and drafted loglines, which Snyder admits is a miserable chore. To meet the criteria (irony, a sense of budget and audience, a clear promise, a strong title), he had to bend the logline away from his original notion. Once he let go of that notion (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?"):
1. the logline changed;
2. listeners responded better;
3. the story changed to fit the logline, and got better;
4. the irony and conflicts came into focus, the characters became distinct, and the actual writing became easier.

Doing this on paper, before any money was spent, saved everyone downstream the cost of fixing the concept in post-production.

### Pitching to strangers
Snyder pitches anywhere, including coffee-shop lines. His two reasons (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?"):
- He does not fear theft. Being afraid of it, he says, marks an amateur.
- Talking to someone face to face teaches you more about your film than having them read it.

His method is to pitch to "civilians" and watch their eyes. The moment they glance away, the pitch has a problem, and he fixes that spot before the next listener. He acts out a short scene of approaching a stranger in a café. He uses this with all ages, and especially with the film's target audience. A listener who would rather be elsewhere is the ideal test: if you can catch and hold that person, and he wants to know more, the idea is good (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

**Warning:** a personal story that means everything to you (his example is a summer-camp memory) may mean nothing to a stranger. Strangers, not friends, buy most of the tickets, so they are the ones to impress (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 8. The "death" of high concept
**High concept** is a term many in Hollywood dislike. Jeffrey Katzenberg and Michael Eisner popularized it at Disney to mean what this chapter has described: making the film easy to "see." Their hits could be grasped from the one-sheet (the poster) alone; Snyder's examples are *Ruthless People*, *Outrageous Fortune* and *Down and Out in Beverly Hills*. The term has gone out of fashion and its death has been announced often (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

Snyder's view:
- Thinking in high concept is **good manners**: it puts you in the shoes of the paying customer, who also pays for parking and a babysitter.
- It is not new. Preston Sturges's 1940s hits (*Christmas in July*, *Hail the Conquering Hero*, *The Lady Eve*, *Sullivan's Travels*) and Hitchcock's thrillers (*Rear Window*, *North by Northwest*, *Vertigo*, *Psycho*) drew audiences on logline and poster, and their titles say what they are without being clumsy (he allows that *Psycho* is borderline).
- A clear, creative answer to "What is it?" never goes out of style. He challenges anyone who calls this salesmanship to beat the title *Legally Blonde* (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## 9. Chapter summary points not covered above
- The screenwriter's job, especially on spec, includes thinking about every reader in the chain, from agent to the executive who greenlights.
- Along with a clear "What is it?", a film needs a clear sense of what it is about and who it is for. Its tone, its potential, the characters' dilemma and the kind of characters they are should all be easy to grasp and compelling.
- The logline is defined as a grabber of one or two sentences. The four elements are restated: irony that is emotionally involving; a mental picture that implies a whole film, often with a time frame; a signal of tone, target audience and cost so buyers can judge profit; and a clever title that says what it is.
- **International market:** high concept matters more than before because films must sell abroad. Snyder says the U.S. home market once supplied about 60% of a film's profit and now accounts for about 40% ⚠️ (his figure, unverified), so more than half the market is outside the U.S.
- Road-test ideas by pitching them, and adjust to what a blank-faced stranger tells you (Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?").

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 1 "What Is It?"; described, not copied)
1. **Pitch the listings:** read this week's films from the newspaper to a friend, and think about how each logline or poster could be improved.
2. **Your own loglines:** write loglines for any scripts you have and pitch them to a stranger. Notice whether the logline shifts, whether it suggests things the script should have tried, and whether the story has to bend to match the pitch.
3. **TV Guide loglines:** read the film loglines in a TV listings guide. Check whether title and logline say what the film is, and whether vague ones line up with films that failed.
4. **Idea-generating games** for writers without an idea yet. Each comes with a sample logline in the book:
   - *Funny [title]:* turn a serious genre film (drama, thriller, horror) into a comedy.
   - *Serious [title]:* recast a comedy as a drama.
   - *FBI out of water:* list five settings where no screen FBI agent has yet been posted on a case.
   - *[blank] School:* list five unusual schools, camps or classes.
   - *Versus:* pair people on opposite sides of a heated issue.
   - *My [blank] Is a Serial Killer:* name an unlikely person, animal or thing a paranoid hero might suspect of murder.

He closes by inviting readers to email him a strong family-comedy logline.

## Terms introduced
- **"What is it?":** the question every film must answer quickly. The book's starting point for concept work.
- **Logline / one-line:** a one- or two-sentence description that says what the film is.
- **Hook:** the ironic, attention-catching core of a logline or poster.
- **Irony (in a logline):** an unexpected, emotionally intriguing twist in the situation. The first of the four logline elements.
- **Compelling mental picture:** a logline that lets you see a whole film, often with a time frame.
- **Promise of more:** the sense that the logline implies more fun and story than it states.
- **Audience and cost:** the logline's built-in signal of target audience, tone and budget.
- **Four-quadrant picture:** a film that appeals across age and gender groups. (Defined further in the Glossary.)
- **Block comedy:** a low-cost comedy set in one neighborhood with few company moves.
- **Killer title / one-two punch:** the title and logline working together.
- **Say What It Is Test:** a title must state what the film is.
- **Bullhead:** Snyder's affectionate label for the stubborn, self-certain screenwriter.
- **Test marketing / road-testing:** pitching an idea to strangers and adjusting to their reactions.
- **Civilians:** people outside the industry, used as test listeners.
- **Page One:** a near-total rewrite.
- **High concept:** an idea easy to grasp from title, logline and poster.
- **One-sheet:** the film poster.
- **Pre-sold franchise:** a property with a built-in audience (book, comic, TV show, game, earlier film).
