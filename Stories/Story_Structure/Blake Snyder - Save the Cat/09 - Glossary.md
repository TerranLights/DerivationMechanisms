# Glossary

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), back matter: Glossary (subtitled as a list of terms heard in Los Angeles's 310 area code). The local text has no page numbers; cited below as (Snyder, Glossary).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

> The Glossary is not a numbered chapter, but several of its entries add rules or examples found nowhere else in the book (booster rocket, callbacks vs. running gags, set piece, first reel, four-quadrant, stakes are raised). Entries are given in the source's order. "New here" marks material not found in the chapters; otherwise a pointer gives the chapter file.

## Story and craft terms

### Arc
The change a character goes through from beginning to middle to end of a script. Snyder notes it is a favorite development-meeting word and jokes about his patience for it (Snyder, Glossary). → the ch. 6 file (`06 - …Physics.md`) (Covenant of the Arc).

### Black holes
Gaps in a beat sheet, step outline or Board where you cannot see what story belongs. He jokes that staring at them makes writers regret not choosing law school or the army (Snyder, Glossary). → `05 - Building the Perfect Beast.md`. *New here:* the term covers beat sheets and step outlines, not just The Board.

### The Board
A corkboard, chalkboard or sketchbook divided into four equal sections: Act One, Act Two's first and second halves, and Act Three. It serves as a workspace for trying ideas with cards, pins and colored pens, then winnowing them to about 40 scenes (Snyder, Glossary). → `05 - Building the Perfect Beast.md`.

### Breaking the fourth wall
Anything that reminds viewers they are watching a film. The fourth wall is the unseen side of a stage through which the audience watches. Breaking it lets the characters, in effect, look back, which pulls viewers out of the story. *New here:* it sometimes works (Woody Allen talking to the camera in *Annie Hall*) but usually does not (a star visibly breaking character, which he says happens in *Aladdin*) (Snyder, Glossary). → the ch. 6 file (`06 - …Physics.md`) (Keep the Press Out).

### Booster rocket
*New here (fullest treatment).* Every script has stretches that threaten to drag, typically just after big moments such as the Act One break, and where the action runs low near the end of Act Two. A booster rocket is something inserted to carry the story past them, often a vivid new character (Snyder, Glossary).
- *Home Alone*: the mother's long trip home starts to drag late in Act Two, and John Candy's polka-band leader arrives just in time.
- *Legally Blonde*: the manicurist appears right after Act Two begins, as the law-school story risks growing tiresome.
→ `15 Beats/07 - B Story.md` (ch. 4 calls the B story's start a booster rocket).

### Callbacks
*New here.* Bits, images, character traits or metaphors planted in Act One and recalled later. A callback often reveals what an obvious set-up was for. In *Back to the Future*, a handbill the hero's girlfriend writes a note on later reminds him of the lightning strike on the town clock that he needs to get home. Other callbacks are less about plot: they recall the past to show how a character has changed, or revive a joke by reminding us where it started (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/03 - Set-Up.md` and `15 Beats/14 - Finale.md`.

### Exposition
Necessary facts (plot details, heist plans, backstory) that cannot just be laid out but must be told entertainingly. To "bury" exposition is to deliver it without dullness, so it slides down like a breakfast-cereal spoonful (Snyder, Glossary). → the ch. 6 file (`06 - …Physics.md`) (Pope in the Pool).

### First reel
*New here.* In the silent era a reel ran about ten minutes, so reel one ended at the ten-minute mark. Producer Joel Silver advises closing every reel with a "whammy," a large action set piece. "First reel" still means a film's first ten minutes, and Snyder recommends that those minutes bring on **every A-story character** (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/03 - Set-Up.md`.

### Genre (conventional sense)
*New here.* Below the broad split of comedy and drama, the familiar subgroups: for comedy, family, romantic, spoof or teen; for drama, action, romance, thriller or horror. Each carries its own conventions, lineage and audience expectations. Mixing genres has become fashionable (he cites a Gothic Western), but Snyder recommends **one genre per film**; more, and he no longer knows what the film is or why to see it (Snyder, Glossary). Note ⚠️: this entry uses "genre" in the ordinary trade sense, not for his ten story types in ch. 2. → the ch. 2 file (`02 - …Only Different.md`).

### High concept
Snyder admits nobody can define the term exactly or say where it came from, but everyone recognizes it. His pairs are *Die Hard* (high concept) against *The English Patient* (not), and *Miss Congeniality* against *Under the Tuscan Sun*. He jokes that the split roughly follows American versus European filmmaking. His advice: make your first script as high-concept as possible (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### Hook
The distilled appeal of a film, on its poster or in its logline, that makes people want to rush to see it, and makes other writers wish they had thought of it. Like Proust's madeleine, it opens up in the mind; it is an easy-to-see image that promises enjoyment and hints at what the story could become. It works on every listener, from agent to ticket buyer, and it answers "What is it?" (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### Inactive hero
The hero who just lies there and won't get up to answer the door. Since being proactive is what defines a hero, an inactive one is a failure. Heroes seek and strive; they do not wait for the phone (Snyder, Glossary). → `07 - What's Wrong with This Picture.md` (The Hero Leads).

### Logline / one-line
A one- or two-sentence description that says what the film is. It must name the hero as a kind of person with a describing adjective, the antagonist the same way, and the hero's primal goal. It needs irony and must expand with potential in the listener's mind. A good logline is currency in Hollywood (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`, `03 - It's About a Guy Who.md`.

### Major turns
The three structural turns, which sit at the ends of the Board's first three rows and deserve the closest care. A pitch hangs the story on them, and executives may remember one. Nail them before pitching or beating out a script (Snyder, Glossary). Note ⚠️: the entry names the first turn as the break into "Act One"; from context it means the break into Act Two. → `05 - Building the Perfect Beast.md`.

### On the nose
An executive's tactful phrase for an idea that is predictable, not funny, or familiar. Snyder suggests treating it as the thing to aim away from (Snyder, Glossary).

### Primal
The quality of a story, goal or premise that ties it to basic human drives. Tales of survival, sex, hunger or vengeance seize attention whether we like it or not. For the writer, every action and story must be grounded in primal motives; characters not driven primally strain the audience's patience. The test is whether it matters to a caveman, and the answer must be yes (Snyder, Glossary). → `03 - It's About a Guy Who.md`, `07 - What's Wrong with This Picture.md`.

### Promise of the premise
A premise proves itself only once the audience watches it play out. Whatever made the poster appealing must be delivered inside the theater, or viewers feel cheated. This beat's content is the scenes that exploit the premise to the full, usually found in Fun and Games (pages 30–55). It is where we fully understand what the film is about, and why we bought a ticket (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/08 - Fun and Games.md`.

### Running gags
*New here.* Distinct from callbacks, which recall Act One moments later: running gags are themes, tics or bits repeated throughout the film. Audiences enjoy them more each time, because remembering makes them feel smart and part of the story. His example is a character who always orders coffee. **Rule:** a running gag must get a twist late in the film, when the character, now ready to change, orders tea instead (Snyder, Glossary).

### Set piece
*New here.* A self-contained action scene or sequence that does less to advance the plot or reveal character than to exploit the situation or premise. Because of that, set pieces are **expendable and swappable**: a freeway chase could happen in a supermarket or at a racetrack, and can be cut or moved to suit the budget, the director or the star (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/08 - Fun and Games.md` (where ch. 4 puts them).

### Six Things That Need Fixing
Snyder's own term. It covers three kinds of item (Snyder, Glossary):
- the hero's minor character flaws;
- the enemies and rivals who bully him;
- a wish list.

All of them are repaired by the end, if we like the hero and think he deserves help. *New here:* he moves back and forth between the Act One set-up and the later payoffs while writing, adding to the list as he goes. Audiences enjoy these payoffs, and the more the better, but each flaw must be planted early for its payoff to work. → `15 Beats/03 - Set-Up.md`.

### Stakes are raised
*New here (aliases).* A development-meeting phrase, also known as the **ticking clock** and the **midpoint bump**: a jump in tension. At the midpoint, something new appears, bigger and more unexpected than anything so far, and apparently insurmountable. Raise the stakes there so the hero faces new challenges on the way to his final win (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/09 - Midpoint.md`.

### Structure
After concept, the most important quality of a script. Producers may love an idea and the writing and still reject a script whose structure is a mess, because they cannot see how the film is organized, and so cannot tell what it is. Structure is a key selling point, the easiest skill to learn, and part of the shared language of development meetings, so writers must be fluent in it (Snyder, Glossary). → `04 - Let's Beat It Out.md`.

### Subtext
The real meaning beneath a scene's surface. His example: a divorcing couple arguing over which apples to buy is really about their failing marriage. Don't spell out what is really going on; what characters avoid saying is what makes the moment rich (Snyder, Glossary). → `07 - What's Wrong with This Picture.md`.

### Thematic premise
Every good film, even a silly monster movie, must be about something. In essence a good film is a debate on the merits and drawbacks of a point of view: a question the film raises and answers. That question belongs up front, often put to the hero by a minor character, around page 5, and the film then proves it one way or the other (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/02 - Theme Stated.md`.

### Thesis, antithesis, synthesis (Acts One, Two, Three)
The three stages of the hero's thematic journey. Act One sets up the hero's world; Act Two turns it upside down; by mastering that strange new world the hero learns enough to combine the old and its opposite into a synthesis, in Act Three. Surviving the journey is not enough: the hero must **transform his world** to be truly great (Snyder, Glossary). → `04 - Let's Beat It Out.md`.

### Whiff of death
The bonus element at page 75, the All Is Lost beat: something dies, literally or metaphorically. It is where mentors and sickly allies die, and where, in Snyder's joke, the family dog called Spot gets removed. It fits there because that point marks the end of the old world and the start of the new one the hero will create (Snyder, Glossary). → `15 Beats/11 - All Is Lost.md`.

### +/- (emotional change)
The symbol for a scene's emotional change. *New here:* Snyder says he first heard of it from Robert McKee, who holds that every scene should swing from one emotional extreme to the other, and Snyder agrees. Think of every scene as a small film with its own before and after; deciding the emotional shift is the key to making a scene work. He marks every Board card with it (Snyder, Glossary). → `05 - Building the Perfect Beast.md`.

### >< (conflict)
The symbol for each scene's conflict: at the outset, who wants what, who blocks it, and who prevails. Don't begin a scene until you know the players and what they want (Snyder, Glossary). → `05 - Building the Perfect Beast.md`.

## Business terms

### "At the end of the day"
This phrase is an agent's or manager's lead-in to bad news (for example, that a star won't do your project after all). Snyder jokes it is also the time of day such calls come (Snyder, Glossary).

### Block comedy
*New here (origin).* A low-budget family film set close to home, with so few location moves it can be shot on a studio backlot (*The 'Burbs*). Snyder first heard it from a Disney executive discussing his script *Poker Night*, who wanted more such films; it may have been that executive's coinage, and Snyder has adopted it (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### Credit jumper
*New here (fullest treatment).* Once your contracted rewrite is done, you are let go, and later drafts show trivial changes (a character's name, the make of his car) made by another writer seeking credit. Writers Guild arbitration decides who wrote what. Being the original spec writer gives you more standing than the later writer, but it is up to you to show why the script is still yours (Snyder, Glossary). → `04 - Let's Beat It Out.md`.

### Four-quadrant picture
*New here (definition).* A film that draws all four audience quadrants: men and women, each split at age 25 into older and younger. Drawing all four practically guarantees a hit. Films target different groups for different reasons. At the time of writing, young men were the most sought-after group, because they go to films with or without dates and bring others along; they are the leading indicator of who goes. He allows that this could shift, but says it accounts for what the multiplex shows (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### In play
Said of talent so in demand that their search for new representation excites the town. For a screenwriter, he jokes, being "in play" just means being available (Snyder, Glossary).

### One-sheet
The old term for the poster, from its printing size. It displays the cast, the title and the film's tone. A good one is valuable and also sells home video later (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### Page One
An executive's despairing verdict on a script whose idea is its only asset: it needs a "page one rewrite." He compares it to a body shop declaring a car totaled (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### Pre-sold franchise
A property (book, comic, cartoon, old TV series) with built-in fans, presumed to turn out for the film. *New here:* it does not always work (he cites two big-budget adaptations that flopped), but any pre-aware audience is a head start, and even a small fan base, like the comic behind *Men in Black*, can build buzz. Spec writers rarely own one, which should not stop them from **creating their own franchises**, as large as possible (Snyder, Glossary). → `01 - What Is It.md`.

### Residuals
Quarterly payments to writers whose films are made, tracked by the Writers Guild for every TV airing, video sale and foreign release. Snyder says he has earned more than $100,000 from two films (Snyder, Glossary).

### Tracking
*New here (detail).* Development executives follow hot scripts before and as they go out, using an inter-studio network to judge whether a script is worth pursuing based on concept, the writer's record and word of mouth. Some spread misinformation to mislead rivals, which can backfire. Snyder names tracking as one of the mainly economic reasons the spec-sale frenzy ended, since it reduces the risk of any studio getting burned (Snyder, Glossary). → `08 - Final Fade In.md`.

## Terms introduced
The Glossary is itself the term list. Terms that appear **only** here, or are first defined here:
- **Booster rocket** (fullest form): a lift past a slow stretch, often a new character.
- **Callbacks vs. running gags:** a set-up recalled later, versus a bit repeated throughout and twisted at the end.
- **First reel / whammy:** the first ten minutes (introduce every A-story character); a big set piece to close each reel.
- **Set piece:** a self-contained, interchangeable action sequence.
- **Ticking clock / midpoint bump:** aliases for the stakes rising at the midpoint.
- **Four quadrants:** men and women, over and under 25.
- **In play, on the nose, residuals, tracking,** and the bad-news phrase about "the end of the day": trade terms.
- **One genre per film:** his rule against genre mixing (conventional sense of "genre").
