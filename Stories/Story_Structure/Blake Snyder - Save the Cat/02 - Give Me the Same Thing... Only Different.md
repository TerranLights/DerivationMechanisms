# Chapter 2: Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different!

## 1. The cliché problem and the twist
Snyder says a screenwriter's daily problem is cliché. You may approach a cliché, circle it, and come close to using it, but at the last moment you must swerve away and **give it a twist**. Refusing the inner voice that says nobody will notice is a struggle every storyteller has always faced (Snyder, ch. 2).

The chapter's title comes from a studio executive who blurted out this rule in a development meeting, in the manner of a Sam Goldwyn malapropism: give me the same thing, only different (Snyder, ch. 2).

**Scope:** the fresh spin applies to everything: the idea, the way characters talk, the scenes themselves (Snyder, ch. 2).

## 2. You must know the tradition you are pushing forward
To avoid a cliché, you have to know which tradition your work belongs to. That means knowing hundreds of films, especially the ones your film resembles. Snyder is dismayed at how many aspiring writers cannot discuss films in their own genre, while top directors such as Spielberg and Scorsese can. By "quote" he does not mean reciting lines; he means explaining how each film works (Snyder, ch. 2).

### Films as machines
He calls films precisely built "emotion machines," like Swiss watches. A writer should be able to take one apart and reassemble it without looking. Knowing a few favorites, or only the last five years of releases, is not enough. You have to trace the lineage: which film descended from which, and how each advanced the form (Snyder, ch. 2).

## 3. Your film belongs to a category, like it or not
Writers often protest that their idea is new and cannot be categorized. Snyder's reply is that it is already too late: every idea resembles one or many films in the canon, and its category has rules. To break the clichés and still offer a fresh version of the familiar, you must know your genre and know how to twist its stock elements. That improves your odds of selling, and he notes that everyone in Hollywood already works this way (Snyder, ch. 2).

## 4. "What is it... most like?"
After the logline is polished and tested, Snyder still holds the writer back from writing. The next question after "What is it?" is what the film is *most like*. In the Saturday-night scenario from ch. 1, once friends pick a comedy, they want to know which kind (Snyder, ch. 2).

### The "X meets Y" pitch (and why he dislikes it)
This question produces the mocked Hollywood pitch that names one hit crossed with another, or a hit relocated to an odd setting. He finds the two-film hybrids especially annoying, because the listener cannot work out what the combination means; usually the pitcher is just borrowing two successes and hoping something appeals. A rule in passing: nobody ever builds these comparisons out of flops (Snyder, ch. 2). He admits he uses the shorthand himself anyway.

### Why categorizing helps the writer
The real reason to categorize is for the writer's own benefit: to know what type of film you are writing. Not knowing is, in his view, the most common way to get lost mid-draft. Professionals routinely reference other films for clues to plot and character within their genre. When stuck, or before starting, you should "screen" around a dozen films like yours to learn why certain plot elements matter, why they work or fail, and where a cliché can be made fresh (Snyder, ch. 2).

## 5. The ten story types

### What they are for
Snyder offers ten genres as a place to start, not an end point. He compares the sorting to a game of gin rummy. He wanted categories that new films could be added to every year, and he believes nearly every film ever made fits one of them. Readers may invent their own or add to the list, but he hopes they won't need to (Snyder, ch. 2).

**Deliberate choice:** he leaves out familiar labels (romantic comedy, epic, biopic) since they tell him nothing about the *story*, which is the thing he needs (Snyder, ch. 2).

He expects the list to surprise: *Schindler's List* and *Die Hard* share a category, and buddy films are really love stories in disguise. He welcomes the confusion as a reason to dig in (Snyder, ch. 2).

### The ten, in the book's order (summaries; full files in `10 Genres/`)
1. **Monster in the House.** A monster, a confined "house," and people trying to survive it, usually after a sin (often greed) summons it. Primal: don't get eaten. → `10 Genres/01 - Monster in the House.md`
2. **Golden Fleece.** The quest or road story, including heists. The hero sets out for one thing and finds himself, and the episodes on the way must change him. → `10 Genres/02 - Golden Fleece.md`
3. **Out of the Bottle.** Wish fulfillment, or its flip side, the comeuppance curse. Magic or luck changes a deserving (or deserving-to-be-taught) hero's life, and a lesson follows. → `10 Genres/03 - Out of the Bottle.md`
4. **Dude with a Problem.** An ordinary person in extraordinary circumstances. The bigger and badder the problem, the greater the heroics. → `10 Genres/04 - Dude with a Problem.md`
5. **Rites of Passage.** Life transitions (puberty, midlife, addiction, grief, breakups). The "monster" is life itself, the hero is the last to understand it, and the end is acceptance. → `10 Genres/05 - Rites of Passage.md`
6. **Buddy Love.** Buddy films and all love stories. Two incomplete halves who first dislike each other, need each other, separate, and surrender their egos to win. Includes a "catalyst" subset where one partner changes the other and leaves. → `10 Genres/06 - Buddy Love.md`
7. **Whydunit.** Investigation stories where *why* matters more than *who*. The audience is the real detective and discovers something dark about human nature. → `10 Genres/07 - Whydunit.md`
8. **The Fool Triumphant.** An underestimated "idiot" set against a powerful establishment, often with an insider accomplice who takes the comic punishment. → `10 Genres/08 - The Fool Triumphant.md`
9. **Institutionalized.** Stories about groups and "families." They honor the group and expose the cost of losing yourself in it, often through a newcomer and a breakout character. → `10 Genres/09 - Institutionalized.md`
10. **Superhero.** The reverse of Dude with a Problem: an extraordinary being in an ordinary world. Sympathy comes from the pain of being different and misunderstood. → `10 Genres/10 - Superhero.md`

## 6. Hollywood's dirty little secret
Having seen the list, the reader may notice that many films are structurally the same and suspect outright theft. Snyder agrees the resemblance is real (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *Point Break* and *The Fast and the Furious* follow nearly the same beats, one about surfing and one about street racing.
- *The Matrix* and *Monsters, Inc.* are, in his reading, the same film.
- *Who Framed Roger Rabbit?* is a retelling of *Chinatown*.
- His own *Blank Check* closely resembles *Home Alone*.

Sometimes the borrowing is deliberate and sometimes it is coincidence, but mostly it happens because story templates work, and work for reasons worth repeating. Audiences do not complain (Snyder, ch. 2).

### What to do with this
The laws of storytelling work in every situation. The writer's job is to understand *why* a template works and how its parts mesh. His guidance (Snyder, ch. 2):
- If it feels like stealing, don't do it.
- If it feels like a cliché, twist it.
- If it feels familiar, it probably is, so find a new route.
- In every case, understand why you were drawn to the familiar version.

Once you stop feeling boxed in by the rules, he says, you find them freeing. Real originality starts only when you know what you are departing from (Snyder, ch. 2).

## 7. Summary, exceptions, and the roots of genre

### No exceptions needed (and the bullheads' test cases)
Snyder claims the ten categories are all a writer needs for now, then anticipates objections from "bullhead" readers and rules on three (Snyder, ch. 2):
- *The Breakfast Club* is Institutionalized, not Rites of Passage.
- *Rain Man* is Buddy Love, not Golden Fleece.
- *Zoolander* is a bad film, one of his favorite bad films, and a Superhero story.

### The point is the tool, not the taxonomy
Hunting for exceptions misses the purpose, which is to use categories as a storytelling tool. You cannot know every film, so this is a way in. Find the category your script is most like. A script may borrow moments from several categories, or start as one type and end as another. He allows that, but doubts such a script will sell (Snyder, ch. 2).

The aim is fluency in your genre's language, rhythm and goals. Know the genre, learn its rules, find what is essential, and you will write a more satisfying film with a better chance of selling (Snyder, ch. 2).

### Ancient roots
Snyder finds the genres inspiring because their lineage reaches far back, which shows that renewing old tales is an ancient job. His examples: *Jaws* retells the Minotaur myth or the medieval dragon-slayer tale; Superman is a modern Hercules; *Road Trip* updates Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*. Not knowing the roots of your story, whether a century of film or a thousand years of tales, fails to honor the job's traditions (Snyder, ch. 2).

He ends by restating that putting new twists on old tales, bringing them up to date and making them meaningful to a current audience has always been the storyteller's work. It is a skill to apply across the whole craft. The next chapter turns to the hero (Snyder, ch. 2).

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 2; described, not copied)
1. **Sort the newspaper:** assign each current release to a genre. If you see one, compare it with others in its genre, and ask whether the genre is what attracted you.
2. **Sort the TV listings:** go through the film loglines in a TV guide, numbering each film you have seen by genre. Check whether every film fits.
3. **Your own project:** decide your script's category, list other films in it, rent as many as you can find, and take notes comparing them. See whether you can now explain more clearly what type of film yours is.
4. **Invent a genre:** for those who like exceptions, name a new genre and find three (then five) films that belong to it. He invites readers to email strong new categories for possible inclusion in later editions.

## Terms introduced
- **"Give me the same thing... only different":** the rule that a story must work within its tradition and still twist it. Attributed to an unnamed executive (Snyder, ch. 2).
- **The twist:** the fresh turn that keeps an element from being a cliché.
- **Emotion machines / Swiss watches:** Snyder's image of films as precise mechanisms a writer should be able to dismantle.
- **Screening:** watching a set of films like yours (about a dozen) to learn your genre's mechanics.
- **"What is it... most like?":** the follow-up to "What is it?", used to identify your film's type.
- **"X meets Y" pitch:** a pitch that combines two hit films. Snyder dislikes it.
- **Genre gin rummy:** his joke for the sorting of films into categories.
- **The ten genres:** Monster in the House, Golden Fleece, Out of the Bottle, Dude with a Problem, Rites of Passage, Buddy Love, Whydunit, The Fool Triumphant, Institutionalized, Superhero.
- **Hollywood's dirty little secret:** that many hits share the same structure, because story templates work.
