# Chapter 7: What's Wrong with This Picture?

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 7 "What's Wrong with This Picture?" The local text has no page numbers; cited by chapter title, shortened below to (Snyder, ch. 7).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## 1. After THE END: the reread slump
Snyder congratulates the writer on finishing a draft, whatever its length. Finishing alone, he says, puts you ahead of half the aspiring screenwriters, whose ideas never leave conversation. Every script you finish makes you better; he counts some 75 scripts of his own, including TV, and sees clear growth. He keeps the attitude that his best script is always the one he writes next (Snyder, ch. 7).

**The slump.** He recommends setting a finished draft aside for about a week, longer if you can bear it. On rereading, the writer typically decides it is terrible: flat characters, nothing happening or happening too slowly. The mood swings from grandiosity to self-loathing. Snyder says this always happens. The fix is honest assessment plus willingness to do the work, and the chapter collects the typical trouble spots that writers before you have hit (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 2. The Hero Leads

### The problem: the inactive hero
A frequent first-draft fault, and hard to see if everything else is right. The plot is carefully built and every beat advances it, but the hero has not been told: he is pulled through the story, turns up where needed for no reason, and has a vague goal and no driving force (Snyder, ch. 7).

Snyder's caricature is a listless detective he names **Johnny Entropy**. He shows up, goes through the motions, never hunts for clues (they are handed to him), has no goals, and shrugs that everyone dies anyway (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The law
**The hero must be proactive.** If he is not, he is not a hero (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The four-question checklist
(Snyder, ch. 7; paraphrased)
1. **Is the goal clear in the set-up?** Is it obvious to you and the audience what the hero wants? If you don't know, work it out. Then have the goal stated aloud, and restate it in word and deed through the story.
2. **Does he seek, or is he handed things?** If the next clue simply arrives, something is wrong. The hero cannot be given his destiny; he must earn it at every step.
3. **Active or passive?** Everything he does must come from a burning desire and a deep need to reach the goal. A hero who cannot be bothered, or will get to it later, is a Hamlet: fine for Shakespeare, wrong for an action star.
4. **Who gives the orders?** A rule of thumb: **the hero never asks questions.** He knows, and others look to him for answers. Many question marks in the hero's dialogue are a warning sign.

If any of these apply, the hero is inactive and the script will lie flat. Push him into the game; heroes lead (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 3. Talking the Plot

### The problem
Characters announcing backstory to each other in unnatural lines (for example, telling a sibling something both obviously already know, or mentioning a past football career and the accident that ended it). Snyder calls it a sure sign of an inexperienced writer and the kind of thing that gets a script thrown across the room. He understands why it happens: the writer has backstory and plot to convey and no other way to do it (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Why it fails
Nobody talks that way. The writer forgets that characters serve themselves, not the writer. Each should enter a scene with his own goal and say what is on *his* mind. Reveal who characters are and what they want (hopes, dreams, fears) as much by *how* they speak as by what they say. Good dialogue carries more in its **subtext** than in its literal words; understatement wins, and plot-talk is a sledgehammer (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Show, Don't Tell (the adjunct)
Another of the most common beginner faults (Snyder, ch. 7):
- A husband eyeing a young woman as he walks with his wife says more about a troubled marriage than pages of dialogue about counseling.
- Film tells stories in pictures, so showing is also more economical.
- To establish a former football career: photos on the wall, a limp (only if it matters), small references.
- To show that two people have fought: have them talk about anything except the fight. Audiences are sharper than writers think.

### Action reveals character
Showing leaves characters free to be active and to pursue their own agendas. In films as in life, people show who they are through **actions rather than words**. In a good film, information emerges through the story's momentum. Deliver backstory on the run, or skip it; care more about what is happening now than about what happened before the story began (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 4. Make the Bad Guy Badder

### The problem
Sometimes the hero does everything right (proactive, overcoming obstacles, doing remarkable things) and still seems small and unheroic. The fault may be the antagonist. The law: **make the villain as bad as you can** (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Why first drafts go soft
Writers want the hero to win so much that they avoid making it impossible. But you cannot shield the hero from danger; you have to throw more at him than he can handle. Making the villain worse automatically makes the hero bigger (Snyder, ch. 7).

### James Bond
What makes Bond Bond is not the gadgets, the women or the car but his famous villains. Against a crooked small-town accountant, Bond would need none of his skills; he could finish with a quick online search. He needs an opponent whose powers match his own (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Two halves of one person
In many well-told films, hero and villain are **two sides of a single persona** competing for dominance, and so roughly equal. Snyder's examples: *Batman* (Keaton and Nicholson), *Die Hard* (Willis and Rickman), and *Pretty Woman* (Gere and his ruthless lawyer, Jason Alexander). Each pair is a light and dark side, a photographic positive and negative of the same person, and each wants something from the other, if only an explanation of what made them that way (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Calibrating the match
Hero and villain should be evenly matched in ability and power, with the **edge to the villain**, because he will go to any length to win; by definition he has given up decent values. He should not be unbeatable, only appear so. Raising his power forces the hero into more admirable feats; keeping victory just out of reach raises the hero (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 5. Turn, Turn, Turn

### The law
A slogan he has kept on a note above his desk for twenty years, the first piece of screenwriting advice he ever heard, though he cannot remember who said it. **The plot must not just move forward; it must spin and intensify as it goes.** Snyder frames it as the difference between velocity (constant speed) and acceleration (increasing speed): the plot must move toward the climax faster and with growing complexity (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Movement without story
When things happen but none of it engages, all you have is a chase. His example is the live-action *The Cat in the Hat*: constant noise and action with nothing actually happening, kinetic spectacle that goes nowhere and risks nothing. It proves a film can be full of action and still have no story (Snyder, ch. 7).

### What turning means
Each step of the plot must reveal more about the characters and what the action means. Show how events affect them: the heroes' flaws, betrayals, doubts and fears, and threats against them; the villains' secret strengths, reserves and sinister motives the hero doesn't know about. Snyder's image is a diamond: it must not just slide across the screen but rotate so that its facets catch the light (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Speed
As the villain's grip tightens, events come faster, until the pressure bursts in the Act Three climax. If the plot is not intensifying after the midpoint, there is a problem. If the plot feels static, examine it from fresh angles and speed it up (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 6. The Emotional Color Wheel

### The law
A good film runs the audience through the full range of emotions: laughter, tears, desire, fear, regret, anger, frustration, close-call suspense and, finally, triumph. The audience leaves exhausted. Wringing out the audience's emotions is the goal, in comedy or drama (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Why
People go to films to escape, to learn a small lesson about life, and to enter a kind of dream in which life's emotions are replayed without risk. Like a good dream, a film has to be lived (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The one-note problem
A comedy that is only funny, or a drama that is only tense, is one-note. Snyder's surprising model is the Farrelly brothers. Their comedies (*There's Something About Mary*, *Shallow Hal*, *Stuck on You*) are known for raunchy set pieces but also contain fear, longing, lust and human weakness. They succeed because the whole palette is in use (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The fix
If a script feels one-note (Snyder, ch. 7):
- Ask where the lust scene is, the frustration scene, the scary scene.
- Take a scene that is only funny or only dramatic and replay it in a missing emotion. Assign a color to each missing emotion and tag scenes to convert.
- Keep the scene's action, its +/-, its conflict and its outcome, but change the emotional key (desire in place of laughs, or jealousy in place of a standoff).
- Varying the emotions makes the experience richer for everyone.

## 7. "Hi How Are You I'm Fine"

### The problem
Flat dialogue, which even good films have: placeholder talk that bores the reader. The chapter's title phrase names the problem: generic small talk that anyone could say. Lines lifted from real life may ring true but still ring dull, and if the dialogue is flat, the characters probably are too (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The principle
Engaging characters speak differently from ordinary people. Even mundane lines lift them above the norm. How a person talks *is* character, and can expose his past, inner demons and outlook. Every line of dialogue is an opportunity to show that (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The Bad Dialogue Test
Learned from Mike Cheda, who told the young Snyder that all his characters sounded alike. Snyder was offended until he tried the test: **cover the character names on a page and read the exchange. Is each speaker identifiable?** He could not, and realized every character spoke in his own voice. In a good script, each character speaks differently, even in throwaway small talk (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Verbal tics
In an early draft of *Big, Ugly Baby!* (a comedy about an alien baby switched at birth), Snyder gave every character a distinctive speech habit: one stuttered, one mangled words, one was a rural philosopher, and the alien parents always shouted (shown by capitalizing a word in each sentence they spoke). Writers need not go that far, but the exercise showed him how to deepen characters and make the script livelier to read out loud (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 8. Take a Step Back

### The problem
Snyder and Sheldon Bull spent ten months and seven drafts on their Golden Fleece road comedy because they broke this rule. Their hero, a boy expelled from military school who finds his family has moved, went on the road as a kind boy who helped strangers and improved their lives. That was the mistake: **he had already changed**. He needed no journey and ended the same as he began. Each draft moved him back a step emotionally, then farther, then all the way back (the text lays this out one word per line for effect) (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Why writers do it
Knowing where heroes end up, writers spare them the pain of growth. Snyder compares this to parenting: you cannot protect characters from every bump; they grow by being knocked around. He and Bull liked their hero so much that they wanted him upbeat and special without showing the struggle, which he likens to copying the answer key without working the problems. The journey *was* the story, and the bumps make the payoff bigger (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The rule
Apply it to **every character**: take each one back to the starting point so the audience sees the whole change. Films must show everything: all the change, growth and action of the hero's journey. His image is an archer's bow: pull it back as far as it will go and the arrow flies farthest. If a story or a character isn't showing the whole flight, take a step back (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 9. A Limp and an Eyepatch

### The problem
Everything works (hero, villain, escalating plot, sharp dialogue) except that minor characters blur together and readers confuse them. Writers excuse this by saying casting will fix it. Snyder's answer: the script will never reach casting if the reader cannot see the characters (Snyder, ch. 7).

### The rule
Every character needs a distinctive way of speaking and **something memorable that fixes him in the reader's mind**: a visual hook, often a recurring visual reminder. The name sounds silly, but the device is reliable whenever a writer remembers it (Snyder, ch. 7).

### *Really Mean Girls*
Often the need is pointed out by a reader. In this script, the lead boy (in love with the heroine, her moral compass and truth-teller, funny and old for his age) mattered to the plot yet left no impression. Their manager, Andy Cohen, kept flagging him; making him funnier or smarter didn't help. Sheldon Bull's fix: introduce him in a black T-shirt with a scraggly soul patch (which fit his wish to seem older and cooler than he looked) and mention it whenever he appeared. The manager said the character now jumped off the page, though almost nothing else had changed (Snyder, ch. 7).

**Not artifice, just the job.** When characters get lost in the shuffle, give them a limp and an eyepatch (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 10. Is It Primal?

### The touchstone
"Primal" runs through the whole book. Asking whether something is primal, or whether a caveman would get it, is his test from start to finish, both when creating and when fixing a script. It checks whether the story reaches the audience at the most basic level (Snyder, ch. 7).
- Does the plot turn on survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones or fear of death?
- Every goal needs a root that basic, whatever it appears to be about on top.
- More primal motives ground the story in something visceral, and make the film easier to sell worldwide: a love story plays everywhere, and so does a creature film where the rule is not to be eaten (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Minor characters and subplots too
Ask whether supporting characters are driven by primal motives, which amounts to asking whether they behave like recognizable people. His example: a lofty premise about stockbrokers rigging the global bond market becomes universal once each character's desire is made primal, so it is about people trying to survive (Snyder, ch. 7).

### Examples and the biological reading
He lists hits by their primal drive: saving family (*Die Hard*), defending home (*Home Alone*), finding a mate (*Sleepless in Seattle*), revenge (*Gladiator*), survival (*Titanic*). He then reads these as biological imperatives: wealth stands for more resources and more offspring; revenge for removing a rival's genes; finding a parent or child for protecting one's own line. However sophisticated your story seems, at its core it must speak to the caveman (Snyder, ch. 7).

## 11. Summary: the "Is It Broken?" Test
Snyder turns the chapter into a diagnostic list for scripts that feel flat or draw vague reader complaints. He introduces it as seven questions, but the list has nine ⚠️ (an inconsistency in the source). Paraphrased (Snyder, ch. 7):
1. Does the hero lead, proactive at every stage and driven by a goal?
2. Do characters talk the plot, saying what a novelist would narrate instead of letting the action show it?
3. Is the villain bad enough, the right challenge, and do both belong in this film?
4. Does the plot speed up and intensify after the midpoint, revealing more about hero and villain as the finale approaches?
5. Is the script one-note emotionally, needing emotional breaks it doesn't have?
6. Is the dialogue flat? Does the Bad Dialogue Test show everyone sounding the same?
7. Are minor characters distinct in look, speech and manner?
8. Is the hero's starting point pulled back far enough that his whole growth shows?
9. Is it primal? Are characters reaching for something basic (love, survival, family, revenge)?

### Do the work
When in doubt, fix it. If you or your first readers see a problem, so will everyone else, and saying no one will notice is wrong. You only make one first impression, so get over your attachment to your own work and fix the script before it reaches an important desk. The difference between a professional and an amateur is that the inner voice saying the script is bad is followed by a mature voice saying it knows how to fix it (Snyder, ch. 7).

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 7; described, not copied)
1. **Run the test on a weak film:** choose a weak film from your genre list and use the diagnostic to find improvements.
2. **Weaken the villain:** take a favorite genre film, imagine its villain made weaker or ordinary, and notice whether the hero becomes less interesting too.
3. **Talk the plot in real life:** at a gathering, say aloud the kind of backstory-stating line bad dialogue uses, and watch how people react.
