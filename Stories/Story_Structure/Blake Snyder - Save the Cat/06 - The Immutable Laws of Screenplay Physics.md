# Chapter 6: The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 6 "The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics." The local text has no page numbers; cited by chapter title, shortened below to (Snyder, ch. 6).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

---

## 1. Why these laws, and how to hold them
Snyder admits the book began with a wish to get credit for a set of catchy rules he and his writer friends had collected over the years (Snyder, ch. 6).
- **Science as well as art.** He treats screenwriting as measurable, governed by constants, some of them timeless, and he cites Joseph Campbell as evidence.
- **Discovered, not invented.** The rules become clear after watching many films, and each one brings a "Eureka!" and an urge to claim it. But they cannot really be discovered; they were true before anyone named them.
- **Why they come so late in the book.** He would have liked the whole book to be these laws, but the reader first needed the process from idea to execution.
- **For the doubters.** He expects rule-breakers among his readers, since that is what screenwriters are, and welcomes them. His point is that you learn the laws in order to override them knowingly, the way Picasso mastered conventional drawing before Cubism (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 2. Save the Cat

### The controversy
The title rule is, Snyder says, surprisingly divisive. Some readers of his manuscript call it his weakest idea, call the *Sea of Love* example (see `00 - Foreword and Introduction.md`) old-fashioned, and find likable heroes saccharine, a way of pandering (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The rule and its adjunct
- **The rule:** "The hero has to do something when we meet him so that we like him and want him to win" (Snyder, ch. 6).
- **Not literal.** It does not mean every film needs a scene of the hero giving money to a blind man.
- **The adjunct** he adds for critics: the writer must take care to bring the audience "in sync" with the hero's predicament right from the start (Snyder, ch. 6). The cat is shorthand for that larger duty.

### *Pulp Fiction*: likable without kindness
The film makes no effort to flatter its audience. Its first heroes are drug-using hit men. Tarantino handles the problem by making them funny and naive: their talk about what fast-food burgers are called in France is comic and almost childlike. We are on their side even as they go to kill someone. Snyder argues Tarantino follows the rule in his own way, because he knows Travolta will become a lead whom viewers must be willing to back (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The trick for anti-heroes: make the antagonist worse
When the hero is a bit of a bad man, or the hero of a comeuppance story, make his opponent worse. In the same opening of *Pulp Fiction*, Travolta tells the story of the boss who had an underling thrown out a window over a foot massage given to the boss's wife. Next to that boss, the hit man looks harmless, and the balance between whom we like and whom we fear is reset (Snyder, ch. 6).

### *Aladdin*: rewriting a hero to be likable
His favorite example. In the source tales, Aladdin is spoiled, lazy and a thief. Disney's writers, Terry Rossio and Ted Elliott (whom Snyder rates the best working screenwriters and wishes were better known), solved it with his introduction. He steals food because he is hungry and escapes palace guards through the market, which also introduces the setting. Then, about to eat, he sees two starving children and gives them his food. From then on we are on his side and hoping he succeeds, though he is no longer the original idle thief (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The point: care
You do not need a literal cat rescue, a kindly act toward an old lady, or a hero splashed by a passing car to win sympathy. You do need to lead the audience gently, every single time, into alignment with your lead and your story, and frame the hero's situation so that we root for him whoever he is and whatever he does. Assuming we will like the hero for no reason, the *Lara Croft* approach, is not doing the job. Some films get away with it, but that does not make it good, careful storytelling (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 3. The Pope in the Pool
More a trick than a rule, and one of the first things Mike Cheda taught him (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The problem: exposition
**Exposition** is backstory or plot detail the audience needs in order to follow what comes next. It is dull and kills scenes; complicated plots suffer from it most (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The solution
Cheda described a thriller script by George Englund about a conspiracy against the Pope. Its key backstory is delivered when officials visit the Pope, and the meeting takes place at the Vatican's pool, with the Pope swimming laps in a bathing suit. Snyder guesses viewers would barely hear the information because they would be marveling that the Vatican has a pool and that the Pope is out of his robes. The scene ends before they notice (Snyder, ch. 6). The trick: stage necessary exposition alongside something so diverting that the audience absorbs it painlessly.

### His own use: *Drips*
In a comedy he and Colby Carr sold to Disney, two dim plumbers are lured by a beautiful woman into a plot to tap an old oil well under a Beverly Hills mansion and pipe the oil through the sewers to a waiting tanker. The villain has to explain the whole heist, a scene that risked boring the audience. Their fix: beforehand, the two heroes try to impress the woman by out-drinking each other on iced tea. During the villain's presentation they are desperate to use the bathroom, while sprinklers start outside, a dog relieves itself on a bush, and the woman pours herself more tea. The information gets across and the scene is funny (Snyder, ch. 6).

### Other examples
- *Austin Powers* goes further with a character named Basil Exposition whose only job is to explain things, joking openly about how dull exposition is.
- The two comic guards in *Pirates of the Caribbean* who fill us in on Jack Sparrow.
- The batting-cage scene in *Clear and Present Danger*.

In each, something to watch takes the sting out of what we need to be told, and keeps it lively (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 4. Double Mumbo Jumbo
A rule writers cannot break, Snyder says, though films break it constantly (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The law
Audiences will accept **a single piece of magic in any film**. You cannot have space aliens land and then have them bitten by a vampire, so they are now aliens and undead at once. That doubling is **Double Mumbo Jumbo**. It confuses the audience even when filmmakers do it anyway (Snyder, ch. 6).

### *Spider-Man*
Why does a hit people happily paid to see not hold them on a rerun? Snyder blames the moment the Green Goblin arrives. The film asks us to accept one source of powers (a radioactive spider bite) and then a second, unrelated one (a lab accident creating the Goblin). That strains suspension of disbelief and breaks the world's reality after we had already granted one leap. He calls it sloppy, though comics get some leeway (Snyder, ch. 6).

### *Signs*
Here there is no comic-book excuse. The film asks us to believe aliens have invaded and also to care about the hero's crisis of faith in God. Snyder argues that proof of alien life overwhelms any question of faith; the minute aliens land, the faith crisis looks absurd. His guess is that the writer-director began with a single magical premise (the aliens and their crop circles), knew it was familiar, and tried to deepen it with a second, which muddied it. God and aliens are two different kinds of magic. He adds a jab at the film's baseball-bat ending (Snyder, ch. 6).

**Rule:** unless you have a hit already or are adapting a comic, do not try it. One piece of magic per film (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 5. Laying Pipe

### The law
Audiences tolerate only so much **"pipe"**: the setup a story requires before it can start. Too much risks their attention, and Snyder believes *Minority Report* and *Along Came Polly* both fell short because they spent too long laying it (Snyder, ch. 6).

### *Minority Report*
The setup is huge: the opening stopped murder, the politics of the pre-crime unit, an investigator looking into the hero, the three psychics in their tank, the hero's lost child and drug habit, and a mentor we are unsure of. Snyder found it tiring and pointless-seeming until the real story starts: the system names the hero himself as a future murderer, and he must find out why and stop it. That hook (the detective learns he is the criminal) arrives about forty minutes in (Snyder, ch. 6).

### *Along Came Polly*
To reach the premise, a cautious divorcé dating a wild woman, the film must first show the hero's wedding, honeymoon, and his discovery of his wife with their scuba instructor. It is funny, and the star buys goodwill, but it still delays what the audience came for (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The lesson
Needing that much backstory bends the whole story out of shape. Snyder is very alert to this and has dropped story ideas because of the pipe they needed. His own *Blank Check* has more pipe than he likes before its young hero reaches the bank to cash his million-dollar check, about half a beat too much in Act One, nearly fatal. His rule of thumb: **if the setup takes more than 25 pages, there is a problem**. Writers call it laying pipe; audiences call it wanting their money back (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 6. Black Vet (also called Too Much Marzipan)

### The law
An offshoot of Double Mumbo Jumbo, applied to concepts. Writers fall in love with elements of an idea and cannot let go; Snyder compares this to Lennie in *Of Mice and Men* squeezing the mouse. When you notice this, step back (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The origin
In the 1970s Albert Brooks made a parody of network promos for *Saturday Night Live*. One fake show starred a vet with a military past: a veteran *and* a veterinarian. Snyder found it brilliant because real TV comes so close to it, cramming too much into one concept. Like marzipan, small amounts suffice, and more is not better (Snyder, ch. 6).

### His own example: *Lefty*
With his first partner, Howard Burkons, he developed a TV series about a blacklisted private eye in the 1950s, called *Lefty* for his politics. Burkons wanted him also to be left-handed, and then also a former boxer. To Snyder this was a case of "pick one"; to Burkons it was getting full value from the idea. Burkons, whose marketing instincts Snyder respects, still says he was right (Snyder, ch. 6).

**Rule:** keep it simple. Use a single concept. Piling on does not improve an idea; it confuses. If you catch yourself doing it, stop (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 7. Watch Out for That Glacier!

### The law
Villains or threats that approach so slowly, or from so far off, that they create no tension. Snyder pictures danger moving at an inch a year (Snyder, ch. 6).

### Examples
- ***Dante's Peak*:** the whole story is that a volcano will erupt, no one believes the scientist, and we wait for him to be proved right.
- ***Outbreak*:** a deadly virus creeps slowly toward the U.S. while the hero searches for a cure. It is basically a Monster in the House film, but it has to build its "house" by quarantining a town and introducing the townspeople late (around page 75, he says) so we will care about them, while we wait for the infected monkey subplot.
- ***Open Range*:** the heroes' friend is killed early, and they then spend most of the film talking about going after the killers.

Bright filmmakers still assume slow-approaching danger will stir emotion, and it does not (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The rule
Danger must be **present**. The stakes must fall on people the audience cares about, and the harm they face must be made clear early so we understand the cost of the immediate threat. He adds a comic list of hopeless "glaciers" (slow, remote or harmless menaces such as a herd of angry turtles or a letter from far away) and jokes that locusts qualify, unless they have been altered to eat people (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 8. The Covenant of the Arc

### The law
**Every character in the film must change** as the story unfolds. Only the villains stay the same; the hero and his allies change greatly (Snyder, ch. 6).

### On the word "arc"
Snyder dislikes the overused term but likes its meaning: the change a character undergoes across his journey, start to finish. Done well, charting every character's change is like poetry. It says the story is so important that it transforms everyone near it, including the audience (Snyder, ch. 6).

### Why everyone must change
A story worth telling must matter deeply to everyone in it. So set-ups and payoffs have to be built and tracked for each character. His example is *Pretty Woman*: both leads, the heroine's friend and even the hotel manager who mentors her are transformed; only the villain, the hero's lawyer, learns nothing. All the films people remember and want to see again do this (Snyder, ch. 6).

### Change as the measure
Change is what stories are about, and the capacity for it separates winners from losers. Good characters accept change as positive. Bad characters refuse it and stay stuck in their rut. Snyder links this to life and to major religions: transformation means rebirth and a fresh start. We want to believe it, and a film where everyone grows makes us want to try something new ourselves (Snyder, ch. 6).

### Practice
"Everybody arcs" is written on a note stuck to his monitor. Before writing, he charts each character's change on The Board, marking the milestones along the way. If a script feels flat, run a Covenant of the Arc check: does everyone (except the villain) change and grow (Snyder, ch. 6)?

## 9. Keep the Press Out

### Origin
Snyder learned this from Steven Spielberg while developing *Nuclear Family* (written with Jim Haggin and sold to Amblin). In that wish-fulfillment comedy, a family camping on a nuclear dump gains powers, each matching a need: the father reads minds and gets ahead of a rival at work; the mother moves objects with her mind; the son gains speed and stars at football; the struggling daughter becomes brilliant. In the end they give the powers up, learning that family matters more than success (Snyder, ch. 6).

### The rule
When Snyder pitched having the media discover the family, Spielberg said no, and explained it with *E.T.* That film has no news crews, though it has every reason to. Spielberg found, in rewrites with screenwriter Melissa Mathison, that bringing in the press broke the reality of the premise. Keeping the secret within the family, the neighborhood and the audience keeps the magic real. Snyder links this to **breaking the fourth wall**: the invisible barrier between a drama and its audience (Snyder, ch. 6).

### *Signs* violates it
The besieged family watches TV coverage of aliens landing around the world, including footage of one at a children's party in South America. Snyder finds it irrelevant to the family's struggle and says it makes their plight less desperate, since now everyone shares it. It pulled him out of the story (Snyder, ch. 6).

### Scope
Use the press carefully. Bring it in only if the story is about the press, or concerns a global problem followed through characters in many places who need to know about each other (Snyder, ch. 6).

## 10. Summary
- These laws are small "Eurekas" that come from watching a great many films. Seeing them is like opening the back of a watch to see the gears, or learning a magician's secrets (Snyder, ch. 6).
- **Why give them labels:** catchy names (Save the Cat, the swimming Pope, Black Vet, keeping the press out) make the lessons stick. When you drift toward a mistake, or want to break a rule, the label gives an instant reckoning of the cost and benefit. He admits to catching himself breaking them often.
- **Shortcuts:** the aim is speed. He compares screenwriting to solving the same kind of puzzle repeatedly; every story broken, outline beaten out and script finished makes you faster (Snyder, ch. 6).

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 6; described, not copied)
1. **The unlikable hero:** name one, judge whether the filmmakers dealt with the problem, and invent new Save the Cat devices that win sympathy without seeming phony.
2. **Spot the Pope in the Pool:** find other films that use it, and judge whether hiding the exposition helps or hurts your understanding of the plot.
3. **Repair *Spider-Man*:** setting aside the comic-book canon, change the script to remove its doubled magic.
4. **The *Signs* challenge:** a joke invitation to readers who liked *Signs* to email him a defense of *Unbreakable*, which he finds even less plausible.

## Terms introduced
- **Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics:** Snyder's name for his collected, catchy storytelling rules.
- **Save the Cat (rule):** the hero must do something early that makes us like him and want him to win.
- **In sync (adjunct to Save the Cat):** the duty to align the audience with the hero's plight from the start.
- **Make the antagonist worse:** offset a flawed hero by making his opponent more terrible.
- **The Pope in the Pool:** delivering exposition during a distracting or entertaining situation.
- **Burying exposition:** making necessary backstory painless (see Glossary, "Exposition").
- **Double Mumbo Jumbo (DMJ):** more than one kind of magic in one film; forbidden.
- **Laying pipe:** setup and backstory needed before the story can start; too much loses the audience.
- **Black Vet / Too Much Marzipan:** overloading a concept with extra gimmicks; keep it to one.
- **Watch Out for That Glacier!:** a threat too slow or distant to create tension.
- **Present danger:** the cure for the glacier; an immediate threat with shown consequences.
- **Covenant of the Arc:** every character but the villain must change.
- **Everybody arcs:** his working slogan for the covenant.
- **Keep the Press Out:** keep the media out of a contained premise to protect its reality.
- **Breaking the fourth wall:** anything that makes the audience aware it is watching a film (see Glossary).
