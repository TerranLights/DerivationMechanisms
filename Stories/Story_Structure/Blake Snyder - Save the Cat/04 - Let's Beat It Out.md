# Chapter 4: Let's Beat It Out!

Source: Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (2005), ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!" The local text has no page numbers; cited by chapter title, shortened below to (Snyder, ch. 4). Each beat's full extraction is in `15 Beats/`.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

---

## 1. Not writing yet
Snyder still holds the writer back from drafting. He reviews what the earlier chapters accomplished: a tested logline, a dozen films screened in the genre, the right hero and antagonist, and an amplified primal goal and conflict. The next step is working out *how* to write it (Snyder, ch. 4).

### "Let's beat it out!"
This is his rallying cry for a newborn idea. It means putting every scene, idea and character up on The Board (ch. 5) to see what goes where, who does what, and which imagined scenes are needed or must be invented. He calls it the measure-twice, cut-once stage: it saves time, lets you pitch the story beat by beat, and builds the frame of the script. In short, it is time for **structure** (Snyder, ch. 4).

## 2. Why structure matters

### Most important after idea and hero
After the idea, and after deciding who the film is about and who it is for, structure matters more than anything else in writing and selling a script. Good structure is solid, and it shows buyers you did the work of a sound blueprint (Snyder, ch. 4).

### Structure protects the writer
Later writers hired to rewrite you (he calls them **credit jumpers**) may reorder scenes, cut dialogue, and add or drop characters. If the structure is sound and you know why the story works, the script stays strong and stays yours. Snyder says the beat structure is the best evidence at a Writers Guild credit arbitration that the work is mainly yours, which also protects your residuals (Snyder, ch. 4). The craft, patience and magic of film storytelling come together in how you carry out structure, so it is a skill a writer must have.

## 3. How Snyder arrived at his beat sheet
He came to structure late and out of desperation. Early on he pitched by offering the concept and a few cool scenes, then had nothing more to say. The first time an executive asked about his "act break," he had no idea what that meant (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Syd Field.** Reading Field's *Screenplay*, which Snyder credits as the origin of the modern template, gave him three acts. It saved his career, but it left long stretches of "open water" between the two act breaks where a writer could get lost and drown. He wanted more islands and shorter swims.
- **Viki King.** *How to Write a Movie in 21 Days* added more landmarks, including midpoints and B stories, but still left too much room for error.
- **His own sheet.** From films he had seen, books he had read and what he found himself relying on, he built the **Blake Snyder Beat Sheet**: fifteen beats that fit, left-aligned, on one page (Snyder, ch. 4).

## 4. The Blake Snyder Beat Sheet (BS2)

### The form
The one-page form has spaces for project title, genre and date, then the fifteen beats with page numbers. Snyder's nickname for it is the **BS2** (Snyder, ch. 4).

| # | Beat | Pages (110-page script) | % (our conversion) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | ~1% |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | ~5% |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10 | 1–9% |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | ~11% |
| 5 | Debate | 12–25 | 11–23% |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 | ~23% |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | ~27% |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30–55 | 27–50% |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | 50% |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55–75 | 50–68% |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | ~68% |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–85 | 68–77% |
| 13 | Break into Three | 85 | ~77% |
| 14 | Finale | 85–110 | 77–100% |
| 15 | Final Image | 110 | 100% |

The page numbers are Snyder's. The percentages are our arithmetic, not his. In the local text the Final Image's page appears as a scan error ("no"); 110 is clear from context and from the Finale's range.

### How he uses it
- **Before every pitch meeting** he fills in every blank, and will not go in until he has. Each beat gets only one or two sentences (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **The one-sentence test.** As with the logline for the whole film, if he cannot state a beat in a sentence or two, he does not have it yet; he is guessing. Often he only discovers the gap by trying to fill in the form.
- **Page targets.** The numbers are page numbers. A script should be about 110 pages, which he jokes is a good jockey's weight. Dramas may run longer, but the proportions hold. He insists that act breaks, midpoint and All Is Lost land on their marks (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Proof from his own work.** In *Blank Check*, the theme is stated about five minutes in (page 5), and the midpoint, the All Is Lost beat and the third-act break land where planned and survived from script to screen. He and Colby Carr worked every draft to keep them there, and the solid structure resisted attempts to rewrite them (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **His own names.** "Fun and games" and "dark night of the soul" are his terms for beats found in both comedy and drama (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Not optional.** He asks whether the writer has a choice about using it, and answers no (Snyder, ch. 4).

## 5. The fifteen beats (summary; full files in `15 Beats/`)

### 1. Opening Image (p. 1)
The first impression of tone, mood, type and scope. His examples are the motorcycle ride in *Lawrence of Arabia*, Kane's looming castle in *Citizen Kane*, and the college motto under the founder's statue in *Animal House*. It is also a "before" snapshot of the hero, paired with the Final Image as a bookend; the two should be opposites that prove the change. He says actors may read only the first and last ten pages to check for that change. All good films have a standout opening image (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/01 - Opening Image.md`

### 2. Theme Stated (p. 5)
Around page 5, someone, usually not the hero, makes a remark to the hero that states the theme. It is casual and offhand, and the hero does not grasp it yet. This is the film's **thematic premise**. Snyder frames a screenplay as an argument for and against a way of living or a goal, which the rest of the film proves or disproves from every angle. Every film needs a subject it is arguing about, and that subject belongs up front, on page 5. You may not know it until the first draft is done (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/02 - Theme Stated.md`

### 3. Set-Up (pp. 1–10)
The make-or-break opening where readers decide whether to keep going. It establishes the hero, what is at stake and what he wants, and introduces or hints at every A-story character. It plants the hero's quirks and behaviors that will matter later, and shows why and how he must change. His examples are the heroines of *Romancing the Stone* and *Legally Blonde* and the hero of *Rain Man* (Snyder, ch. 4). Two wider tools live here:
- **Six Things That Need Fixing.** Snyder's term for the list, which must be *shown*, of what is missing or wrong in the hero's life. "Six" is arbitrary. These flaws and tics are planted like time bombs to be reversed and paid off later as running gags and callbacks. In *Big*, the boy is too short for a ride, cannot get the girl and has no privacy, and Act Two grants all of it. The payoffs only work because we saw the set-ups (Snyder, ch. 4).
- **Thesis, antithesis, synthesis, and Stasis = Death.** Snyder thinks of the three acts as three worlds: thesis (Act One, the world as it is), antithesis (Act Two, its upside-down version) and synthesis (Act Three). The set-up documents the "before" world, a calm before the storm that would stay the same if nothing happened. But there is a sense the storm must come, because staying the same would be death; things have to change. The *Miss Congeniality* breakdown names this "Stasis = Death" (Snyder, ch. 4).
→ `15 Beats/03 - Set-Up.md`

### 4. Catalyst (p. 12)
The event that knocks down the world the set-up built: a telegram, a firing, a betrayal, a terminal diagnosis, a knock at the door. His examples are the package in *Romancing the Stone*, the call about the father's death in *Rain Man*, and the fiancé dumping Elle at dinner in *Legally Blonde*. It often arrives as bad news but leads, in the end, to happiness. In early drafts it tends to drift toward page 20 under too much set-up; cut until it lands on page 12, or readers will lose patience (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/04 - Catalyst.md`

### 5. Debate (pp. 12–25)
The hero's last chance to say the journey is crazy. Should he go? Does he dare? The section must pose a question. In *Legally Blonde* it is whether Elle can get into Harvard Law, answered by her test score, her admissions video and her acceptance. Snyder's own road comedy uses it to show how daunting the road will be (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/05 - Debate.md`

### 6. Break into Two (p. 25)
No later than page 25 of 110. Snyder checks page 25 first in any script he is handed. The hero leaves the thesis world for its upside-down antithesis, and the step must be definite. **The hero must choose it.** He may not be coaxed, fooled or carried along into Act Two, because being proactive is what makes him a hero; Luke chooses to go in *Star Wars* (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/06 - Break into Two.md`

### 7. B Story (p. 30)
Usually the love story, and the thread that **carries the theme**. It also acts as a **booster rocket** that softens the jolt of the act break and gives the audience a breather. It often brings new characters who are upside-down versions of Act One's people. In *Legally Blonde*, the manicurist mirrors the sorority girls, and she is where Elle is nurtured, confides what she is learning, and draws strength for Act Three (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/07 - B Story.md`

### 8. Fun and Games (pp. 30–55)
"The promise of the premise" (Snyder, ch. 4): the heart of the poster, where the trailer moments and set pieces live. Stakes are not raised yet; the tone is lighter. It answers why the audience bought a ticket. His examples are his own "world's slowest chase" in *Stop! Or My Mom Will Shoot*, the scene that sold the script; the first time the hero gets the better of the hijackers in *Die Hard*; and the hero first playing God in *Bruce Almighty*. In buddy films, it is where the buddies clash most (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/08 - Fun and Games.md`

### 9. Midpoint (p. 55)
The threshold between the film's two halves. It is either an "up" (a **false victory**: the hero seems to peak) or a "down" (a **false defeat**: the world seems to collapse). Choosing which one fixes a spike in the wall to hang the story on. He discovered it by taping films to listen to while driving: *What's Up, Doc?* split exactly at a "down." At the midpoint the fun and games end and **the stakes are raised**. It pairs with All Is Lost as its inverse: things are never as good as they look at one, or as bad as they look at the other (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/09 - Midpoint.md`

### 10. Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55–75)
The hardest section to write; he says you just have to muscle through it. The defeated bad guys regroup and bring heavier weapons, while doubt, dissent and jealousy split the hero's team. His examples are *Blank Check*'s weak stretch and the *Really Mean Girls* fix, where the rivals regroup, the heroines fall out over credit, and the midpoint is reversed. The pressure is both internal and external, and the hero has nowhere to turn (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/10 - Bad Guys Close In.md`

### 11. All Is Lost (p. 75)
The opposite of the midpoint: a "false defeat" that looks total. Snyder's trade secret is the **whiff of death**: something, literal or symbolic, dies here (a mentor such as Obi-Wan, a goldfish, a flower, an aunt). Here the former world, former self and former way of thinking are killed off, so that thesis and antithesis can fuse into synthesis. In *Elf*, the hero considers suicide on a bridge at about page 75 (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/11 - All Is Lost.md`

### 12. Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75–85)
How the hero feels about the loss. It may last seconds or minutes, but it is required: the darkness before the dawn, the forsaken moment. The hero has to be defeated, and aware of it, before he yields to fate, finds humility and finds the solution (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/12 - Dark Night of the Soul.md`

### 13. Break into Three (p. 85)
The solution, found thanks to the B story's characters, the theme discussed there, and the hero's last effort in the A story. The external (A) and internal (B) stories meet and intertwine. The classic version: a clue from the love interest lets the hero solve both at once. Synthesis is now within reach (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/13 - Break into Three.md`

### 14. Finale (pp. 85–110)
Act Three. The lessons are applied, the tics mastered, and the A and B stories end in triumph. The old world is turned over and a new order created. The bad guys are dispatched in **ascending order** (lieutenants and henchmen first, then the boss), and the root of the problem must be removed completely. Winning is not enough; the hero must **change the world**, and in an emotionally satisfying way (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/14 - Finale.md`. Note: this 2005 book has no five-point finale. See that file.

### 15. Final Image (p. 110)
The reverse of the first image, and evidence that real change occurred. If you cannot find it, the problem is in Act Two (Snyder, ch. 4). → `15 Beats/15 - Final Image.md`

## 6. Proof case: *Miss Congeniality*

### Answering the skeptics
Snyder expects younger writers to say the beats are old-fashioned, that heroes need not be likable, and to point to *Memento*. He answers with a recent hit in his own PG-comedy field (Snyder, ch. 4).

### The film
He picks *Miss Congeniality* because it has a strong title, and its logline (an unglamorous FBI agent goes undercover in a beauty pageant to catch a killer) meets all four ch. 1 criteria. His beat-by-beat mapping, condensed (Snyder, ch. 4):
- **Opening Image:** the heroine as a child, a playground tomboy fighting boys. In the present she is still among men, as an FBI agent.
- **Theme Stated:** her claim that an agent need not worry about being feminine. The film is an essay on whether a woman can be tough and feminine at once.
- **Set-Up:** by page 10 we meet the A-story cast: her crush, who prefers "classy" women; her boss; the macho FBI world. She is a happy loner, and he labels this "Stasis = Death."
- **Catalyst:** a threat against the pageant; we meet its organizers and host; a plan needs a female agent undercover, and she is chosen.
- **Debate:** can she pull it off? Answered through comic scenes with a pageant coach (Michael Caine) who takes on her makeover.
- **Break into Two:** she emerges from the makeover looking glamorous, then stumbles; not easy, but she commits.
- **Fun and Games:** the premise in action: a gun-carrying agent among contestants (the water-glass talent act that ends in a tackle). The fish out of water.
- **B Story:** the real love story is with the other contestants. They embody the theme of femininity; they turn out to need and like her, and she grows through them.
- **Midpoint:** a new threat is announced, the fun ends, and the stakes rise.
- **Bad Guys Close In:** her self-doubt grows, friction with the coach worsens, and the hidden villains draw nearer.
- **All Is Lost:** her boss tells her to quit or be fired, and she stays. She is worse off than at the start. Her whiff of death is a dying identity: who is she without the badge? Her mentor gives her a last "weapon," a dress.
- **Dark Night:** she arrives at the final night lost between agent and woman.
- **Break into Three:** the contestants put her back together; in helping her they help themselves.
- **Finale:** at the pageant she uses FBI skills in the talent round, fusing both worlds and answering the theme: she can be both. She catches the culprits (the organizer and her son, driven by the organizer's warped idea of femininity).
- **Final Image:** the reverse of the opening: surrounded by women, voted Miss Congeniality by her peers.

He adds a joke beat, "the real happy ending": about $100 million at the domestic box office ⚠️ (his figure). Note that his breakdown lists Fun and Games before B Story, while the form numbers B Story 7 and Fun and Games 8.

## Exercises
(Snyder, ch. 4; described, not copied)
1. **Carry the sheet:** type up the BS2, keep it with you, and in idle moments try to fit a favorite film into fifteen one-sentence beats.
2. **Fill it from your genre:** watch 6–12 films in your genre and fill in the BS2 for each.
3. **The *Memento* challenge:** for extra credit, test whether *Memento* (which he classes as Dude with a Problem) fits the beats or is a gimmick that cannot be repeated. His hint is to look up how much it earned, and he invites debate by email.

## Terms introduced
- **Beating it out:** laying out a story's beats before writing.
- **Structure:** the beat skeleton of a script. After concept, the most important quality (see Glossary).
- **Credit jumper:** a later writer who alters a script to claim credit (see Glossary).
- **Act break:** the turn from one act to the next.
- **Blake Snyder Beat Sheet / BS2:** his fifteen-beat, one-page structure form.
- **The fifteen beats:** Opening Image, Theme Stated, Set-Up, Catalyst, Debate, Break into Two, B Story, Fun and Games, Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost, Dark Night of the Soul, Break into Three, Finale, Final Image.
- **Thematic premise:** the film's debated question or statement, raised around page 5.
- **Six Things That Need Fixing:** the shown list of the hero's lacks and flaws, planted in the set-up and paid off later.
- **Thesis / antithesis / synthesis:** Acts One, Two and Three as the world as it is, its upside-down version, and their fusion.
- **Stasis = Death:** the feeling, planted early, that if the hero's world stays as it is, he is finished; change must come.
- **Booster rocket:** a lift (often the B story or a new character) past a potential slow stretch.
- **Promise of the premise:** the scenes that deliver what the poster and premise promised.
- **False victory / false defeat:** the two kinds of midpoint, with All Is Lost as the inverse.
- **Stakes are raised:** the midpoint escalation.
- **Whiff of death:** a literal or symbolic death placed at All Is Lost.
- **Ascending order (of dispatch):** in the finale, minor villains are defeated before the chief one.
