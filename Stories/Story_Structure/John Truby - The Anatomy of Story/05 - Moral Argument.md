# Moral Argument (Truby, ch. 5)

## 1. Why theme matters, and the "message" problem (p. 52)

Truby opens with the old Hollywood joke, credited to Samuel Goldwyn, that anyone with a message should use a telegraph company rather than a movie. He agrees with half of it: a message delivered bluntly and preachily is a mistake. But he argues that stories whose themes are handled well are both more respected and more popular. A great story is not a string of entertaining events or surprises; it is a chain of actions that carry moral consequences, arranged to express something larger (Truby, PDF p. 52).

## 2. Theme is not subject matter (p. 52)

Most people treat theme as a topic: death, love, corruption, redemption, class, good and evil, and so on. Truby rejects that definition. For him, theme means **how the writer believes a person ought to act in the world**, which he calls the writer's **moral vision**. The moment a character uses some means to reach an end, the story is posing a moral predicament, asking what the right action is, and arguing for a way to live. That vision belongs to the writer alone, and conveying it is a chief purpose of telling the story at all (Truby, PDF p. 52).

## 3. Theme as the brain of the story (p. 52)

Truby continues his metaphor of the story as a living body made of subsystems that hang together and also connect to one another. Character was the heart and circulation; structure is the skeleton; theme plays the part of the **brain**, since it holds the overarching design. In that role it should steer the writing, but it must not take over so completely that the story turns into a philosophy paper (Truby, PDF p. 52).

## 4. How much theme different forms carry (p. 52)

The weight of moral vision varies by form:
- **Heavily thematic forms:** drama, allegory, irony, "serious" literary fiction and religious stories. These build a complex moral vision and use dialogue that brings out the contradictions in the characters' moral position.
- **Lightly thematic forms:** adventure, myth, fantasy and action. These put nearly everything into surprise, suspense, imagination and the characters' emotional and psychological states, and say little about their moral difficulties (Truby, PDF p. 52).

## 5. The preachy trap and the mouthpiece character (p. 52)

Whatever the form, weaker writers push their moral vision almost entirely through dialogue, so the "morals" smother the story. Truby's examples of stories faulted as preachy and too on-the-nose are *Gandhi* and *Guess Who's Coming to Dinner?* At worst, such stories feel heavy and lecturing, and audiences pull away from the clumsy narrative. Never let a character become a mouthpiece for the writer's ideas. Skilled writers let the moral vision come out slowly and quietly, mainly through structure and through how the hero handles a specific situation: how he chases his goal against one or more opponents, and what he learns, or fails to learn, along the way (Truby, PDF p. 52).

## 6. The argument of action (p. 52)

The writer is in effect arguing a moral case through what the characters do in the plot. Truby calls this the **argument of action**, and the rest of the chapter explains how to build it (Truby, PDF p. 52).

## 7. The theme line (pp. 52-53)

**What it is.** The first step is to boil the theme down to one sentence. The **theme line** states the writer's view of which actions are right or wrong and what such actions do to a person's life (Truby, PDF pp. 52-53).

**Its limits and its value.** A single line can't capture a nuanced moral vision and may sound heavy-handed. Its value is that it forces every moral element of the story to converge on one moral idea (Truby, PDF p. 53).

### 7a. Getting the theme line from the designing principle (p. 53)

As with the premise (ch. 2), the seed of the theme line is the **designing principle**, the organizing idea that makes the story's actions organic. To find the theme line, look at the story's actions purely for their **moral effects**: how do the characters' deeds injure others, and do they ever repair the damage? The same designing-principle techniques that deepen a premise also open up a theme (Truby, PDF p. 53). Truby gives three:

- **Traveling (the journey).** A journey can hold a whole moral sequence inside one line, because the physical trip doubles as a moral trip. Examples: Huck's raft trip down the Mississippi also carries him deeper into slavery (*Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*); Marlow's trip upriver is also a descent into moral confusion (*Heart of Darkness*); in *King Kong* the trip from Manhattan to Skull Island looks like a move from civilization to raw nature, yet once the expedition is back in Manhattan the real theme shows: both islands run on ruthless competition, and the human one is crueler (Truby, PDF p. 53).
- **A single grand symbol.** One symbol can carry the theme line or the central moral element. In *The Scarlet Letter*, Hester Prynne's letter A stands first for her adultery, where the story starts, and then for the deeper wrong the story exposes: townsfolk who conceal their own failings and use conformist law to crush real love. Hemingway's *For Whom the Bell Tolls* uses the bell to signal death, but the title points back to John Donne's meditation that no one is an island; that image of the individual within a community organizes the story and implies a theme along the lines of: facing death, life gets its meaning from sacrifice for the people you love (Truby, PDF p. 53).
- **Two grand symbols joined in a one-line process.** Linking two symbols works like the journey: each symbol is one pole of a moral sequence. It usually signals moral decline, though it can run upward. *Heart of Darkness* combines this with the journey: both halves of its title, a heart that is dark and a darkness at the core, point to an inquiry into human depravity (Truby, PDF p. 53).

Other designing-principle devices also help clarify a theme line: units of time, a storyteller, or some special way the story unfolds (Truby, PDF p. 53).

### 7b. Worked examples: designing principle to theme line (pp. 53-54)

Truby returns to the eleven stories whose designing principles he set out in ch. 2 and proposes a theme line for each. In our paraphrase:

| Story | Gist of Truby's theme line |
|---|---|
| Moses (Exodus) | Accepting responsibility for your people is repaid with God's guidance on how to live. |
| *Ulysses* | Real heroism is enduring ordinary daily hardship and being compassionate to someone who needs help. |
| *Four Weddings and a Funeral* | Once you find your true love, commit to that person completely. |
| *Harry Potter* | Great talent and power oblige you to lead and to sacrifice for others. |
| *The Sting* | Some lying and cheating is acceptable if it topples a wicked man. |
| *Long Day's Journey into Night* | Confront the truth about yourself and the people around you, and then forgive them. |
| *Meet Me in St. Louis* | Sacrifice for family outranks the pursuit of personal glory. |
| *Copenhagen* | We can never be sure why we act or whether it is right. |
| *A Christmas Carol* | Giving to others makes for a far happier life. |
| *It's a Wonderful Life* | A man's wealth lies in the people he serves, family and friends, not in money. |
| *Citizen Kane* | Trying to force everyone to love you leaves you alone. |

(Truby, PDF pp. 53-54)

## 8. Splitting the theme into oppositions (pp. 54-56)

A theme line is the moral argument squeezed into one sentence. To make it dramatic, you break it into opposing pieces, the **thematic oppositions**, and pin them on the hero and opponents during their struggle. Truby gives three techniques for this: the hero's moral decision, characters as variations on the theme, and the characters' values in conflict (Truby, PDF p. 54).

### 8a. Technique 1: the hero's moral decision (pp. 54-55)

**The moral frame.** The hero's moral development runs between two endpoints: where he starts, with a **moral need**, and where he ends, with a **moral self-revelation** and then a **moral decision**. Truby calls this line the story's **moral frame**; it traces the core moral lesson the writer wants to convey (Truby, PDF p. 54).

**The classic strategy.** Give the hero a moral flaw at the outset, then show how his frantic need to defeat the opponent draws out his worst traits. He must decline before he can improve. Bit by bit he comes to see that his central moral problem boils down to choosing between two courses of conduct (Truby, PDF p. 54).

**The decision as the funnel's neck.** However complicated the story's actions are, the final moral decision reduces everything to a choice between two options, and the choice is final. The two options are the most important moral actions open to the hero, so together they give the **primary thematic opposition** of the whole story (Truby, PDF p. 54).

**Timing.** Normally the decision follows directly on the moral self-revelation, which tells the hero which way to choose. Occasionally the order flips: the hero chooses first, and his self-revelation is the recognition that the choice was right or wrong (Truby, PDF p. 54).

**Key point.** Because the hero's moral line ends with that final choice, start working out the story's moral oppositions from the choice itself (Truby, PDF p. 54).

**Examples (pp. 54-55):**
- *Casablanca*: Rick could escape to America with Ilsa on the two exit visas; he chooses the fight against the Nazis over her.
- *The Maltese Falcon*: Spade learns Brigid killed his partner and, when the police arrive, picks justice over Brigid, whom he loves.
- *Sophie's Choice*: on arrival at Auschwitz, Sophie must pick which of her children the Nazis will kill, a choice between two negatives; Truby notes one could argue it is not a real choice.
- *The Iliad*: after killing Hector and dragging the body, Achilles lets Priam take it for proper burial.
- *Vertigo*: the decision precedes the self-revelation. Scottie refuses to forgive Madeleine, and the realization that this wrong choice caused his lover's death destroys him (Truby, PDF pp. 54-55).

### 8b. Technique 2: variations on a theme (p. 55)

Once the hero's final choice has shown you the deepest moral opposition, spread that opposition across the character web, so that every major character becomes a different take on the theme. Truby's sequence, in summary:
1. Revisit the final moral decision and the premise work so the hero's **central moral problem** is clear.
2. Have every major character face that same moral problem, each in a different way.
3. Begin with hero versus main opponent, since they embody the main moral opposition; then compare the hero with each other opponent.
4. Over the story, let each major character argue, in dialogue, for what he does to reach the goal. Truby adds that moral argument is mostly, but not only, structural; moral dialogue is covered in ch. 10 (Truby, PDF p. 55).

**Examples:**
- *Tootsie*: an actor disguised as a woman to get TV work. The central moral problem is a man's treatment of a woman he loves; every opponent and ally shows a variation on how men behave toward women, or on what women will accept from men.
- *L.A. Confidential*: three detectives, all main characters, share one moral problem, how justice is carried out. Bud acts as judge and executioner on his own; Jack has forgotten why he joined and arrests people for cash; Ed wants justice but has become absorbed in playing politics to rise. Every other major character embodies another way justice gets corrupted.
- *Dances with Wolves*: an army officer drawn into Sioux life. The central moral problem concerns his conduct toward a different race and culture, and toward animals and the land; every opponent and ally handles it in its own way (Truby, PDF p. 55).

### 8c. Technique 3: the characters' values in conflict (pp. 55-56)

Use the character web to set the major characters' **values** against each other while they compete for the same goal. Values, for Truby, are the deep convictions a person holds about what a good life is. His steps, in summary: name a set of values for the hero and each major character; give each character a cluster of values rather than one; make each cluster as unlike the others as possible; and make sure those values collide directly as hero and opponents fight over the goal (Truby, PDF p. 55).

**Examples:**
- *It's a Wonderful Life*: George Bailey and Potter compete over their town. George stands for democracy, decency, kindness, hard work and the worth of ordinary workers (Bedford Falls); Potter stands for one-man rule, money, power and survival of the fittest (Pottersville) (Truby, PDF p. 55).
- *The Cherry Orchard*: the competition is over who controls a debt-ridden estate, focused on what the orchard is worth. Madame Ranevsky's family prizes its beauty and its link to their past; Lopakhin sees only its cash value and wants to cut it down for rental cottages. Truby also gives value clusters for Varya, Trofimov and Anya, each distinct (Truby, PDF pp. 55-56).
- *Field of Dreams*: Truby calls it an American version of *The Cherry Orchard*, except that here the "orchard" comes out on top. Ray values baseball, family and following your dreams; Mark values money and practical use of the land (Truby, PDF p. 56).

### 8d. Four-corner opposition as a moral web (p. 56)

With variations on a theme and opposed values, the **four-corner opposition** from ch. 4 (hero, main opponent, and two or more secondary opponents) is especially useful. Each corner can embody a basically different way of handling the single moral problem and carry a whole value system, which gives even a complex story organic unity without turning it into a muddle (Truby, PDF p. 56).

**Key point.** A two-sided opposition such as good versus evil will always make the moral argument simplistic. Only a **web** of moral oppositions, of which four-corner opposition is one kind, conveys how morally complicated real life is (Truby, PDF p. 56).

### 8e. Why these techniques avoid preachiness (p. 56)

All three techniques express the theme **through** the characters rather than laying it on top of them, so the story doesn't feel preachy. They also deepen the story: the characters are opposed not only over a plot goal but over entire ways of living, which raises the emotional stakes for the audience considerably (Truby, PDF p. 56).

## 9. Moral argument through story structure (pp. 56-57)

**Not a debate scene.** Moral argument does not mean the hero and opponent trade speeches about ethics in scene one. It is an argument of action, made by showing the hero and opponent using particular means to reach a goal. That is how theme gets woven through structure rather than preached in dialogue (Truby, PDF p. 56).

**Structure is content.** Truby states one of his core principles: structure is not just a container; in his words, it "is content," and more powerful content than anything the characters say. He says theme is where this is most true (Truby, PDF p. 56).

**Converging structure, expanding theme.** In well-built stories, the structure narrows toward the end while the theme widens in the audience's mind (Truby, PDF pp. 56-57).

*The diagram (p. 57, an image in the book; described from the page itself).* Three labels run across the top: hero on the left, theme in the middle, opponent on the right. Two arrows start at the hero and opponent labels, slant toward each other, cross, and close in on a single point near the bottom center: the structure converging. Between them, the theme starts as a single point under its label and opens into two lines that spread wider as they descend: the theme expanding. Beneath the point where everything meets, three captions are stacked: the battle and the endpoint of the goal, then the moral self-revelation, then the moral decision. In other words, the picture shows hero-versus-opponent conflict narrowing to the battle at the very moment the theme reaches its widest reach (Truby, PDF p. 57).

The prose walks through the same movement:
- **Beginning:** hero and opponent are set against each other, but the conflict is mild and the audience can't yet see how their values clash, so it has almost no sense of theme.
- **Middle:** conflict intensifies (the structure converges), a difference in values emerges, and the theme starts to grow. Through most of a strong story the theme stays largely out of sight, growing quietly in the audience's mind.
- **End:** the convergence point is the **battle**, followed closely by the hero's self-revelation and then his moral decision. During the battle, viewers see which values, not just which force, prove stronger, and their grasp of the theme widens sharply. It widens again at the self-revelation (especially a moral one) and again at the moral decision. Because the theme came through structure, it feels as if it rose from inside the audience rather than being imposed like a sermon (Truby, PDF p. 57).

## 10. Basic strategy: the default moral sequence (pp. 57-58)

Truby lays out the default sequence of moral beats across a whole story. In our words:

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Values** | The hero starts out holding certain beliefs and values. |
| **Moral weakness** | At the start the hero is hurting others somehow, not out of evil but from weakness or from not knowing how to treat people properly. |
| **Moral need** | Because of that weakness, he has to learn to treat others rightly if he is to grow and have a better life. |
| **First immoral action** | Very early he does something that harms others, which shows the audience his basic moral flaw. |
| **Desire** | He settles on a goal that everything else is sacrificed to. It pits him against an opponent chasing the same prize but holding different values. |
| **Drive** | Hero and opponent each act repeatedly to reach the goal. |
| **Immoral actions** (with **criticism** and **justification**) | Through the early and middle parts the opponent generally has the upper hand; the hero grows desperate and starts acting immorally to win. Other characters criticize his methods; he justifies them. He may grasp what is truly right by the end, but not yet. |
| **Attack by ally** | The hero's best friend makes a forceful case against his methods. |
| **Obsessive drive** | Spurred by fresh discoveries about winning, he grows obsessed and will do nearly anything to succeed. |
| **Immoral actions, intensified** | His wrongdoing escalates; the criticism grows; he defends himself more fiercely. |

(Truby, PDF pp. 57-58)

As the sequence plays out, action and dialogue make plain how differently hero and opponent believe one should live. Then the theme "explodes" for the audience at four points near the end (Truby, PDF p. 58):

| Beat | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Battle** | The last conflict settles who gets the goal. Win or lose, it shows the audience whose values and ideas are better. |
| **Final action against opponent** | Right before the battle or in the middle of it, the hero may make a last move, moral or not, against the opponent. |
| **Moral self-revelation** | The pressure of the battle forces the hero to see that he has misjudged himself and mistreated others, and to see how he should treat people. Because the audience identifies with him, this lands the theme hard. |
| **Moral decision** | The hero picks between two courses of action, which proves the self-revelation. |
| **Thematic revelation** | A new structure step. It belongs not to the hero but to the audience: an insight into how everyone, not just these characters, ought to behave and live. It reaches past these particular characters into the audience's own lives, and shows them the story's "total design" and its full implications on a much larger scale (Truby, PDF p. 58). |

### 10a. Balance of power applies to the moral argument too (p. 58)

Hero and main opponent must be evenly matched morally, not just in plot and character. A hero who is too powerful or too virtuous never gets tested hard enough by the opponent to make moral mistakes. An overpowering opponent facing a naive or unaware hero turns into a **spider**, spinning a web the hero can't escape: the hero becomes a victim and the opponent reads as evil (Truby, PDF p. 58).

*Example:* Henry James's *The Portrait of a Lady* is masterful but suffers here. Isabel Archer deceives herself throughout, even when she finally resolves to aid Pansy, who is beyond help; she is no match for Osmond, a master schemer who enjoys spinning his web, and the moral argument weakens accordingly (Truby, PDF p. 58).

### 10b. Keeping moral argument and plot in balance (p. 58)

Truby calls too much moral argument for the amount of plot the biggest single cause of preachiness. Even a perfectly sequenced structural argument, backed by subtle moral dialogue, will collapse into a sermon if there isn't enough plot to hold it up. Plot, defined more fully in ch. 8, is the intricate choreography of hero's and opponents' actions designed to surprise the audience; that surprise is what keeps the moral sequence afloat and lends it impact (Truby, PDF p. 58).

### 10c. Example: *The Verdict*, and a lesson in timing (pp. 58-59)

Truby walks through *The Verdict* beat by beat. In brief: Frank, the hero, starts out valuing alcohol, money and expedience; his weakness is that, addicted and hopeless, he'll do anything for money; his moral need is to treat people justly rather than use them. His first immoral act is crashing a funeral, posing as a friend of the deceased to drum up business. His desire is to win his trial and get his clients their damages. The immoral actions that follow include planning to settle so he can pocket a third for no work, tricking a woman into talking about a missing nurse, breaking into a mailbox to get the nurse's phone number, and hitting Laura on learning she was spying for the other side. The ally's attack comes not from his colleague Mickey but from his clients, who call him incompetent and immoral for turning down the settlement without asking them. The battle is the trial, where the nurse's testimony is disallowed; Frank's final action against the opponent is nothing immoral, just shrewd lawyering. Truby's thematic revelation: only by acting justly can our lives be saved (Truby, PDF pp. 58-59).

**The instructive flaw.** The moral self-revelation (seeing his comatose client and grasping what he nearly turned his back on) and the moral decision (refusing the settlement to seek justice) come only about twenty-five minutes into the film. From then on the hero is in no **moral jeopardy**; the audience still wonders whether he'll win, but knows he has already learned to act justly. Truby's lesson: moral argument is strongest when most dramatic, which means delaying the moral self-revelation and decision as near to the end as you can, and keeping the audience wondering whether the hero will do the right thing, and do it in time (Truby, PDF p. 59).

### 10d. Example: the *Iliad*, the sequence run twice (pp. 59-60)

The *Iliad* follows the basic pattern of gradual moral decline and recovery at the self-revelation, but runs it twice:
- **First cycle (about the first three-quarters):** Achilles is right to be angry when Agamemnon takes his war prize, a woman he had won, but excessive pride (his moral weakness) makes him overreact by sitting out the fighting, and many comrades die. He grows more unjust and selfish until the death of Patroklos makes him see his guilt; he makes peace with Agamemnon and rejoins the fighting (first self-revelation and decision).
- **Second cycle (last quarter, shorter and more intense):** Achilles is at first justified in his fury at Hector, then sinks again when he desecrates Hector's body. When Priam begs for Hector's body, Achilles reaches a second and much deeper self-revelation, that compassion matters more than vengeance, and lets Priam take the body for burial (Truby, PDF pp. 59-60).

## 11. Variants of moral argument (pp. 60-64)

The basic strategy has several variants, depending on form, story and writer. More than one may suit a story, but Truby warns that combining them is risky (Truby, PDF p. 60).

### 11a. Variant 1: good versus bad (p. 60)

The simplest variant: the hero stays good and the opponent stays bad all the way through. It is typical of myth, action and melodrama, forms built as simple moral tales with readily recognizable characters. The pattern: the hero has flaws of a psychological kind but is basically good; the opponent is morally flawed or outright evil; competing for the goal, the hero errs but never acts immorally, while the opponent commits a series of immoral acts; the hero wins simply because he is good, as though the moral ledger were totaled up and the good side won life's "game" (Truby, PDF p. 60).

*Examples Truby lists include:* *Star Wars*, *The Matrix*, *The Wizard of Oz*, *Shane*, *The Terminator*, *Forrest Gump* and *Field of Dreams*, among others (Truby, PDF p. 60).

### 11b. Variant 2: tragedy (pp. 60-62)

Tragedy keeps the basic strategy but twists both ends: a **fatal character flaw** from the outset, and a self-revelation that arrives **too late**, close to the end. The pattern, in summary: a community in crisis; a hero with huge potential and an equally large flaw; deep conflict with a strong opponent; an obsession with winning that leads to questionable or immoral acts; a conflict that exposes the flaw and shows the hero worsening; self-knowledge that comes too late to avert destruction (Truby, PDF p. 60).

**The key.** Heighten the feeling of **might-have-been**, the lost potential, while making clear the hero's actions are his own responsibility. Truby calls might-have-been the most important element for winning audience sympathy, while the fatal flaw keeps the hero responsible and stops him becoming a victim. The audience grieves the waste, sharpened by the insight arriving just minutes too late, yet leaves inspired by the hero's moral and emotional success even in death or downfall (Truby, PDF p. 60).

**A shift from Greek drama.** In this form the hero's fall comes from his own choices, not from vast impersonal forces, which Truby marks as a crucial change from classical Greek tragedy (Truby, PDF p. 60).

*Examples Truby lists include:* *Hamlet*, *Othello*, *King Lear*, *Citizen Kane*, *Vertigo*, *Amadeus* and *American Beauty*, among others (Truby, PDF p. 60).

**Extended example: *Wuthering Heights* (pp. 60-61).** Truby reads it (novel and 1939 film) as a love story built as tragedy, with many strands in which characters do devastating things to each other and all are broken by their sense of responsibility. Key points:
- Cathy, the hero, is not a passive girl; she freely trades a great love for wealth and comfort, refusing to live poor with Heathcliff.
- The moral back-and-forth plays out in specific scenes: she wounds Heathcliff after returning from the Lintons', then defends him to Edgar and begs his forgiveness; then, with Nellie (the ally) leading the criticism, she says marrying Heathcliff would degrade her, not knowing he is listening.
- Truby praises how Brontë pairs strong moral dialogue with a highly emotional plot beat: Heathcliff leaves, unseen by Cathy, just before she reverses herself and declares that she and Heathcliff are one. That self-revelation comes too late.
- Brontë then effectively **swaps heroes**, giving Heathcliff the lead. He returns rich and polished (Truby's name for this is the "return of the man," Monte Cristo style), and the audience relishes his revenge until Brontë flips the moral argument: Heathcliff goes too far, marrying Isabella to strike at his enemies.
- The ferocity of the two lovers' attacks on each other is what makes their heaven-made love believable.
- Truby judges that the novel's later section, in which Heathcliff goes on to try to destroy the Linton line, is overkill once the organic Cathy-Heathcliff story is done, which is why the classic film cut it (Truby, PDF pp. 60-61).

**Extended example: *King Lear* (pp. 61-62).** Shakespeare makes a subtler moral argument than most tragedy by creating **two heroes**, Lear and the subplot's Gloucester. Both start with moral flaws, decline, reach moral self-revelations, and die; yet there is no sense of a noble death or restored order. Two kings making the same mistakes and dying without pity suggests a blindness endemic to humanity, where one fall alone would inspire. Killing Cordelia, the one truly good character, cruelly, adds to the bleakness. Edgar has beaten his brother and Lear's two daughters, but in the wreckage only a thin trace of what a good life is worth survives, captured in the play's last line about the young never seeing or living so much. For late Shakespeare, Truby says, humans can't be expected to manage much more nobility than that (Truby, PDF pp. 61-62).

### 11c. Variant 3: pathos (p. 62)

Pathos scales the tragic hero down to an ordinary man and moves the audience with the beauty of persistence, of lost causes, of the man who is doomed. The hero doesn't get a late self-revelation; he is incapable of one, but he keeps fighting to the end. The pattern, in summary (Truby, PDF p. 62):
- His beliefs and values have atrophied: they are outdated or rigid.
- He does have a moral need; he isn't merely a victim.
- His goal is beyond reach, unknown to him.
- The opposition vastly outmatches him and could be a whole system or set of forces beyond his comprehension; it is not evil, just impersonal, uncaring and very strong.
- He acts immorally to win and ignores his allies' warnings and criticism.
- He fails; the opponent wins overwhelmingly, but the audience senses the fight was unfair.
- He ends in despair, broken, with no self-revelation, dying of heartbreak or, as all that is left of his moral decision, killing himself.
- The audience feels the world's injustice and sorrow for the little man who never understood what hit him, but also admiration for the beautiful failure and his refusal to give in.

*Examples Truby lists include:* *Death of a Salesman*, *Ikiru*, *Don Quixote*, *Hedda Gabler*, *A Streetcar Named Desire*, *The Conversation* and *Madame Bovary*, among others (Truby, PDF p. 62).

### 11d. Variant 4: satire and irony (pp. 62-63)

**Definitions.** The two are distinct but often paired. **Satire** is comedy aimed at beliefs, particularly the ones a whole society is built on. **Irony** is a kind of story logic where a character's efforts bring him the reverse of what he is after. Used across a whole story rather than in a moment, irony becomes a grand pattern linking every action and expressing a whole philosophy of the way the world operates. It also carries a bemused tone that lets the audience chuckle at how inept the characters are (Truby, PDF p. 62).

**How the argument works.** You keep contrasting a character who believes he is being moral, upholding his society's beliefs, with the results of his actions and beliefs, which are plainly immoral (Truby, PDF p. 62). The sequence, in summary:
- The hero lives in a clearly defined social system, and usually someone explains the values it rests on.
- The hero believes in the system, wants to rise to the top, and pursues a goal of ambition or love.
- A rival just as devoted to the system pursues the same goal.
- Competing, both are led by their beliefs into silly and destructive acts.
- The middle is driven by juxtaposition: characters claiming to act on society's highest ideals, set against disastrous results.
- The battle exposes pretension and hypocrisy on both sides.
- The hero's self-revelation usually questions the system's beliefs, but the hero or someone else often undercuts it, showing it hasn't truly sunk in.
- The hero takes a personally right moral action that usually changes nothing about the system's folly.
- A marriage of love or friendship suggests the pair will make a better little world of their own while leaving society largely as it was (Truby, PDF p. 62).

*Examples Truby lists include:* *Pride and Prejudice*, *Emma* and *Clueless*, *M\*A\*S\*H*, *The Graduate*, *The Player*, *Being John Malkovich* and *Trading Places*, among others (Truby, PDF pp. 62-63).

**Extended example: *Emma* (p. 63).** Truby calls Austen the master of this form and *Emma* her finest work. In outline: Emma is headstrong, self-righteous and socially blind, always matchmaking. She talks the orphan Harriet out of accepting the farmer Robert Martin and into hoping for the rector Mr. Elton, while unwittingly making Elton think she herself is interested. The results are bad all round. Austen focuses the argument through two matching scenes in which someone is socially snubbed: Elton, now married, refusing to dance with Harriet at a ball until Mr. Knightley partners her; and Emma cruelly mocking Miss Bates at a picnic, for which Knightley rebukes her. When Emma learns Harriet has her eye on Knightley, she realizes she loves him herself and sees what a meddler she has been. Points Truby draws out:
- Austen sketches a system of rigid class with women wholly dependent on men, and quietly undercuts it by making the "beneath her" farmer a good man.
- The two key scenes argue for a morality of simple human kindness rather than social rank, and avoid sermonizing because they hurt to watch, and Knightley's corrections feel good.
- The closing marriage reaffirms the system (the two are of equal high rank) yet subtly undercuts it, because they come together through Emma's maturing and Knightley's character, not class.
- In the novel, though not the film, even the marriage and Emma's self-revelation are undercut: she can marry only because her father, afraid of chicken thieves, wants a younger man in the house (Truby, PDF p. 63).

### 11e. Variant 5: black comedy (pp. 63-64)

**Definition.** Black comedy finds its humor in the logic of a system, or rather its illogic. Truby calls it advanced and difficult. It shows destruction arising less from individual choice (as in tragedy) than from people caught inside a system that is destructive by nature. Its defining move: **the self-revelation is withheld from the hero so that the audience receives it more strongly** (Truby, PDF p. 63).

**The sequence, in summary (p. 64):**
- Many characters inhabit an organization, and someone explains its rules and logic in great detail.
- Many of them, hero included, pursue a destructive goal, such as a killing or the wrecking of something.
- Each fully believes the goal makes sense; in fact it is completely illogical.
- Opponents inside the same system chase the same goal with elaborate yet crazy justifications.
- A lone sane voice, typically the ally, keeps pointing out that none of it makes sense and it will end in disaster. He acts as a chorus, and nobody listens.
- Everyone, the nominal hero included, uses extreme, even murderous methods, which bring death and destruction to nearly all.
- The battle is fierce and ruinous; everyone still thinks he is right; the outcome is death and madness.
- Nobody gains a self-revelation; the hero so clearly ought to have had one that the audience gets it in his place.
- The survivors, badly maimed, go right back to chasing the goal.
- In somewhat more hopeful versions, the sane person watches in horror and then leaves the system or tries to change it (Truby, PDF p. 64).

**Pitfalls and fixes.** The form is easy to botch. Make the hero **likable**, or the comedy turns into an abstract intellectual essay and the audience feels superior to the characters; you want them drawn in until they discover they *are* these people. Letting the hero argue with passion for the reasoning behind his goal also pulls the audience in. To add hope, give the lone sane person a fully worked-out alternative to the madness (Truby, PDF p. 64).

*Examples Truby lists include:* *Dr. Strangelove*, *Catch-22*, *Network*, *Goodfellas*, *Wag the Dog* and *Brazil*, among others (Truby, PDF p. 64).

## 12. Combining moral arguments (p. 64)

The variants are distinct but not mutually exclusive, and advanced storytellers sometimes combine them. *Ulysses* starts with the myth's simple good-versus-bad argument and deepens it with satire-irony; *The Cherry Orchard* blends pathos with satire-irony. *American Beauty*, mixing tragedy with satire, irony and black comedy, shows how hard this is: Truby finds it brilliant in many ways but never fully realized as any of the three. The variants differ for a reason; they work differently and produce different emotional effects, so blending them seamlessly takes exceptional mastery. Further mixed examples: *Dog Day Afternoon*, *Madame Bovary* and *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (Truby, PDF p. 64).

## 13. The unique moral vision (pp. 64-65)

The most advanced level is a writer who builds a **unique moral vision**. Hawthorne's *The Scarlet Letter* uses a three-person opposition to argue for a natural morality grounded in real love. Joyce, in *Ulysses*, builds a natural religion and a heroism of ordinary days by following a symbolic "father" and "son" through a single day in Dublin. Truby stresses that this is not moral argument alone: these writers' craft shows in their webs of character, plot, symbol and story world, as broad and detailed as their moral arguments (Truby, PDF p. 64).

A few blockbusters have one too, and Truby says their success is not mainly about visual effects. *Star Wars* blends Eastern and Western morality, pairing a Western-style hero with a knightly order of Zen flavor and the Force; less advanced than Hawthorne or Joyce, but the attempt, and its brevity, gave it universal appeal, and its famous blessing became a creed for many viewers. *The Godfather* both portrays the 1940s Mafia and lays out a moral system modeled on modern business and modern war; its famous lines work as a catechism for a present-day, American-flavored version of Machiavelli's *The Prince*. Both deal in moral shorthand, but setting out a moral system with some success is a large part of why they appeal (Truby, PDF pp. 64-65).

## 14. Moral argument in dialogue (p. 65)

Structure is the main vehicle, but dialogue is also needed. When structure carries the weight of the moral case, dialogue is free for its real strengths: subtlety and emotional force. (Detailed technique is deferred to ch. 10.) Truby names three prime places for moral dialogue (Truby, PDF p. 65):

1. **Ally criticizes hero.** The most common: the ally objects to an immoral action the hero takes to win, and the hero, who hasn't had his self-revelation, defends himself.
2. **Hero versus opponent.** Can occur anywhere but most often in a battle scene; Truby's example is Fast Eddie and Bert in *The Hustler*. *It's a Wonderful Life* places one much earlier, when George stops Potter from shutting down his father's Building and Loan. An early clash has the advantage of telling the audience which values are really at stake, so the drama can build.
3. **The opponent's own moral justification.** A mark of strong writing: a scene where the main opponent justifies his conduct on moral grounds, despite being in the wrong.

**Why the opponent's justification matters.** A purely evil opponent is inherently bad, which makes him mechanical and dull. Real conflicts rarely divide cleanly into good and evil. In strong stories, hero and opponent alike are convinced they are in the right and have reasons for it, and both are also wrong, in different ways. A strong (though mistaken) justification gives the opponent depth, and since a hero can be no better than the opponent he faces, it deepens the hero too (Truby, PDF p. 65).

*Examples:* in *The Verdict*, opposing attorney Concannon tells his paid spy that their job is to win; Colonel Jessup in *A Few Good Men* defends ordering a Marine's killing by casting himself as the last defense against the barbarians at the gate; and in Thornton Wilder's script for *Shadow of a Doubt*, the serial killer Uncle Charlie justifies murdering widows by likening them to overfed animals (Truby, PDF p. 65).

**The key: no straw man.** Don't give the opponent a hollow case that only looks formidable. Give him the strongest argument you can, let him be right about some things, and make sure his logic contains one fatal flaw (Truby, PDF p. 65).

## 15. Exercises: Writing Exercise 4, "Outlining the Moral Argument" (pp. 65-67)

The exercise has two parts.

**Part 1: building the theme and its oppositions (p. 65).** The writer is asked to:
- turn the story's designing principle into a one-sentence theme line about right and wrong action, focusing on the principle's main actions and what they do morally;
- look for techniques, such as symbols, that compress the moral statement into a line or capture the story's unique structure;
- note the crucial choice the hero faces near the end;
- after reviewing the premise work, state in a single line the central moral problem the hero faces throughout;
- describe how each major character, starting with hero and main opponent, approaches that problem differently;
- list each major character's key values and explain how they will collide as each pursues the goal.

**Part 2: the moral argument through structure (pp. 65-66).** The writer then works through the basic-strategy sequence for his own story: restating the hero's beliefs and values; naming his main weakness toward others; stating what he must learn about acting and living rightly; describing his first harmful action, which must grow from that weakness; restating his goal and listing the actions he takes for it; judging which of those actions are immoral and, for each, the criticism and the hero's justification; detailing the ally's main moral attack and the hero's defense; pinpointing when the hero becomes obsessed with winning and what immoral steps follow, with criticism and justification; showing how the battle reveals whose values are superior; deciding whether the hero makes one last move against the opponent; stating what, if anything, the hero learns morally (it must be about treating others rightly); checking whether he faces a two-way decision near the end; and looking for a story event that conveys the writer's vision of how people should act other than through the hero's self-revelation (the thematic revelation) (Truby, PDF pp. 65-66).

**Worked example: *Casablanca* (pp. 66-67).** Truby fills in the whole exercise for *Casablanca*. Highlights, paraphrased:
- **Designing principle:** an ex-freedom fighter who dropped out over a lost love is pulled back into the fight when she reappears. **Theme line:** a great love between two people may need to be given up for the struggle against tyranny. **Moral choice:** the woman he loves or the fight against dictatorship. **Moral problem:** how to weigh personal desire against sacrifice for the wider good.
- **Variations:** Rick cares only for himself for most of the story; Ilsa tries to do right but love overpowers her; Laszlo will give up anything, love included, to lead the fight; Renault is a pure opportunist after pleasure and money. Their value clusters differ accordingly.
- **Sequence:** Rick's weaknesses are cynicism, selfishness and cruelty; his need is to stop serving himself at others' cost and return to lead against fascism. His first immoral action is taking the letters of transit despite suspecting where they came from; a second is refusing to help Ugarte. A string of further cruelties follows, mostly aimed at Ilsa, who (as his first opponent) delivers the main moral attack, with Sam playing a lesser ally role. His last "immoral" act, lying to Ilsa that they will go away together, is in fact the beginning of his noble act.
- **Battle and ending:** at the airport Rick forces Ilsa onto the plane with Laszlo and shoots Strasser, which Truby treats as justified within that world. His self-revelation is that his love matters less than helping Laszlo fight Nazism; his decision is to hand over the letters, send Ilsa with Laszlo, and leave to join the Free French. The **thematic revelation** comes from Renault's last-minute switch to join Rick (a double reversal): in the fight against fascism, everyone has to play a part (Truby, PDF pp. 66-67).

## 16. Terms introduced

| Term | Meaning in Truby |
|---|---|
| **Theme** | How the author thinks people ought to act; not a subject or topic. |
| **Moral vision** | The writer's own, original view of right action, expressed through the story. |
| **Argument of action** | A moral case made through what characters do in the plot, not through speeches. |
| **Theme line** | The moral argument in one sentence: which actions are right or wrong, and what they do to a life. |
| **Single grand symbol** | One symbol that embodies the theme or the story's core moral element. |
| **Two grand symbols (one-line process)** | Two linked symbols marking the poles of a moral sequence, usually a decline. |
| **Thematic oppositions** | The pieces a theme line is split into, attached to the hero and opponents as they fight. |
| **Moral frame** | The hero's line from moral need to moral self-revelation and moral decision. |
| **Moral decision** | The hero's final, binding choice between two courses of action; the narrow neck of the theme's funnel. |
| **Central moral problem** | The moral question every major character faces, each in a different way. |
| **Variations on a theme (characters as)** | Technique of giving each major character a different approach to the central moral problem. |
| **Values** | Deeply held convictions about what a good life consists of; given to characters in clusters and set in conflict. |
| **Web of moral oppositions** | Multi-sided moral opposition (e.g., four-corner) as opposed to simple good versus evil. |
| **Converging structure / expanding theme** | Structure narrows toward the battle while theme widens in the audience's mind. |
| **Moral weakness** | How the hero is hurting others at the start, from weakness or ignorance rather than evil. |
| **First immoral action** | The hero's early act that shows the audience his moral flaw. |
| **Criticism / justification** | Paired beats after each immoral action: others object; the hero defends himself. |
| **Attack by ally** | A best friend's forceful objection to the hero's methods. |
| **Obsessive drive** | The point where the hero will do almost anything to win. |
| **Final action against opponent** | A last act, moral or not, aimed at the opponent around the time of the battle. |
| **Moral self-revelation** | The hero's recognition that he misjudged himself and wronged others, and of how to act rightly. |
| **Thematic revelation** | An audience insight, beyond the hero's, into how all people should live; reveals the story's "total design." |
| **Moral jeopardy** | The open question of whether the hero will act rightly; lost if the moral self-revelation comes too early. |
| **Spider (opponent)** | An overpowered opponent who traps a too-weak hero, making the hero a victim and the opponent simply evil. |
| **Might-have-been** | In tragedy, the sense of the hero's lost potential; the main source of audience sympathy. |
| **Return of the man** | The wronged character's triumphant return, newly powerful (Monte Cristo style). |
| **Straw man** | An opponent who looks strong but whose argument is hollow; to be avoided. |
| **Unique moral vision** | A writer's whole original moral system, built through all the story's webs. |
| **Good versus bad / tragedy / pathos / satire and irony / black comedy** | The five variants of moral argument (see §11). |
