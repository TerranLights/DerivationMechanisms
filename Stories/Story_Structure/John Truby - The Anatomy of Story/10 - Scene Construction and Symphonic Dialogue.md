## 1. Scenes are where the story becomes real
- Everything built so far (premise, structure, character, moral argument, world, symbol, plot, weave) reaches the audience only through scenes.
- The writer's two tools for that are description and dialogue.
- This is the stage at which the story comes to life (Truby, PDF p. 179).

## 2. A scene is a ministory
- Truby repeats his definition of a scene (a single action, a single time, a single place) and then asks what it consists of (Truby, PDF p. 179).
- His answer: a scene is a whole story in small. A good one holds six of the seven key steps. The exception is self-revelation, which is kept for the hero late in the full story.
- Within a scene, that missing step is normally replaced by a twist, a surprise or a reveal (Truby, PDF p. 179).

## 3. Two objectives for every scene
Every scene has two jobs (Truby, PDF p. 179):
1. Fit into the hero's overall development and move it forward.
2. Work as a good ministory in its own right.

These two govern everything else, and the hero's arc always takes priority (Truby, PDF p. 179).

## 4. The scene as an upside-down triangle
**Key point.** Picture the scene as an inverted triangle (Truby, PDF pp. 179-180):
- The start frames what the scene is about.
- The scene then narrows to one point.
- The single most important word or line comes at the very end.

## 5. The construction sequence: nine questions
Truby gives an ideal order of questions for building a scene (Truby, PDF p. 180):

1. **Position on the character arc.** At what stage of the hero's development does this scene fall, and how does it move him on?
2. **Problems.** What does the scene have to solve or get done?
3. **Strategy.** What approach will solve those problems?
4. **Desire.** Which character's want drives the scene, and what is it? It may be the hero or someone else. That want is the scene's spine.
5. **Endpoint.** Where does that want end up?
   - Knowing this in advance lets you aim the whole scene at it.
   - It should fall at the tip of the triangle, together with the scene's most important word or line.
   - That pairing lands a knockout blow that also propels the audience into the next scene.
6. **Opponent.** Who stands against the want, and what is the fight over?
7. **Plan.** How does the character with the want go after it within this scene? The plan is either direct or indirect (see below). Truby stresses that this is the plan inside the scene, not the plan for the whole story.
8. **Conflict.** Escalate until things either break or get solved.
9. **Twist or reveal.** Sometimes something happens that catches the characters, the audience or both off guard, or one character lets another have it. This acts like a self-revelation within the scene, though it isn't final and can be mistaken.

### Direct and indirect plans within a scene
- **Direct plan:** the character simply says what he wants (Truby, PDF p. 180).
- **Indirect plan:** the character acts as if he wants one thing while actually after another. The other party either sees through it and goes along, or is taken in and ends up giving exactly what was really wanted (Truby, PDF p. 180).
- **Rule of thumb for choosing** (Truby, PDF p. 180):
  - Going direct sharpens the conflict and pushes people apart.
  - Going indirect softens the conflict at first and pulls people closer, but it can blow up more badly later, once the pretense is exposed.

## 6. Start late
Many writers, aiming for realism, open a scene too early and climb slowly toward the real fight. Truby says the result is not realism but dullness (Truby, PDF p. 180).

**Key point:** enter the scene as late as you can while keeping every structural element it requires (Truby, PDF p. 180).

## 7. Complex or subtext scenes
**The usual definition falls short.** The standard description of subtext is a scene in which people don't say what they actually want. Truby accepts that but says it gives the writer no method (Truby, PDF p. 180).

**Subtext is not always best.** Here the received wisdom is wrong (Truby, PDF p. 180):
- Characters in subtext are generally scared, hurting or too embarrassed to voice their real thoughts or wants.
- For the most conflict a scene can hold, avoid subtext.
- Use it when it suits these particular characters in this particular scene.

**How to build subtext.** It rests on two structural pieces, desire and plan. For the most subtext (Truby, PDF pp. 180-181):
- **Hidden desires.** Give many of the characters a concealed want, with the wants directly at odds. Truby's illustration is a chain of secret attractions in which A loves B and B loves C.
- **Indirect plans for all of them.** Every character with a concealed want goes after it indirectly, saying one thing and meaning another. Some are trying to deceive. Others use a pretense they know is see-through, betting that its charm will win them what they want.

## 8. Dialogue: two misconceptions
Truby leaves description to other books, since it is not a story skill, but treats dialogue as part of story craft (Truby, PDF p. 181).
- **Misconception 1: dialogue should carry the story.** Writers often make dialogue do the heavy work that belongs to structure. It then sounds stiff, strained and fake (Truby, PDF p. 181).
- **Misconception 2, the more dangerous one: good dialogue is real talk.** It is the mirror image of the first error (Truby, PDF p. 181).

**Key points** (Truby, PDF p. 181):
- Dialogue isn't real speech. It is language chosen with great care so that it seems as though it might be real.
- Good dialogue is always brighter, funnier, more figurative and better reasoned than speech in life.
- Even the least educated character talks at the peak of his own ability. A character in error is more articulate in his error than real people are.

## 9. Dialogue as a technique of the small
- Like symbol, dialogue works at the finest scale. Laid on top of all the larger elements (structure, character, theme, world, symbol, plot, weave), it is the most delicate tool a storyteller has.
- It can still hit very hard (Truby, PDF p. 181).

## 10. Dialogue as music: the symphony of three tracks
- Truby treats dialogue as a kind of music: communication with rhythm and tone, at its best when several "tracks" play at once (Truby, PDF p. 181).
- **One track is not enough.** Most writers produce only the melody: lines that explain the plot as it unfolds. Truby calls single-track dialogue a mark of mediocre work (Truby, PDF p. 181).
- **Great dialogue is a symphony** running three tracks together: story dialogue, moral dialogue, and key words and phrases (Truby, PDF p. 181).

### Track 1: story dialogue (melody)
**What it is.** Story dialogue is the plot carried in speech: characters discussing what they are up to along the main line of action (Truby, PDF p. 181).
- People usually treat talk as the opposite of action, as the proverb about actions and words suggests. Truby counters that talking is itself a form of action.
- For brief stretches, speech alone can even carry the story.

**How to write it.** It follows the same pattern as building a scene (Truby, PDF p. 181):
1. The scene's lead character (not necessarily the story's hero) declares a want. The writer should know where that want ends up, because that endpoint is the line on which the scene's talk hangs.
2. A second character pushes back against it.
3. The first replies with a direct or indirect plan to get his way.
4. The exchange grows hotter and closes on a last burst of anger or a resolution.

**Advanced technique: from action to being.** In key scenes, the dialogue moves from what people are doing to who they really are (Truby, PDF p. 181).
- **How it works:** at the peak, one character says some version of "You are…" and spells out his verdict on the other, from "liar" to "winner."
- **Why it deepens the scene:** suddenly the characters are arguing about how their deeds define them as people.
- **The claim need not be right.** Simply making it pushes the audience to sum up its own view of these characters so far.
- **What it amounts to:** a kind of in-scene self-revelation that often brings in values (Track 2). Most scenes don't have it; the key ones usually do.

**Example, *The Verdict*:** the victim's brother-in-law confronts the lawyer Frank Galvin in a courthouse hallway for rejecting a settlement without asking the family. Galvin insists he will win. The man's last speech lumps Galvin in with the doctors who harmed his sister-in-law, all professionals who promise much and then apologize, while ordinary people live with their mistakes. The exchange shifts from what Galvin did to what kind of man he is (Truby, PDF pp. 181-182).

### Track 2: moral dialogue (harmony)
**What it is.** Moral dialogue is argument over whether actions are right or wrong, and over values, meaning what makes a life worth living (Truby, PDF p. 182).
- Like harmony under a melody, it adds depth, texture and range.
- Its subject is not the events of the story but how the characters feel about those events.

**Its sequence** (Truby, PDF p. 182):
1. One character proposes or takes some action.
2. Another objects because the action is hurting someone.
3. The two attack and defend, each giving reasons.

**Values and ways of life.** In moral dialogue characters always show their values, what they like and dislike. Truby reminds the reader that values come out of a deeper idea of how one ought to live. At its most advanced, moral dialogue lets you set not just two actions but two or more whole ways of life against each other (Truby, PDF p. 182).

### Track 3: key words, phrases, taglines and sounds (repetition, variation, leitmotif)
**Key words** are words that can carry a special symbolic or thematic charge, much as a symphony brings in one instrument here and there for emphasis (Truby, PDF p. 182).
- To build that charge, have characters say the word far more often than people normally would.
- Repeating it, especially in varied settings, has an effect on the audience that accumulates.

**The tagline** is one line of dialogue repeated many times over the story (Truby, PDF p. 182).
- Each repetition adds meaning until it becomes the story's signature line.
- It is chiefly a tool for expressing theme.
- **Classic examples:** several lines from *Casablanca*, and the famous repeated lines of *Cool Hand Luke*, *Star Wars*, *Field of Dreams* and *The Godfather* (which has two).

**Textbook case, *Butch Cassidy*** (Truby, PDF p. 182):
- **First use:** "Who are those guys?" means nothing special at first. Butch says it, looking back at a posse they can't lose.
- **Repetition:** Sundance says it again later, closer to desperation, as the posse gains.
- **Growing meaning:** working out who "those guys" are becomes the pair's central task.
- **The answer:** they are not just another posse. They are the next stage of society, elite lawmen from across the West hired by an Eastern corporate boss the heroes never see.
- **The stakes:** if the heroes don't understand this in time, they die.

## 11. Special kinds of scenes
Truby next looks at particular scene types that both carry out and bend the basic principles (Truby, PDF p. 182).

### 11a. The opening
**Why it is hardest.** Every character and action in the story rests on the opening scene, so it is likely the toughest to get right (Truby, PDF p. 182).

**It has two jobs** (Truby, PDF pp. 182-183):
- As the first scene of the whole story's inverted triangle, it must draw the widest frame and tell the audience in broad terms what the story concerns.
- It must also stand as a small story itself, with gripping characters and action and a strong first blow.

So Truby pictures the opening scene as a small inverted triangle sitting inside the large inverted triangle of the whole story (Truby, PDF p. 183).

**Thematic patterns.** The opening also hints at the patterns of identity and opposition the author will weave through the story. These must be anchored in specific characters, or the scene comes off as theoretical or preachy (Truby, PDF p. 183).

**Worked example: the first two scenes of *Butch Cassidy*** (William Goldman). Truby ranks them among the finest film openings, because they hook the audience and also set out the patterns and oppositions that rule the whole story (Truby, PDF p. 183). He analyzes each scene with the full checklist: position on the arc, problems, strategy, desire, endpoint, opponent, plan, conflict, twist, moral argument and values, and key words and images.

**Scene 1, Butch at the bank** (Truby, PDF pp. 183-184):
- **Position and problems.** This is the first look at Butch and the first step of a process that ends with an Old West robber dead. The scene must introduce the world (outlaws in a West that is nearly gone) and the first of the two buddies, and hint that both men, like their frontier, are aging out.
- **Strategy:**
  - make a scene that is typical of these two men and sets up the key thematic patterns;
  - show the whole story's process (everything closing down) in one scene;
  - keep it light and funny with a darker undertow;
  - show a would-be robber discovering the job is far tougher than it used to be;
  - hide who the man is, so the audience has to work out he is casing the bank. That makes the closing joke funnier and defines him as a confident trickster and talker.
- **Mechanics:**
  - Desire: scout a bank to rob.
  - Endpoint: the bank is now more secure and is closing for the night.
  - Opponents: the bank, and the guard who locks it up.
  - Plan: an indirect one, posing as an admirer of the bank's looks.
  - Conflict: the bank shuts down around him like a living thing.
  - Twist: the onlooker is casing it.
- **Values.** The joke turns on aesthetics against practicality, and Truby says that is the story's basic value opposition: the world is turning practical while Butch and Sundance are men of style, attached to a vanishing way of life.
- **Key images:** bars coming down, time running out, lights going off, space closing in.
- **The punch line** about beauty arrives just as we learn who he is. It means two opposite things: he doesn't care about beauty, he wants to rob the place; and yet the line truly defines him as a man of style, which will eventually get him killed.

**Scene 2, the card game** (Truby, PDF pp. 184-185):
- **Position and problems.** The scene sets Sundance's starting point on the same doomed arc and adds detail to Butch. It must introduce the second buddy, show how he differs from Butch, and show the two working as a team.
- **Strategy:**
  - a second scene typical of the pair, with no effect on the plot, whose only job is to define the two men in one snapshot;
  - define them through crisis, because crisis exposes essence at once;
  - define Sundance mainly, but Butch by contrast. Sundance makes trouble and Butch tries to defuse it; Sundance says little and Butch is a talker and con man;
  - take a stock Western beat, the card-game showdown, and flip it twice. First it becomes the goofy way a man defends his honor when called a cheat. Then the goofy man turns out to be truly that good, a bigger Western hero than the form usually gives;
  - above all, fool the audience about Sundance at the same moment Sundance fools his opponent.
- **Mechanics:**
  - Desire: the card player Macon wants Sundance's money and wants him gone, humiliated.
  - Endpoint: Macon is humiliated but sees he chose wisely once Sundance shows what he can do with a gun.
  - Opponents: first Sundance, then Butch as well.
  - Plan: direct (leave or die).
  - Conflict: escalates toward a gunfight; Butch's attempt to negotiate fails.
- **Twist.** Goldman withholds information so the audience flips at the same moment Macon does. Sundance starts weak and is made weaker: he protests like a child that he wasn't cheating, and Butch teases him about being past his prime. When the tables turn, the effect is huge. What proves his greatness is less the gunplay than his readiness to seem as if he might lose.
- **Values.** This is warrior culture at its extreme: the public showdown, the contest of skill and nerve, the weight of a name. Butch belongs to a later social stage and just wants everyone alive.
- **Key images:** growing old, time closing in, but not just yet.
- **Dialogue.** The showdown dialogue is lean, often one line a side, like trading blows, and stylized with a stand-up comic's timing. Sundance's very first line is a single cocky word that defines him.
- **The team routine.** The middle of the scene turns into a squabble between the two buddies, then plays out their absurd joint solution (they'll leave if invited to stay), which Butch tries to sell politely. They are a comedy team.
- **Key words last.** In the punch line that flips the power, Sundance's name comes last. The scene's final line, a sarcastic remark that his partner is over the hill, is the key thematic line. It looks wrong at that moment but proves true in hindsight: the two really are past it without knowing it, and so they die. That last line is the point of the opening's triangle and hints at the point of the whole film.

### 11b. Scene-writing technique: the first sentence
- A story's opening sentence condenses everything just said about opening scenes into one line.
- It is the widest statement the story makes, framing its subject, and it must also land a punch (Truby, PDF p. 185).
- Truby examines three classic openings, each with the same arc/problems/strategy analysis, and prints the lines that follow so the sentence can be seen within the author's strategy.

**Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*** (Truby, PDF p. 185):
- **Position.** Before any hero appears, the opening presents the world: women hunting for husbands.
- **Problems:** signal that this is a comedy, suggest the world and its rules, and establish that a woman's perspective will tell it.
- **Strategy:** a mock-solemn sentence that poses as a universal truth and a generous act, while actually voicing an opinion about naked self-interest. It announces the subject (marriage, families pursuing men, and the bond between marriage and money).
- **Narrowing.** The text then moves from the general arena to one particular family who will live out the principle. Truby says the lines contain no fat at all.

**Charles Dickens, *David Copperfield*** (Truby, PDF pp. 185-186):
- **Position.** A first-person storyteller at the end of his arc tells of its very beginning, so the young hero comes with a certain wisdom.
- **Problems:** where to start and end the story of a whole life, and how to signal what kind of story this is.
- **Strategy.** The chapter title, three words announcing his birth, works as the book's first sentence.
  - It plants a flag: this life matters, and the tale will be a grand one.
  - It signals a coming-of-age story told in myth form, starting from the hero's birth, with big ambitions.
- **What follows.** The next sentence shows a hero who thinks in story terms (he is, as it happens, a writer) and cares about fulfilling his life's potential. The narrator then dramatizes his birth at the stroke of midnight.
- **Effect.** The reader settles in for a long, rewarding journey.

**J. D. Salinger, *The Catcher in the Rye*** (Truby, PDF p. 186):
- **Position.** The narrator tells last year's events from a sanitarium. He is close to the end of his growth but still lacks the final insights that telling the story will bring.
- **Problems:** where to begin and what to include; reveal himself through how he tells the story; state the guiding theme and value.
- **Strategy:**
  - First person in a "bad boy" voice. It marks a coming-of-age story, and the sanitarium setting and slang signal the opposite of the usual one.
  - A narrator hostile to the reader, warning that he won't flatter anyone. The implication is brutal honesty: saying things exactly as he sees them is, for him, a moral duty.
  - One long, rambling sentence whose form expresses both the hero and the plot to come.
  - A sneering early reference to *David Copperfield*, the nineteenth century's model coming-of-age novel. It signals the reverse: small plot, even antiplot, and a modest journey. It also suggests an ambition to write the twentieth century's equal.
- **The guiding value** is "nothing phony": real characters, real emotion, real change if any.

### 11c. Values in conflict
**Ideas in battle.** Great drama comes not from two people colliding but from their values and ideas going to war (Truby, PDF p. 187).
- **Two forms of Track 2.** Both the clash of values and moral argument count as moral dialogue. The first is a quarrel over beliefs; the second is a quarrel over whether an action is right or wrong (Truby, PDF p. 187).
- **Usually carried by plot talk.** Values mostly clash while the characters discuss the plot (Track 1), which keeps the talk from sounding too openly thematic. When the story grows into a fight between two whole ways of living, the values have to meet head-on in the dialogue (Truby, PDF p. 187).
- **How to stage the head-on clash.** Anchor it to a specific course of action the characters can fight over. But have them argue mainly about the larger question of what a good life is, not about whether that one act is right (Truby, PDF p. 187).

**Example, *It's a Wonderful Life*: George and Potter argue over the Building and Loan** (Truby, PDF pp. 187-189).

*Why Truby rates the scene:*
- He calls it the film's most important argument. The writers make Potter a greater opponent by letting him lay out his values and the logic he lives by, in direct opposition to George's (Truby, PDF p. 187).
- **Politics in the human sense.** As social fantasy, the argument is about how a whole society should live, so it is political. Not in a specific, quickly dated way, but in terms of how people live under those who lead them (Truby, PDF p. 187).
- **Made personal.** The writers turn the broad argument into something emotional by tying it to one act, shutting the institution down, and to the recent loss of George's father (Truby, PDF p. 187).
- **Two long monologues.** Apart from a brief exchange in the middle, the scene is two long speeches, against the Hollywood habit of short back-and-forth. Each man needs room to argue for a whole philosophy of living. Grounding it in mutual personal loathing keeps it from becoming dry political philosophy (Truby, PDF p. 187).

*The checklist:*
- **Arc.** George has just had his life's dream thwarted for the first time and has made his first sacrifice for family and friends, and is about to leave for college.
- **Problem:** stage a fight over the values a town, and America, should be built on, without preaching.
- **Strategy:** have hero and main opponent argue over the institution that funds the town, and over the dead man who built it; then focus the whole philosophical argument onto one word at the end of George's speech.
- **Mechanics.** Potter means to shut the institution down and is defeated, because George blocks him. Both plans are direct. The fight sharpens when Potter turns from the institution to George's father. The twist is that young George can stand up to a man who bullies everyone (Truby, PDF p. 187).

*Potter's case, in five moves* (Truby, PDF pp. 187-188):
1. He separates being a businessman from being a man of ideals.
2. He claims ideals without common sense can wreck the town. This tells the audience the town is the battleground and the central question is which way of life makes it a better place.
3. He uses a concrete example the audience likes (a taxi driver's home loan), claiming it came through friendship.
4. He says lending this way produces a resentful, idle mob in place of a frugal laboring class. That exposes his view of America as a class society he is entitled to rule. Truby notes the dialogue may overreach here, making Potter both classic patriarch and evil capitalist.
5. He ends by attacking what George stands for: the dreamer, and the personal, communal ties that make a town worth living in.

*George's case* (Truby, PDF p. 188):
- **Key point:** a few scenes earlier, George's father put this very case to him, and George argued the other side. That earlier scene makes George's eloquence now more convincing and more touching.
- **His five moves:**
  1. He gives ground first: his father had no head for business, and George himself has no taste for the small-time institution.
  2. He shifts the argument to his father's selflessness, which cost both sons their schooling.
  3. He meets Potter on Potter's own turf, money: helping families out of the slums turned them into better members of the town and better clients.
  4. He raises the level to the heroism of the ordinary man. The people Potter scorns do most of the community's work and living and dying, so nobody should be ranked as lower class.
  5. He ends on human rights. His father saw people as ends in themselves; Potter sees them as cattle, as tools for his own goal of getting rich.
- **Key point:** the writers make their widest argument, for ordinary people's rights, at the most personal level, and save the crucial line and word for the end (Truby, PDF p. 188).
- **The insult carries a double charge.** George calls Potter a frustrated old man, which matters because frustration is George's own most obvious trait (Truby, PDF p. 188).
- **The final word.** The last line claims his father died "richer" than Potter ever will. The word carries two values: money, which defines Potter, and what a person gives to and receives from others, which defines George. Key word: *richer* (Truby, PDF p. 188).

**Example, *Shadow of a Doubt*: the dinner-table speech** (Truby, PDF pp. 189-190).

*Why Truby rates the scene:*
- He rates it as perhaps the finest thriller screenplay ever, and Thornton Wilder's script as a model of blending drama technique into the thriller so it rises above its genre (Truby, PDF p. 189).
- **A killer with an argument.** A weaker writer would have made the murderer an unexplained monster, which would shrink the story into a record of a killing machine. Wilder gives him a detailed, understandable moral argument, and that makes him far more frightening. His target is the dark side of American life that most people ignore: the scramble for money, and the many who never reach the American dream (Truby, PDF p. 189).

*The checklist* (Truby, PDF p. 189):
- **Arc.** The opponent has no arc of his own, but the scene falls at a crucial moment for the heroine, who is torn between her old adoration of her uncle and her new revulsion and desperate to understand.
- **Problem:** let the opponent hint at his motive for killing without confessing.
- **Strategy:**
  - set it at the family dinner table, so the justification sits inside everyday American family life;
  - give him a natural reason to talk about older women (his sister has booked him to speak to her women's club);
  - let the horrifying grow out of the mundane.
- **Mechanics:**
  - Desire: justify, to the girl, his disgust for older women, and scare her away.
  - Endpoint: he realizes he has said too much.
  - Opponent: Young Charlie.
  - Plan: indirect. He philosophizes about city widows in general, which keeps his cover while reaching the one listener who will understand.
  - Conflict: although she pushes back only once, the conflict builds through his escalating contempt.
  - Twist: this charming man thinks such women are animals to be put down.
- **Moral argument and values.** It runs in three steps: such women are useless, then greedy beasts consuming money, then it is right to put them out of their misery. The opposed values are usefulness and humanity against money, sensuality, uselessness and animality.
- **Key words:** five words about money, marriage, uselessness, greed and animals.

*Why it works* (Truby, PDF pp. 189-190):
- It is at once mundane and murderous, moving from ordinary husbands and wives to women as animals.
- The key last line is a question. He never says they should be killed; instead he asks the girl what ought to happen to them, and his logic leaves her only one answer.
- A comic tag returns the scene to its starting point: his sister, oblivious, worries about his speech to her club (a wolf guarding the henhouse, as Truby puts it) and mentions a widow she has lined up for him.

### 11d. Monologue
**What it is** (Truby, PDF p. 190):
- Dialogue reaches truth and emotion through conflict between characters. Monologue reaches them through a character's conflict with himself.
- A monologue is a small story playing out inside one character's mind. It is another kind of miniature, condensing the character's identity, his core struggle, and the change he undergoes over the whole story.
- **Uses:** show the audience a mind in full detail, or show how intensely the character is suffering.

**Rule:** a good monologue must first tell a complete story, covering all seven key steps and finishing on the key word or line (Truby, PDF p. 190).

**Example, *The Verdict*: the closing argument** (Truby, PDF pp. 190-191).
- **Why it fits.** David Mamet ends the battle with a monologue. Because it is a lawyer's closing argument to a jury, a speech needs no justification even in "realistic" mainstream American film.
- **Two stories at once:** the injured woman's path, and the lawyer's own.
- **Arc.** The self-revelation is already behind Frank. This is the final step of his arc: winning at trial proves it.
- **Problem:** wrap up the case so that it hits as hard as possible.
- **Strategy:** argue the case and urge the jurors to act morally while covertly telling the story of Frank's own growth.
- **Mechanics:**
  - Desire: move the jurors to act for justice.
  - Endpoint: he comes to see each juror as a person who wants to do right.
  - Opponent: the wealthy and powerful who grind ordinary people down.
  - Plan: speak sincerely and thereby make justice real.
  - Conflict: a man fighting to know and do right while urging the jurors toward the same.
  - Twist: the audience realizes he is talking about himself.
- **The argument as a seven-step story.** People feel lost and powerless (weakness). They want to be just (desire), despite those who beat them down (opponent). If they realize their power (plan) and believe in themselves (self-revelation), they can act justly (moral decision, battle, new equilibrium).
- **Key words:** justice and believing.

### 11e. Closings
**Why the end matters most.** Truby cites Chekhov's view that a play's last ninety seconds matter most, because the final scene is the story's ultimate point of convergence (Truby, PDF p. 191).
- **Plot is usually finished.** Now and then the final scene adds one more plot kick, a revelation, but usually the plot business is already done.
- **A miniature of the whole.** Like the opening, the final scene becomes a miniature of the whole story. The author underlines the thematic patterns once more, and the audience sees that these characters' way is the way of the wider world too. In short, a thematic revelation.

**The final triangle** (Truby, PDF p. 191):
- The closing scene is the tip of the whole story's inverted triangle and is shaped as one itself, ending on the most important word or line of both.
- Done well, this is the ultimate funnel: the last word or line sets off an explosion in the audience that echoes long after the story ends.

**Example, Hemingway's *The Sun Also Rises*** (Truby, PDF p. 191):
- **The story:** friends drift around Europe; one man is kept from the woman he loves by an injury from the war. Their great love can't happen, so they spiral down into lives made of nothing but chasing sensations. They are aimless, aware of the trap and unable to escape.
- **The final taxi scene** is typical of them. Brett's closing line is ordinary, even throwaway, yet it stands for the whole book: what might have been a grand romantic tragedy has shrunk to having a good time.
- **Jake tops it** with an ironic reply. He is cursed not only by his wound but by a temperament that lets him believe a comforting fiction while seeing it for what it is, which dooms him.

**Example, *Seven Samurai*** (Truby, PDF pp. 191-192):
- **Truby's estimate:** storytelling at the level of highest art, executing nearly every technique in the book.
- **The final scene.** The battle is won, but four samurai are buried on the hill, and the young samurai's peasant love has turned away to plant the next crop with the villagers. The lead samurai, watching all this, sees that though they won, the samurai have lost. Their era has ended, the old divisions between people are back, and the dead men's heroism is as fleeting as the wind.
- **Why it doesn't read as a bald self-revelation:**
  1. It follows an epic fight in which seven men beat forty bandits for strangers, so it is a huge emotional twist.
  2. It is a big revelation placed at the very last moment, like the shock endings of two well-known twist films Truby names.
  3. It also reveals theme: the hero watches a whole social world, beautiful in many respects, pass away.

**Example, Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*** (Truby, PDF p. 192):
- **The situation:** Gatsby is dead, and Nick has seen through his own pursuit of success and is going home to the Midwest.
- **The movement of the final page:**
  - The big houses closed for the season are a plain story fact and at once a symbol: the false paradise of parties has died with Gatsby.
  - Nick then reaches back to the past and widens the view, imagining the island as untouched Eden when the continent was new. That sets up a stark contrast with the island now, where real people's desires have turned forest into the false idols of mansions and empty parties.
  - The focus narrows again to Gatsby, whose desire aimed like a laser at the green light. Like the hero of classic myth, he is a deluded dreamer who never knew he had everything back where he began.
- **The ending's special quality.** Unlike the many stories that close on a false note, with the goal reached and everything settled, Fitzgerald ends on desire that never stops, an effort that redoubles as the goal recedes. The final sentence reveals the theme and stands for the whole book.

**Example, the ending of *Butch Cassidy*** (Truby, PDF pp. 192-193):
- **A mirror of the opening.** The final scene reflects the first two scenes.
- **The tragedy:** these likable men can't change or learn. The new world arriving is too much for them, and they can only die.
- **Problem:** express their essential qualities and show the cost of not learning.
- **Strategy:**
  - As in scene 1, a tight space closing in.
  - As in scene 2, a defining crisis. They face death with total confidence, and Butch is already planning the next move. The crisis also shows how they differ: Butch keeps producing schemes, and Sundance keeps rescuing them from the mess each scheme creates.
  - Their teamwork is on display again, with Sundance covering Butch's run for ammunition, but it is their comic bickering, present from the start, that shows the audience this is a perfect "marriage."
- **The crosscut.** Goldman crosscuts their argument over Butch's newest escape plan (another continent) with the approach of what seems to be a whole national army. The growing distance between the heroes' knowledge and the audience's shows they can't see past their own small world.
- **Final audience revelation:** even supermen die, and it hurts.
- **The scene's final line is also the story's key line:** Butch's relief that their old pursuer isn't outside, because for a moment he thought they were in trouble.

## 12. Masterpieces of scene construction: *Casablanca* and *The Godfather*
- To finish, Truby studies scenes from the beginning and end of two films he considers masterpieces.
- He chooses beginnings and endings because so much of scene-writing success depends on placing each scene correctly on the hero's arc.
- He urges the reader to rewatch both films (Truby, PDF p. 193).

### 12a. *Casablanca*, the first Rick-Louis scene (Truby, PDF pp. 193-194)
**Position on the arc.** This is where the relationship between the two men begins; it will end in shared redemption and a buddy "marriage" in the last scene.
- Truby uses it to show why you must always start a scene by fixing its place on the arc.
- It isn't the opening scene and seems like one more beat in the story's flow. Only by starting from the end of Rick's arc (freedom fighter, bonded to Louis) can you see that it is the crucial first step.

**Problems:**
- show that Louis matches Rick's wit and is the right partner for him;
- show Louis has as much moral need;
- add to Rick's ghost, revealing this hard cynic was once good and even heroic.

**Strategy:**
1. Let Louis question Rick about his past under cover of his job of stopping Laszlo. That delivers exposition without being dull, while Rick's claim that he fought only for good pay keeps him from seeming sentimental.
2. Have the two men wager on Laszlo's chances of getting away. That gives them a desire line all their own and shows their shared cynicism, turning a freedom fighter's cause into a wager.
3. Build up the reputations of Laszlo and Ilsa before they appear.
4. Clarify the tangled chain of authority between Louis and the German officer.

**Mechanics:**
- Desire: Louis wants Rick's history, then wants to warn him off helping Laszlo.
- Endpoint: Rick reveals nothing and claims interest only as a sporting matter.
- Opponent: Rick.
- Plan: direct.
- Conflict: they disagree, but Rick defuses it into a bet.
- Twist: Laszlo travels with a remarkable woman, and cynical Rick once fought for causes himself.

**Values and dialogue:**
- **Moral argument.** The scene is about acting morally. The wager is about whether Laszlo can get away, not whether he deserves to. Rick denies any moral motive for his past fighting on the "right" side, and predicts Laszlo will leave his companion behind.
- **Value opposition:** money and self-interest against romance and selfless fighting for what is right.
- **Key words:** two words for a soft-hearted idealist.
- **The dialogue** is stylized and witty throughout. Truby points to Louis's playful guesses about Rick's crimes, and Rick's deadpan story about coming to a desert city "for the waters."

### 12b. *Casablanca*, the final Rick-Louis scene (Truby, PDF pp. 194-195)
**Position on the arc:**
1. Rick's transformation into a committed fighter for his country's cause is complete.
2. Structurally a double reversal: both men change, not only Rick.
3. The end point of their relationship: the buddy "marriage."

**Problems:** give the last scene the greatest possible impact, and show big changes in two people in a way that is believable but not dull.

**Strategy:**
1. Delay the reveal of Louis's turn, and of the new partnership, to the last possible moment.
2. Use the double reversal so both men see the light while keeping their hard-nosed opportunism. The key is returning to the bet: it lets both make huge moral turns and stay tough guys, which avoids syrupy sentiment.

**Mechanics:**
- Desire: Louis wants to throw in his lot with Rick and begin a friendship.
- Endpoint: Rick welcomes him.
- Opponent: the two still look like adversaries over the escape and the wager, until Louis smooths that away.
- Plan: indirect. Louis hides his intention and seems as if he might still make trouble.
- Conflict: they bargain over the escape and the money owed, and Louis finds a stylish solution.
- Twist: Louis joins rather than arrests Rick, at the cost of Rick's winnings.
- Values: both accept it is time to be patriots, without wholly forgetting money.
- Key words: patriot, friendship.

**What the scene shows:**
- **One point.** It funnels to a single point for scene and story, friendship: Rick loses his great love but gains an equal friend. Everything leads to the big reveal of Louis's stylish way of joining.
- **Density.** The dialogue is witty but very dense. Huge story turns are packed into a few short lines: each man's noble act, Louis's proposal, Rick's turn back to the bet, Louis's line combining the two, Rick's realization, and a last line of lasting friendship.
- **Combination punches.** Truby likens the series to a run of punches that ends in a knockout, a model of Chekhov's rule about the final ninety seconds.

### 12c. *The Godfather*: start from the big picture (Truby, PDF p. 195)
Before building scenes, Truby lays out what the writers wanted to play out over the whole film.

**Story strategies or processes:**
- the crown handed down from one ruler to his successor;
- three sons with different gifts trying to be king;
- a family under siege that strikes back to survive and prevail.

**Patterns of identity** (things normally seen as different that the story shows to be the same):
- the crime family as a corporation;
- the crime family as an army;
- the profane as sacred and the sacred as profane, the "god" as the devil.

**Patterns of opposition:**
- the family against the law;
- the family's private justice against the courts of the nation;
- the immigrant world against the American mainstream and its elite;
- the men against the women.

**Values and symbols.** The final preparatory step is to pin down the values and symbols, the key words, that will clash across the story. Only the whole story shows which are central and organic. Then they can be drawn out and stressed through repetition (Track 3). Here they form two groups: on one side honor, family, business, appearances and crime; on the other freedom, nation, and action that is moral and lawful.

### 12d. *The Godfather*, opening scene (Truby, PDF pp. 195-197)
**The choice.** An average writer would open this big, violent story with a plot scene in story dialogue only, for a running start. Puzo and Coppola instead followed the inverted triangle for story and scene. They built a scene typical of the Godfather's world that frames the whole story and narrows to one point (Truby, PDF p. 195).

**The checklist** (Truby, PDF p. 196):
- **Arc.** Because the film follows one ruler's fall and his heir's rise, the opening begins with the reigning Don rather than Michael, and shows what a Godfather does.
- **Problems,** in a story about a monarch inside a democracy:
  - introduce the Godfather and his role;
  - show how the Mafia system works, its hierarchy and rules;
  - announce the epic scope, so the audience knows at once that this family's world stands for the nation rather than some ghetto to look down on;
  - start the patterns of identity and opposition.
- **Strategy:** open with the defining Godfather activity, the Don acting as judge over his domain, and set it inside a wedding, where the whole system is gathered and family is stressed.
- **Desire.** The undertaker asks the Don to have his daughter's attackers killed.
  - He is very minor but knows nothing of how the organization works, so he serves as the viewer's proxy. Letting him drive the scene means the audience learns the rules alongside him.
  - Truby notes that his full name can be read as "Good evening, America."
- **Endpoint:** the Don traps him.
- **Opponent:** the Don.
- **Plan.** Bonasera's is direct (asking for the killing and naming a price), and it earns a refusal. The Don's is indirect, using guilt over past slights to draw Bonasera into his web.
- **Conflict.** The Don, angered by Bonasera's slights, refuses. But the fight can only escalate so far, since the Don holds all the power and the undertaker is too shrewd to push.
- **Twist.** They strike a deal, and the audience sees that Bonasera has just sold his soul.
- **Moral argument.** Bonasera asks for killing as justice; the Don says that isn't justice, then turns the argument on Bonasera's disrespect.
- **Key words:** respect, friend, justice, Godfather.

**How the three tracks work here** (Truby, PDF pp. 196-197):
- **Why symphonic matters.** With story dialogue alone, Truby says, the scene would be half as long and a tenth as good. Woven on three tracks, it is a masterpiece.
- **Frame and point.** The scene ends with Bonasera saying "Godfather" at the moment he is caught in a Faustian bargain. It begins with the framing line of the whole film, a statement of belief in America. Truby treats that line as a value that signals two things: an epic is coming, and its subject will be the routes to success.
- **The flow of the scene:**
  1. A monologue in a nearly featureless space, packed with values and key words (freedom, honor, justice).
  2. A slight moral jab from the Don throws the undertaker onto the back foot, and the Don hands down his ruling.
  3. A quick disagreement over what justice is.
  4. Bonasera, the audience's stand-in, blunders because he doesn't know how payment works here.
  5. The power shifts and the Don takes over, making a moral argument full of respect, friendship and loyalty, aimed at making Bonasera his servant.
  6. Bonasera grasps what the Don's indirect approach is really after, bows, and says the key word.
  7. The Don's last line, a request for a future service that may never be called in, has the shape of the devil's pact with Faust. The Godfather and the devil become one, and the sacred equals the profane.

### 12e. *The Godfather*, closing scene (Truby, PDF pp. 197-198)
**Shape.** The final point of the story's triangle. The scene is at once a "trial," with his sister charging Michael with murder, and his crowning. It matches the opening: the earlier pact with the devil has become the new devil crowned king.

**The checklist:**
- **Arc.** Michael is accused of murder as he rises to Godfather, and he poisons his marriage to Kay beyond repair.
- **Problem:** make the moral case against Michael without his accepting it.
- **Strategy:** hand the accusation to Connie but let it be brushed off as the hysteria of a woman; withhold the self-revelation from Michael and give it to his wife instead, based on what she sees in him rather than what Connie says.
- **Mechanics:**
  - Desire: Connie wants to charge her brother with her husband's murder.
  - Endpoint: the door shuts in Kay's face.
  - Opponents: Michael and Kay.
  - Plan: direct, a public accusation.
  - Conflict: begins at high intensity and then dies away.
  - Twist: Michael lies to Kay, and she sees what he has become.
- **Moral argument.** Connie calls him a cold-blooded killer indifferent to her. Michael doesn't answer her; he deflects by suggesting she is sick and needs a doctor, then denies it all to Kay.
- **Key words:** three titles for Michael (Godfather, emperor, murderer).

## Exercises the chapter gives the writer
**Writing Exercise 9, "Writing Scenes"** (Truby, PDF p. 198):
- **Character change first.** Before writing any scene, state the hero's character change in a single line.
- **Scene construction.** Build each scene by working through ten questions: the nine-question construction sequence above (arc position, problems, strategy, whose desire, endpoint, opponent, direct or indirect plan, whether the scene ends at peak conflict or with a solution, whether there is a twist or reveal), plus a tenth: will one character close the scene by naming what another character truly is underneath?
- **Scenes without dialogue.** Draft the scenes first with no dialogue at all, so that only what the characters do carries the story. This gives the "clay" to shape in later drafts.
- **Dialogue in three passes:**
  1. Rewrite each scene with story dialogue only (Track 1), the talk about the characters' actions in the plot.
  2. Rewrite again, adding moral dialogue (Track 2), argument over whether those actions are right and statements of what the characters believe.
  3. Rewrite again, bringing out the Track 3 material: the objects, images, values or ideas central to the theme.
- **The portrait analogy.** Truby compares the three passes to drawing a portrait: first the general outline of the head (story dialogue), then the main shading that gives depth (moral dialogue), then the fine lines that make the face one particular person (key words).
- **Unique voices.** Check that every character speaks in a distinct way.

## Terms introduced
- **Ministory:** Truby's view of a scene (and of a monologue) as a complete small story with six of the seven key steps, a twist or reveal standing in for self-revelation.
- **Upside-down (inverted) triangle:** the shape of a well-built scene, and of a whole story: a broad framing opening that narrows to one point, with the most important word or line last.
- **Position on the character arc:** where a scene falls in the hero's development; the first question in building any scene.
- **Endpoint:** where the driving character's desire resolves in the scene. It should coincide with the triangle's point and the key line, giving a knockout punch.
- **Direct plan / indirect plan:** stating what one wants outright, versus pretending to want one thing while pursuing another. Direct plans raise conflict; indirect plans lower it at first but can cause more later.
- **Start late:** begin a scene at the latest moment that still keeps every needed structural piece.
- **Subtext scene:** a scene where characters don't say what they want. Built from hidden, clashing desires pursued through indirect plans.
- **Symphonic dialogue:** dialogue working on three tracks at once rather than the single "melody" track.
- **Track 1, story dialogue (melody):** speech about the characters' actions along the main plotline.
- **Dialogue about action vs. dialogue about being:** the advanced shift from what characters do to who they are, often through a "You are…" statement at the scene's peak.
- **Track 2, moral dialogue (harmony):** argument over right and wrong conduct and over values; at its most advanced, an argument between ways of life.
- **Track 3, key words, phrases, taglines and sounds:** words repeated far more than normal, across contexts, so they build symbolic or thematic meaning (repetition, variation, leitmotif).
- **Tagline:** a single line repeated through the story that gains meaning each time and becomes the story's signature, mainly expressing theme.
- **Prototypical scene/experience:** an opening scene that shows the characters doing what is most typical of them, framing the whole story (Butch at the bank, the Don as judge).
- **Opening as a triangle within a triangle:** the opening scene's own inverted triangle sitting inside the whole story's.
- **First sentence:** the opening scene's principles compressed into one line, broadest frame plus a punch.
- **Values in conflict:** a head-on argument over ways of life, grounded in one disputed action; with moral argument, a form of Track 2.
- **Monologue:** a ministory inside one character's mind, reaching truth through conflict with himself. It must hit the seven steps and end on the key word.
- **Thematic revelation:** the audience's closing realization that the characters' way is the way of the wider world.
- **Chekhov's last ninety seconds:** the principle that the final moments matter most because the closing scene is the story's point of convergence.
- **Double reversal:** a closing scene in which two characters change, not just the hero (*Casablanca*).
- **Patterns of identity / patterns of opposition:** elements the story shows to be secretly the same, and elements it sets in conflict. They are fixed at the big-picture stage, before scene writing.
