## Opening example: why *Tootsie* works
The common explanation for *Tootsie*'s success is that its star dressed as a woman. Truby says that is wrong. The real source of the humor, and of the film's success, was the **web of characters** around the lead that defined him and made his comedy possible. Under the gimmick, each person in the film embodies a different take on the moral issue at the hero's core: the way men mistreat women (Truby, PDF p. 27).

## The wrong way to create character
Most writers catalog the hero's traits, build a plot around him, and then force some change on him in the final scenes. Truby says this fails however hard you work at it (Truby, PDF p. 27).

## Truby's five-stage process
The chapter follows a different sequence (Truby, PDF p. 27):
1. Look first at **all the characters together** as an interconnected web, not at the lead alone, and tell them apart by comparing each with the others by **story function** and **archetype**.
2. **Individualize** each character through **theme** and **opposition**.
3. Build the **hero** step by step into a layered, complex person the audience cares about.
4. Build the **opponent** in detail, since he ranks second only to the hero in importance and in many ways the key to defining the hero.
5. Finish with techniques that use character to **build conflict** as the story unfolds.

## Part 1. The character web

### The biggest mistake: characters in isolation
The worst error in creating characters is treating the hero and everyone else as separate individuals, so that the hero stands alone in a vacuum. The result is a weak hero, cardboard opponents and even weaker minor characters (Truby, PDF p. 27).

Screenwriting makes this worse because of its stress on the high-concept premise, where only the hero seems to matter. Ironically, that spotlight doesn't sharpen the hero; it turns him into a one-note marketing device (Truby, PDF p. 27).

### Characters define one another
Think of the whole cast as a web in which each character helps define the rest. In other words, much of what defines a character is **what he is not** (Truby, PDF p. 27).

**Key point:** for the hero and for everyone else, the single most important creative step is to **link and contrast** each character with all the others (Truby, PDF p. 27). Every comparison with the hero forces you to set him apart in a new way, and you begin to see secondary characters as whole people, as complex and valuable as the hero.

Characters link up and define one another along **four main axes:** their function in the story, their archetype, theme, and opposition (Truby, PDF p. 27).

### 1. The web by story function
Every character must serve the story's purpose, which lies in its **designing principle** (chapter 2), and each has a specially designed role in helping the story achieve it. Truby cites director Peter Brook passing on Brecht's point that actors must serve the play's action, and that excess characterizing detail can work against the play and even make the actor less striking (Truby, PDF pp. 27-28). The audience cares most about how the hero changes, but that change can't be shown unless every character, hero included, plays his assigned team role (Truby, PDF p. 28).

The main functional roles (Truby, PDF p. 28), with Truby's *Hamlet* casting for each:

#### Hero
The most important character: the one with the central problem, who drives the action to solve it. He chooses a goal to pursue (his desire), yet his weaknesses and needs keep him from succeeding. Every other character stands for opposition, alliance or a mix of both, and much of a story's twisting comes from the ebb and flow of hostility and friendship between the hero and the others. *Hamlet:* Hamlet.

#### Opponent
The character most determined to keep the hero from his desire. A mere obstacle is mechanical; the opponent must be after **the identical goal**, which puts the two in direct conflict all the way through. When the goals look different, dig for the deepest conflict between them (Truby, PDF p. 28).
- No relationship in the story matters more than **hero versus opponent**; the larger issues and themes unfold through their struggle.
- The opponent need not be someone the hero hates. He is just whoever stands on the opposing side, and he may be kinder or more ethical than the hero, or may even be the hero's friend or lover.
- *Hamlet:* main opponent King Claudius; second, Queen Gertrude; third, Polonius.

#### Ally
The hero's **helper**, and also a **sounding board** through whom the audience hears the hero's values and feelings. The ally usually shares the hero's goal but sometimes has his own. *Hamlet:* Horatio (Truby, PDF p. 28).

#### Fake-ally opponent
A character who seems to be the hero's friend but is really an opponent. Truby calls this one of the main ways to strengthen the opposition and add plot twists. He is almost always among the richest, most compelling characters, because a **dilemma** usually splits him: in playing the friend he starts to feel like one, and so, even as he works for the hero's defeat, he often winds up helping the hero win. *Hamlet:* Ophelia, Rosencrantz, Guildenstern (Truby, PDF p. 28).

#### Fake-opponent ally
The reverse case: someone who appears to battle the hero but is actually on his side. Less common than the fake-ally opponent because less useful: plot comes from opposition, especially hidden opposition (chapter 8), and an ally, even one disguised as an enemy, can't supply an opponent's conflict and surprise. *Hamlet:* none (Truby, PDF p. 28).

#### Subplot character
Truby calls this one of the most misunderstood characters in fiction. Writers usually take him to be whoever leads a secondary story line (a detective's romantic interest, say), but Truby says that figure doesn't qualify (Truby, PDF p. 28).

His precise function is **comparative**: the subplot sets the hero's handling of a **shared problem** beside another character's somewhat different handling of it, and the comparison highlights the hero's traits and dilemmas. In *Hamlet*, both Hamlet and Laertes must avenge a murdered father; the contrast is that one father's death was a planned murder and the other a rash, misguided mistake. *Hamlet:* Laertes, son of Polonius (Truby, PDF pp. 28-29).

**Key point:** as a rule, the subplot figure is **someone other than** the ally. Both define the hero by comparison and move the plot, but the ally helps the hero toward the main goal, while the subplot character runs a **parallel** line to the hero's and gets a **different result** (Truby, PDF pp. 28-29).

#### Function breakdowns
- *The Silence of the Lambs* (Truby, PDF p. 29): an FBI trainee hunting a serial killer is sent by her boss to a jailed serial killer for help; he starts out hostile but trains her better than the Bureau does. Hero, Clarice; main opponent, Buffalo Bill; second opponent, Dr. Chilton; ally, Jack, her boss; fake-opponent ally, Hannibal Lecter; no fake-ally opponent and no subplot character.
- *American Beauty* (Truby, PDF p. 29): a suburban comedy-drama, so Lester's opposition comes mostly from home. Hero, Lester; opponents, in order, his wife Carolyn, his daughter Jane, Jane's friend Angela (an opponent because he is married and she is a teenager), the rigid neighbor Colonel Frank Fitts, and Brad, the colleague who tries to get him fired; ally, Ricky Fitts, the neighbor's son and Lester's pot dealer; subplot characters, Ricky and Frank. Lester's problem is how to live meaningfully and express his deepest wants in a conformist society obsessed with looks and money. Ricky answers his stifling, militaristic home by dealing pot and filming people; Frank answers his hidden homosexual desire by imposing iron discipline on himself and his family.

### Character technique: two main characters
Two popular forms appear to have a pair of leads: **love stories** and **buddy pictures**. Truby sees the buddy picture as a three-way blend of the action, love and comedy genres (Truby, PDF p. 29).

#### Love stories
The love story exists to show the value of a **community of two equals**. Its central idea, which Truby calls profound, is that no one becomes a true individual alone: a person becomes unique and authentic only by joining a community of two, growing into his or her deepest self through love of the other (Truby, PDF p. 29).

The structural difficulty: two leads give the story two spines and two desire lines, two tracks competing for one train. So (Truby, PDF p. 29):
- Make one character slightly **more central**.
- Lay out **both** characters' needs at the start, but hand the principal desire line to **one** of them.
- Writers usually hand it to the man, since the culture casts men as the pursuers. Handing it to the **woman** is among the strongest ways to make a love story distinctive: *Moonstruck*, *Broadcast News*, *Gone with the Wind*.
- Whoever has the desire line becomes the more powerful character. Functionally, the **beloved is the main opponent**, not a second hero.
- Round out the web with **outside opponents** (relatives who object to the match, for instance) and perhaps rival suitors, which lets you contrast several versions of an attractive partner (Truby, PDF pp. 29-30).

Breakdowns (Truby, PDF p. 30):
- *The Philadelphia Story:* Tracy Lord is the hero. Her former husband Dexter is the main opponent, followed by Mike (a reporter) and George (her pompous, status-hungry husband-to-be). Her sister Dinah is a fake-ally opponent, her mother an ally and her father a fake-opponent ally; Liz, a photographer, is the subplot character.
- *Tootsie:* Michael is the hero, Julie the main opponent; further opponents are Ron (the director), John (the actor playing the soap's doctor) and Julie's father Les. Sandy is a fake-ally opponent; his agent George and roommate Jeff are allies; Ron and Sandy double as subplot characters; there is no fake-opponent ally.

#### Buddy stories
The buddy web is as old as Gilgamesh and his friend Enkidu; Don Quixote and Sancho Panza show a more unequal but very instructive pairing of dreamer and realist, master and servant (Truby, PDF p. 30).

How it works (Truby, PDF p. 30):
- The buddy form essentially **splits the hero in two**, showing two ways of living and two kinds of talent. The pair are "married" into a team so the audience sees both their differences and how those differences make them work well together, a whole greater than its parts.
- As with lovers, one buddy should be **more central**, usually the thinker or schemer, since he devises the plan and launches the desire line. Truby calls the buddy a sort of **double**: like the hero in important respects, unlike him in others.
- Structurally the buddy is the hero's **first opponent and first ally**, not a second hero. Their friction is rarely grave or tragic; mostly it shows up as good-humored squabbling.
- Add at least one **external opponent who is dangerous and persistent**. Because most buddy stories are mythic journeys, the pair also run into a string of **lesser opponents** along the way, usually strangers dealt with in quick order. Each should embody a **negative side of the society**, one that despises the pair or tries to separate them. This defines minor characters fast, tells them apart, and broadens the form by defining parts of the society against the two leads.
- Crucially, the friendship has a recurring **snag**, a sore point that keeps interfering. It gives the story an ongoing opposition between the leads while most other opponents come and go.

Breakdown, *Butch Cassidy* (Truby, PDF p. 30): Butch is the hero and Sundance his main opponent. Next come the railroad magnate E. H. Harriman (never seen on screen) with his elite hired posse under Joe Lafors, then Bolivia's police and soldiers. Harvey, who contests Butch's command of the gang, is a fake-ally opponent. Etta, Sundance's lover, is the ally. Sheriff Ray is a fake-opponent ally. There is no subplot character.

### Character technique: many heroes, and keeping the story moving
Every popular genre has one main character, but some non-genre stories have several. Recalling chapter 1's two extremes of story movement, linear and simultaneous, Truby says multiple heroes are the main way to create a feeling of **simultaneous** movement: instead of tracking one person's growth, the story compares what many are doing at roughly the same time. The danger is showing so many at once that the story stops being a story and loses forward drive. However simultaneous a story is, it still needs some linear sequence, one event after another (Truby, PDF pp. 30-31).

**Rule:** every main character in a multihero story must pass through **all seven steps** (chapter 3). Otherwise he isn't really a main character, because the audience hasn't seen him go through the minimum stages of development (Truby, PDF p. 31).

More heroes mean less narrative drive, automatically; each additional character laid out in depth raises the risk that the story stalls completely. Truby's techniques for restoring drive, paraphrased (Truby, PDF p. 31):
- let one character gradually emerge as more central;
- give the whole cast a shared desire line;
- cast one line's hero as another line's opponent;
- tie the characters together as examples of one subject or theme;
- end one line on a cliffhanger that triggers the cut to another;
- funnel characters from many places into one;
- compress the time span, for instance to a single day or night;
- return to one recurring holiday or communal event, three times or more, to mark progress and change;
- let characters cross paths by chance now and then.

Truby's examples of multihero stories using some of these devices: *Nashville*, *Crash*, *Pulp Fiction*, *L.A. Confidential*, *Hannah and Her Sisters*, *American Graffiti*, *Smiles of a Summer Night*, *La Ronde*, *The Canterbury Tales* (Truby, PDF p. 31).

### Character technique: cutting extraneous characters
Unneeded characters are a leading cause of episodic, inorganic stories. For every character, ask whether he does real work for the story as a whole. If all he adds is texture or color, think about removing him: his small value probably won't repay the space he takes (Truby, PDF p. 31).

### 2. The web by archetype
The second way characters connect and contrast is by **archetype**. Archetypes are basic psychological patterns within a person: roles one may play in society, fundamental ways of dealing with others. Because they are common to all people, they cross cultures and appeal universally (Truby, PDF p. 31).

Why use them (Truby, PDF p. 31):
- They give characters **apparent weight fast**, because each expresses a pattern the audience recognizes, reflected both inside the character and in his dealings with the wider society.
- They resonate deeply and stir strong feeling.

The risk: an archetype is a **blunt tool**, and without detail it slides into a **stereotype**. **Key point:** tailor every archetype so it becomes specific to, and individual in, your own character (Truby, PDF p. 31).

**The shadow.** Many writers since Carl Jung have explored the archetypes and their relationships. For novelists and screenwriters, Truby says the key idea is probably the **shadow**: the archetype's negative tendency, a psychological trap a person can fall into while playing that role. He translates each archetype into practice by giving both its **strength** (beneficial role) and its **inherent weaknesses** in a story (Truby, PDF p. 31).

Truby's archetypes (strength / inherent weakness / examples, all paraphrased; example lists rearranged alphabetically):

| **Queen or Mother** | gives the care and protective shell inside which a child or a people can grow | shields or dominates so tightly that it becomes tyranny, or uses guilt and shame to keep the child close for her own comfort |
| **Mentor** (also wise old man or woman, teacher) | hands on knowledge and wisdom so people live better and society improves | forces students into one way of thinking, or speaks for his own glory instead of his ideas' |

### 3 and 4. Individualizing characters through theme and opposition
With the essential characters set against one another in the web, the next job is to turn those functions and archetypes into **real individuals**. Again, they aren't invented one by one from scratch and then dropped into the same story by chance. You make the hero, opponent and minor characters distinct by **comparing** them, this time mainly through **theme** and **opposition** (Truby, PDF p. 33).

**Theme in brief** (developed in chapter 5): theme is your view of how people ought to act, expressed through characters acting in the plot. It is **not a subject** like racism or freedom. It is your moral vision, your sense of what living well or badly means, and it is different for each story (Truby, PDF p. 33).

**Key point:** individualizing begins with locating the **moral problem at the premise's core**; the story's body then explores that problem's possible variations (Truby, PDF p. 33).

You play out those variations **through the opposition**: assemble opponents, plus allies, who make the hero grapple with the central moral problem, each embodying a **variation on the theme** by tackling that one problem in its own way (Truby, PDF p. 33).

**The method** (Truby, PDF pp. 33-34):
1. Write down what you take to be the story's central moral problem; the premise work in chapter 2 should already have produced it.
2. Compare the hero with every other character on six points: their weaknesses; their psychological and moral needs; what each wants; what each values; their relative power, rank and skill; and the stance each takes toward the central moral problem.
3. Begin with the story's most important relationship, hero against main opponent. In many respects this opponent unlocks the story, since he defines the hero better than anything else and also shows you how a great web is built.
4. Then set the hero beside the remaining opponents, next beside the allies, and last of all set opponents and allies against each other.

Throughout, every character should model a **distinct way of handling the hero's core moral problem** (Truby, PDF p. 34).

#### Example: *Tootsie* (Truby, PDF p. 34)
Truby uses *Tootsie* to show how to grow an organic story out of a high-concept premise. It is a **switch comedy**, a premise device in which the hero suddenly finds himself turned into someone or something else; the form goes back at least to Mark Twain, a master of it. Most switch comedies fail badly because their writers don't know the high concept's built-in weakness: it yields only two or three scenes. *Tootsie* has those scenes (Michael first dressing as a woman, auditioning, and visiting his agent in triumph at a restaurant), but its writers go much further. They give Michael a central moral problem, men's conduct toward women, and a moral need to learn to behave well toward them, above all the woman he falls for. Then they build opponents who each vary that theme, showing either male conduct toward women or what women put up with from men:
- **Ron**, the director, lies to and cheats on Julie, then excuses it by claiming the truth would hurt her more.
- **Julie**, the actress Michael loves, is lovely and gifted but lets men, Ron especially, mistreat and bully her.
- **John**, who plays the soap's physician, is a lecher who exploits his stardom to press himself on the actresses.
- **Sandy**, Michael's friend, thinks so little of herself that she apologizes when he lies to and mistreats her.
- **Les**, Julie's father, falls for Michael-as-Dorothy and courts "her" with flowers and dancing, treating her with great respect.
- **Rita Marshall**, the producer, has suppressed both her womanliness and her solidarity with other women in order to rise.
- **Michael** himself, as Dorothy, helps the show's women confront the men and win the regard and affection owed them; but as a man he hits on every woman at a party, fakes romantic interest in Sandy, and plots to take Julie from Ron.

#### Example: *Great Expectations* (Truby, PDF p. 34)
Dickens is famous for his character webs, and Truby calls this one more advanced than most. Its signature is **double pairs**: Pip with Magwitch, Estella with Miss Havisham. Both pairs share the same basic bond, mentor and student, yet differ sharply. The convict Magwitch, absent from Pip's life, funds him in secret, giving him money and liberty but never teaching him responsibility. At the opposite pole, Miss Havisham rules Estella with an iron hand and, embittered by a man's betrayal, raises her to be incapable of love.

#### Example: *Vanity Fair* (Truby, PDF p. 34)
Thackeray called it a "novel without a hero," meaning no character worth imitating. Every character is a kind of predator, scrambling over others to get wealth, influence and rank, which makes the whole web unique; the choice of web is one of Thackeray's main ways of expressing, and making original, his moral vision. The central contrast is Becky against Amelia, two radically different approaches to how a woman lands a man: Amelia is immoral through obtuseness, Becky through masterful scheming.

#### Example: *Tom Jones* (Truby, PDF pp. 34-35)
This shows how much the choice of web shapes the hero. A picaresque comic novel with a huge cast spreads its action across many people at once and goes deep with none of them; in comedy, its truth comes from watching many people behave foolishly or badly, the hero included. Because Fielding makes Tom a foolish innocent and builds the plot on mistaken beliefs about who Tom is, he limits how much self-revelation and depth Tom can have. Tom still wrestles with a central moral problem (staying true to the woman he loves), but he answers for relatively little.

## Part 2. Creating the hero
Making a character on the page look like a whole person is complex and takes several steps. As a great painter would, you build him up **layer by layer**. Starting from the larger web gives you a much better chance, since whatever web you build will shape the hero heavily and guide you as you detail him (Truby, PDF p. 35).

### Hero step 1: what every great hero requires
Every hero in every story must meet certain requirements, all stemming from his function: **he drives the whole story** (Truby, PDF p. 35).

#### Requirement 1: make him constantly fascinating
A character who drives the story must grab and hold attention every moment, with no dead spots, no treading water and no padding; the moment the lead bores us, the story halts. A prime way to hold attention is to make him **mysterious**: let the audience see he is **hiding something**. That pushes a passive viewer to lean in and take part, wanting to discover what is hidden (Truby, PDF p. 35).

#### Requirement 2: identification, in moderation
"Identification" gets used a lot and defined rarely. People who build characters by piling on traits assume audiences identify with traits such as background, job, clothes, income, race and sex. Truby says the opposite is true: if that were how it worked, nobody would identify with anybody, since every character has too many traits a given viewer lacks (Truby, PDF p. 35).

Audiences identify with a character through **two things: his desire and his moral problem**, in other words, the two opening steps of the seven, desire and need. Desire pulls the story forward because viewers want the hero to win; the moral problem is the deeper struggle over living rightly among others, and viewers want him to resolve it (Truby, PDF p. 35).

But too much identification is a problem: viewers need enough distance to watch the hero change and grow. Truby again quotes Peter Brook on how an actor who sees his part within the whole play judges its sympathetic and unsympathetic sides differently than one who thinks identification is all that counts. Chapter 8 explains when and how to pull the audience back from the hero (Truby, PDF p. 35).

#### Requirement 3: aim for empathy, not sympathy
Everyone says the hero must be likable. A likable (sympathetic) hero helps, since the audience wants him to win and in effect becomes a partner in the telling. Yet many of fiction's strongest heroes are not likable in the least, and they still fascinate. And even an initially likable hero often starts doing immoral, unlikable things once the opponent starts beating him, and still nobody leaves the theater (Truby, PDF p. 35).

**Key point:** what matters is that the audience **understands** the character, not that they like all he does (Truby, PDF p. 35). Empathy means caring about and understanding someone. So the way to keep interest in a hero who is unlikable or acting immorally is to show his **motive**.

**Key point:** always reveal the hero's reasons. Seeing why he chose an action lets viewers grasp its cause (empathy) while possibly still disapproving of it (sympathy) (Truby, PDF p. 35).

Revealing the motive to the **audience** is not the same as revealing it to the **hero**. He is often mistaken at first about his real reason for chasing the goal and learns his true motive only at the self-revelation (Truby, PDF pp. 35-36).

#### Requirement 4: both kinds of need
The strongest characters always have a moral need and a psychological one. A psychological need affects only the hero; a moral need concerns learning to treat others properly. Adding the moral need widens the hero's effect within the story and so raises its emotional power (Truby, PDF p. 36).

### Hero step 2: character change
Character change (other names: arc, development, **range of change**) is how a character develops across the story. Truby calls it perhaps both the hardest and the most vital step of the entire process (Truby, PDF p. 36).

**The "light switch school."** Like "identification," "character development" is a buzzword few understand. The standard method imagines a solitary person, piles up traits, builds a plot around him, and has him change at the finish. Truby calls this the "light switch school": one flick in the final scene and the character has supposedly been transformed. It doesn't work (Truby, PDF p. 36).

#### The self as a character
To understand change you first need a sense of the **self**, since that is what changes, and of the job the self does in a story. A character is an invented self, made to show two things at once: that every person is endlessly unique, and that every person shares a common humanity. Stories then put this self into action through space and time and set it beside others, so as to show what living well or badly looks like and how someone can grow across a life (Truby, PDF p. 36).

Stories have no single concept of self. Truby lists four major ones (Truby, PDF p. 36):
- **A single, tightly self-governed personality,** cleanly separate from others and seeking its "destiny," what it was born to do given its deepest abilities. Common in myth, with its typical warrior hero.
- **One unit built from many needs and wants that often clash,** strongly drawn to bond with others and at times to swallow another person up. Very widespread, especially in modern playwrights (Chekhov, Ibsen, O'Neill, Strindberg, Tennessee Williams).
- **A succession of roles** played according to what society demands at the moment. Twain may be the best-known holder of this view: two of his switch comedies, *The Prince and the Pauper* and *A Connecticut Yankee*, show a person shaped largely by social position, and even *Huckleberry Finn* and *Tom Sawyer* stress how we tend to become what society says we are.
- **A loose bundle of images,** so shaky, permeable and pliable that it can take on a wholly different form. Kafka, Borges and Faulkner are its main literary voices; in popular fiction it appears in horror, especially vampire, cat-people and werewolf stories.

Despite their differences, the purpose of change and the techniques for achieving it are much the same for all four (Truby, PDF p. 36).

#### Change is set up at the beginning
**Key point:** change happens not at the story's end but at its start; more exactly, the opening setup is what makes it possible (Truby, PDF p. 36).

**Key point:** the hero is not a fixed, completed person you then write about. From the outset, treat him as a **range of change**, a span of possibilities. Fix that range at the outset of writing, or change at the end will be impossible (Truby, PDF p. 36).

Truby says he can't overstate this: master the range of change and you win the storytelling "game"; miss it and endless rewrites still won't fix the story. A rule of thumb: the **smaller the range, the duller** the story; the **larger the range, the greater the interest and the risk**, because people alter little in the brief span that most stories cover (Truby, PDF p. 36).

**What the range of change is.** It is the span of who the character could be, bounded by how he understands himself. The moment of change is when the hero at last turns into **the person he was always going to become**. Except in rare cases he doesn't suddenly flip into someone else; he completes a process, running through the whole story, of becoming himself more deeply and with sharper focus (Truby, PDF pp. 36-37).

#### Change versus character change
Because that idea can seem vague, Truby is precise: a character can go through many changes that are **not character change**. Going from poor to rich, from peasant to king, or from drunk to sober are changes, but not changes of character (Truby, PDF p. 37).

**Key point:** true character change means **challenging and changing basic beliefs**, which leads to **new moral action** (Truby, PDF p. 37).

What a character knows about himself is made of his **beliefs**, about the world and about himself: his idea of a good life and of what he'll do to reach his goals. In a good story, chasing the goal forces him to test his most deeply rooted beliefs. In the heat of crisis he discovers his true convictions, chooses which to act on, and demonstrates the choice through moral action (Truby, PDF p. 37).

#### Acting stories and learning stories
Recalling chapter 1's two "legs," acting and learning, Truby traces a long historical shift (Truby, PDF p. 37):
- from almost total weight on **acting**, as in myth, where viewers learn simply by copying the hero's example,
- toward heavy weight on **learning**, where viewers must puzzle out what is happening, who the characters truly are and which events actually occurred before they arrive at a complete grasp of the good life.

Learning-story authors he names: Joyce, Woolf, Faulkner, Godard, Stoppard, Frayn, Ayckbourn; films: *Memento*, *Blow-Up*, *The Usual Suspects*, *The Conformist*, *Last Year at Marienbad*, *The Conversation*. In such stories, change is not just a character picking up some final insight about himself. The audience must **take part in the change** and become different characters along the way, both by living through their perspectives and by working out which perspective they are currently seeing (Truby, PDF p. 37).

The possibilities for change are limitless: the hero's development depends on his starting beliefs, the way he tests them and where they end up, and this is one way a story becomes uniquely yours (Truby, PDF p. 37).

#### Common kinds of character change
Truby lists frequent types, not as required forms but to help master the technique (Truby, PDF pp. 37-38):

1. **Child to adult** (the coming-of-age story). Not about physically growing up, and not, as many writers wrongly assume, about a first sexual experience, which may be tragic or funny but isn't character change. A genuine coming-of-age story has a young person question and revise core beliefs and then act morally in a new way. Examples: *Big*, *Forrest Gump*, *The Sixth Sense*, *Good Will Hunting*, *Stand by Me*, *Scent of a Woman*, *Mr. Smith Goes to Washington*, *The Catcher in the Rye*, *Huckleberry Finn*, *David Copperfield*, and *Tristram Shandy*, which Truby credits as the original coming-of-age novel and, at once, the original anti-coming-of-age novel (Truby, PDF p. 37).
2. **Adult to leader.** The character moves from caring only about finding his own right path to realizing he must help others find theirs. Examples: *Hamlet*, *Braveheart*, *Elizabeth*, *The Matrix*, *The Lion King*, *Schindler's List*, *Forrest Gump*, *Saving Private Ryan*, *Dances with Wolves*, *The Grapes of Wrath* (Truby, PDF p. 37).
3. **Cynic to participant,** a special form of adult to leader. The character starts out valuing only himself, withdrawn from society and after pleasure, personal freedom and money, and ends having learned the worth of setting the wider world right, rejoining society as a leader. Examples: *Casablanca*; Han Solo in *Star Wars* (Truby, PDF p. 37).
4. **Leader to tyrant,** a negative change. The character shifts from guiding a handful of people toward the right way to compelling others down his way. Many actors avoid playing it for fear of looking bad, but it usually makes great drama. Examples: *Macbeth*, *The Godfather*, *Red River*, *Howards End*, *A Few Good Men*, *L.A. Confidential* (Truby, PDF p. 37).
5. **Leader to visionary.** The character moves from guiding a few to seeing how a whole society ought to transform itself and live from now on. The great religious narratives and certain creation myths show it, and writers often borrow the **Moses structure**: in *Close Encounters*, an ordinary man, Roy, is haunted by a vision of a mountain, climbs it and at the top sees the universe's future as a giant spaceship. **The danger:** you must actually supply the vision. Many writers reach the end, discover they have no vision of how society should act differently, and fall back on a white light or pretty nature images. That fails. What's required is a **detailed moral vision**, as the Ten Commandments and the Sermon on the Mount are sets of moral laws; if you can't supply one, don't write this kind of story (Truby, PDF p. 38).
6. **Metamorphosis.** In fairy tales, fantasy, horror and some intense psychological dramas, the character undergoes extreme change and actually becomes another person, an animal or a thing (Truby, PDF p. 38).
   - It is radical and costly, and implies a self that starts out weak, fractured or devastated. At best it shows an act of extreme empathy; at worst it means the old self is wiped out and the new one becomes a prison.
   - In horror such as *The Fly*, *Wolfen* and *The Wolf Man*, turning into an animal marks full surrender to sexual passion and predation, a devolution back to animal roots.
   - Rarely, a beast becomes human: arguably King Kong, who seems to fall for Fay Wray's character and dies for her (the far more predatory producer remarks that beauty killed the beast); the feral child of *The Road Warrior*, who learns humanity by watching Max and ends up leading his tribe; Enkidu in *Gilgamesh*, who becomes human after being lured into bed with a woman.
   - Kafka's *The Metamorphosis* is what Truby calls a "switch tragedy": Gregor Samsa wakes as an insect, a rare case where the change comes at the start and the rest of the story explores the experience.
   - Change this extreme always involves a **symbol**; chapter 7 covers attaching symbols to characters.

#### Building the change in your story
Truby recaps the chapter 2 method (the basic action and its opposites, with *The Godfather*'s W, A and C) and the chapter 3 setup of the seven steps, then focuses in more detail on building the change that will be the story's foundation. He says "build" deliberately, because this is where the story's **frame** is set (Truby, PDF p. 38).

**Key point:** always begin where the change finishes, at the self-revelation; next return to where it starts, with need and desire; last, fill in the developmental stages between (Truby, PDF p. 38).

Truby rates this among the most valuable techniques in fiction. Every story is a **learning journey** for the hero, with or without a physical trip, and as with any trip you must know where you're headed before you set out, or you'll go in circles or drift. Starting from the self-revelation means every step leads there, with no padding or filler. It is the sole way to make a story organic, meaning internally logical, so that each step is bound by necessity to all the others and to make the journey build to a crescendo (Truby, PDF pp. 38-39).

Some writers fear this makes them schematic. Truby says it actually frees you, because it acts as a **safety net**: knowing the destination wherever you are, you can risk events that look off the path but actually reach the goal more creatively (Truby, PDF p. 39).

#### The two parts of a good self-revelation
Because the start of the story is what makes the self-revelation possible, a good one has two parts: the **revelation** and its **setup** (Truby, PDF p. 39).

The moment of revelation should:
- come **suddenly**, for maximum dramatic force on hero and audience;
- release a **burst of emotion** as the audience shares the realization;
- be **news** to the hero, who realizes for the first time that his self-image has been a lie and that he has harmed people;
- **immediately trigger new moral action,** which proves the insight is genuine and has transformed him.

The setup should establish that:
- the hero is a **thinking person,** able to see the truth and know right action;
- the hero is **concealing something from himself**;
- that self-deception is **doing him real damage**.

A thinking person lying to himself looks contradictory, but Truby says it is real and universal; one power of stories is to show how a mind capable of brilliant, creative thought is just as capable of intricate, enslaving delusion (Truby, PDF p. 39).

#### Character technique: the double reversal
The standard way to show change is a need plus a self-revelation: the hero challenges and changes his beliefs and acts morally anew, and because the audience identifies with him they learn when he does. But how can an author express a moral vision **distinct from** the hero's, since the two needn't match? And the author may want the change rendered with greater complexity and emotional force than the usual approach allows (Truby, PDF p. 39).

Truby's advanced answer is the **double reversal**: give the **opponent** a self-revelation as well as the hero. Each is taught by the other, and viewers come away with two insights about living instead of one (Truby, PDF p. 39).

Advantages (Truby, PDF p. 39):
- Through comparison, it conveys how one ought to act and be both more subtly and more clearly than one revelation can; Truby likens it to stereo versus mono.
- The audience is less locked onto the hero and can step back to see the story's larger implications.

Steps, paraphrased (Truby, PDF p. 39):
1. Give both hero and main opponent a weakness and need (they needn't match or even resemble each other).
2. Make the opponent human, meaning able to learn and change.
3. Give each of them a self-revelation, during the battle or right after it.
4. Link the two: each learns something from the other.
5. Your own moral vision then consists of the best lessons from the two.

It is powerful but uncommon, because most writers create opponents incapable of self-revelation. A purely, innately evil opponent won't realize he was wrong; a villain who eats people's hearts isn't going to decide he needs to change (Truby, PDF p. 39). It appears most in **love stories**, which are designed for hero and beloved (who is the main opponent) to teach each other: *Casablanca*, *Pretty Woman*, *Adam's Rib*, *The Music Man*, *Kramer vs. Kramer*, *Scent of a Woman*, *Pride and Prejudice*, *sex, lies, and videotape* (Truby, PDF p. 39).

#### From self-revelation back to need
With the self-revelation fixed, return to the need. Building the revelation first hands you the need automatically: the revelation is the lesson he learns, so the need is that lesson **before he has it**, the thing he must come to know for a better life. To beat the weakness that is crippling him, he has to see past the big **delusion** that rules his life (Truby, PDF pp. 39-40).

### Hero step 3: desire
The third step is the desire line, which chapter 3 called the story's spine. Truby gives **three rules** for a strong one (Truby, PDF p. 40):

1. **Only one desire line, rising steadily in importance and intensity.** With two or more, the story literally heads in several directions at once, losing drive and confusing the audience. In good stories the hero has one overriding goal that he chases ever harder, so the story accelerates and its drive becomes overwhelming.
2. **Specific, as specific as possible.** The test: is there a **particular moment** when the audience knows whether the hero has reached the goal or not? In *Top Gun*, the flight-school commander gives the trophy to someone else; in *Flashdance*, a letter tells the heroine she's been accepted to the ballet school. A goal like "to become independent" fails the test: there's no single moment (leaving home, marrying, divorcing?) when anyone becomes independent. Independence belongs to **need**; as a desire it is useless.
3. **Reached, if ever, close to the finish.** Should the hero get his goal at the midpoint, you must either stop there or start a new desire line, which glues two stories together. Stretching the desire almost to the end keeps the story one unit and gives it huge narrative drive.

Desire lines that pass all three tests (Truby, PDF p. 40): *Saving Private Ryan* (find Ryan and get him home alive), *The Full Monty* (earn big money stripping for a crowd of women), *The Verdict* (win the case), *Chinatown* (learn who murdered Hollis), *The Godfather* (avenge the shooting of Vito Corleone).

### Hero step 4: the opponent
Truby says, without exaggeration in his view, that the secret to defining the hero and working out the story is to work out the **opponent**. The hero-main-opponent link is the most important connection in the web, and it determines how the whole drama builds (Truby, PDF p. 40).

So the writer should **love** this character, who helps in endless ways. In structural terms he is always the key, since **the hero learns through his opponent**: only because the opponent attacks the hero's great weakness is the hero forced to face it and grow (Truby, PDF p. 40).

**Key point:** a hero can be no better than the adversary he faces (Truby, PDF p. 40).

**The tennis analogy.** If the world's best player faces a weekend amateur, he hits a few shots, the other flails, and the crowd is bored. Against the world's second-best, he must play his best, the opponent answers with brilliant shots of his own, they run each other ragged and the crowd goes wild. Good stories work the same way: each side pushes the other to excel (Truby, PDF p. 40).

The drama unfolds from the hero-opponent relationship once it is set. Get it right and the story will almost surely work; get it wrong and it will surely fail (Truby, PDF p. 40). The elements of a great opponent:

#### 1. Make the opponent necessary
The single most important quality. Structurally, the main opponent is **the person uniquely equipped to exploit the hero's central flaw**, and he should go after it without letup. Such an opponent leaves the hero two outcomes, conquer the flaw or be ruined by it; put differently, he is what makes the hero's growth possible (Truby, PDF p. 40).

#### 2. Make him human
Not merely a person rather than an animal, object or phenomenon, but someone **as complex and valuable as the hero** (Truby, PDF p. 40). Structurally, a human opponent always functions as a kind of **double** for the hero. Some writers use the double (doppelgänger) only to make an opponent closely resemble the hero, but Truby treats it as a much broader principle for any hero-opponent pair, offering several ways the two should compare, contrast and define each other (Truby, PDF pp. 40-41):
- the opponent-double has weaknesses that make him wrong others or block his own happiness;
- he has a need growing out of those weaknesses, just as the hero does;
- he wants something, ideally the hero's own goal;
- he has great power, status or ability, enough to squeeze the hero as hard as possible, make a final battle possible, and drive the hero to a bigger victory (or defeat).

#### 3. Give him values opposed to the hero's
Both characters act from beliefs or **values**, each one's idea of the good life. In the best stories these collide, and the collision lets the audience judge which way of living wins out. A large share of the story's force rides on how well this clash is built (Truby, PDF p. 41).

#### 4. Give him a strong but flawed moral argument
A purely **evil** opponent, bad to the core, is mechanical and dull. Real conflict rarely divides cleanly into good and evil. In a well-drawn story, hero and opponent each believe they've chosen rightly, each has reasons, and **both are misguided**, in different ways. Like the hero, the opponent offers moral reasons for what he does; a good writer spells out the opponent's moral argument and makes it powerful and persuasive, yet ultimately wrong (method in chapter 5) (Truby, PDF p. 41).

#### 5. Give him similarities to the hero
The hero-opponent contrast has force only when the two are strongly alike; each then takes a somewhat different path through one shared dilemma, and the key, instructive differences show up best against the similarities. Shared traits also stop the hero from being spotless and the opponent from being pure villainy. Never treat them as extreme opposites: they are **two points within a range of possibilities**, and their argument is not good against evil but a dispute between two flawed people, each with needs of his own (Truby, PDF p. 41).

#### 6. Keep hero and opponent together
This goes against common sense: people who dislike each other usually go separate ways, but in a story that makes conflict very hard to build. Come up with a **plausible reason** that keeps the pair in one location for the story's duration (Truby, PDF p. 41).

#### The Lecter example
Truby calls Lecter (*Silence of the Lambs*) a textbook demonstration of an opponent working on a hero, even though Lecter is really the **fake-opponent ally**, seeming to oppose Clarice while being her greatest friend; Truby dubs him a hellish Yoda whose brutal training far outstrips anything the FBI Academy gives her. In their first meeting he shows in miniature how an opponent hammers the hero's weaknesses until she either repairs them or goes down: after Clarice oversteps and insults his intelligence, he dissects her ambition, her cheap shoes, the poor-white roots and West Virginia accent she has tried to shed, and her teenage sexual history (Truby quotes the speech at length) (Truby, PDF p. 41).

Each of Truby's examples is less a free-standing individual than **the ideal adversary for that specific hero** (Truby, PDF p. 41):
- *Othello:* Othello is a warrior-king who always charges straight in, all force and no cunning. A lesser writer, trusting the maxim that drama is conflict, would oppose him with another warrior-king: plenty of conflict, little story. Shakespeare instead began from Othello's great weakness, insecurity about his marriage, and created **Iago**, poor at frontal attack but expert at striking from the rear through words, innuendo, intrigue and manipulation. He spots the weakness and works it brilliantly and ruthlessly until the warrior-king falls (Truby, PDF pp. 41-42).
- *Chinatown:* Jake is a simple, overconfident, overly idealistic detective who thinks uncovering the truth brings justice, and who also likes money and luxury. Noah Cross, one of the city's wealthiest and mightiest figures, outwits him, then uses money and influence to smother the truth Jake has found and walk away from a murder (Truby, PDF p. 42).
- *Pride and Prejudice:* Elizabeth Bennet is bright and charming but too pleased with her own cleverness and too quick to judge. Darcy is guilty of great pride and contempt for the lower classes, and it is through those very faults of his, and his struggle to overcome them for her sake, that she finally sees the same faults in herself (Truby, PDF p. 42).
- *Star Wars:* Luke is rash and naive, eager to do good, with a huge but untrained gift for the Force. Darth Vader, who has mastered the Force completely, can outthink and outfight him and uses his knowledge of the Force and of his son to tempt Luke to the dark side (Truby, PDF p. 42).
- *Crime and Punishment:* Raskolnikov, a brilliant young man, kills to prove his theory that he stands above law and ordinary people. His opponent, Porfiry, is a minor police official, an ordinary man of the law who is cleverer and, above all, wiser. He reveals to Raskolnikov his philosophy's error and draws a confession by showing that true greatness lies in self-revelation, responsibility and suffering (Truby, PDF p. 42).
- *Basic Instinct:* Nick, a sharp, hard detective guilty of drug use and of killing without enough cause, faces Catherine, his equal in intelligence, who challenges him constantly and exploits his appetite for drugs and sex to pull him into her trap (Truby, PDF p. 42).
- *A Streetcar Named Desire:* Blanche, a faded beauty barely holding on to reality, has used lies and sex to shield herself from her collapsing life. Stanley, a brutal, competitive "top dog," won't let her tall tales stand; believing she is a lying whore out to cheat him and dupe his friend Mitch, he forces the "truth" on her so relentlessly that she breaks down into madness (Truby, PDF p. 42).
- *Vertigo:* Scottie is decent, a little naive, and afflicted with a fear of heights; his old college friend Gavin Elster exploits those weaknesses in a scheme to murder Elster's wife (Truby, PDF p. 42).

## Part 3. Building conflict

### Pressure through distributed attacks
Once hero and opponent are competing for one goal, the conflict must build steadily up to the final battle. The aim is constant pressure on the hero, since pressure is what forces him to change, and how you build it depends mainly on **how you spread the attacks** on him (Truby, PDF p. 42).

Ordinary, simple stories give the hero just **one opponent**. That is clear, but it can't produce a rich, escalating series of conflicts, and it can't show the hero acting within a wider society (Truby, PDF p. 42).

**Key point:** a bare two-person opposition rules out depth, complexity and any real sense of human life; for those, the story requires a **web of oppositions** (Truby, PDF p. 42).

### Four-corner opposition
Stronger stories move past a lone hero-opponent pair to what Truby calls **four-corner opposition**: the hero and his main opponent plus **two or more secondary opponents** (more if the extra ones serve an important function). Picture each of the four occupying a corner of a box, which means making them maximally unlike one another (Truby, PDF p. 42). Five rules (Truby, PDF pp. 43-47):

#### Rule 1: each opponent attacks the hero's great weakness differently
An opponent exists chiefly to attack the hero's weakness, so the first way to tell opponents apart is to give each a **distinct method of attack**. This ensures all the story's conflict is organically tied to the hero's great flaw. The pattern also yields a **complete society in miniature**, each character personifying one of its basic pillars. Truby's diagrams place the hero top left, the main opponent opposite him and the two secondary opponents below, each tagged with an archetype where one applies, and he stresses that the pattern underlies good stories in every medium, genre and era (Truby, PDF p. 43).

**The two comparison diagrams** (Truby, PDF p. 43, images): the first, "standard" opposition, is just two points, hero and opponent, joined by one double-headed arrow. The second puts the hero and three opponents at the corners of a square, with double-headed arrows along all four sides and across both diagonals, so every character is linked in conflict with every other.

**His four-corner examples** (Truby, PDF pp. 44-47, charts; hero listed first, main opponent second; a "+" joins a character's partners or agents to his corner):

| Story | Hero | Main opponent | Secondary opponent | Secondary opponent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Hamlet* | Hamlet (rebel prince) | Claudius, with Rosencrantz and Guildenstern (king) | Gertrude (queen) | Polonius (mentor), with Ophelia (maiden) |
| *The Usual Suspects* | Keaton and his crew (trickster-warriors) | Agent Kujan (no archetype) | Verbal (artist-trickster) | Keyser Soze and his go-between (warrior-king) |
| *American Beauty* | Lester, with Ricky (deposed king and trickster) | Carolyn, with the real-estate king (queen-mother) | Jane, with Angela (rebel princess, plus princess) | Colonel Fitts (warrior) |
| *Wuthering Heights* (1939 film) | Cathy (lover) | Heathcliff (lover and rebel) | Hindley, her brother (no archetype) | Linton, with his sister Isabella (king) |
| *Butch Cassidy* | Butch (trickster) | Sundance, with Etta (warrior and lover) | Harvey (warrior) | Harriman and his posse under Lafors (king plus warriors) |
| *The Philadelphia Story* | Tracy (goddess) | Dexter (lover) | George, her fiancé (king) | Mike, with Liz (artist) |

⚠️ The charts give only positions and archetype labels; Truby adds no commentary on them.

#### Rule 2: put each character in conflict with every other, not only with the hero
Compared with standard opposition, the possible conflict jumps exponentially: the hero fights three characters instead of one, and the opponents can also fight one another (the arrows in Truby's diagram). What you get is fierce conflict and a thickly packed plot (Truby, PDF p. 44).

#### Rule 3: put all four characters' values in conflict
Great storytelling is conflict not just between characters but between their **values**. In changing, the hero challenges and changes basic beliefs and acts morally anew; a good opponent's beliefs come under attack too. The hero's beliefs mean nothing, and never get expressed, unless they collide with at least one other character's, ideally the opponent's (Truby, PDF p. 45).

Normally, hero and lone opponent compete for a single goal and their values and ways of life collide along the way. Setting **values in four-corner opposition** allows potentially epic scope while keeping organic unity: each character can stand for a distinct value system, a way of life that clashes with three others, which gives the story great texture and thematic depth (Truby, PDF p. 45).

**The values diagram** (Truby, PDF p. 45, an image) repeats the four-corner square, but each corner now holds a character's values rather than the character (the hero's values, opponent 1's, opponent 2's, opponent 3's), again with every corner linked to every other by arrows.

**His values example, *The Cherry Orchard*** (Truby, PDF p. 46, a chart), with each character's cluster as Truby lists it, paraphrased:
- Madame Ranevsky (queen and lover), grouped with her brother Gaev (prince): genuine love, beauty, the past.
- Lopakhin (businessman): money, status, power, the future.
- Varya (worker): hard work, family, marriage, practicality.
- Trofimov (student and teacher): truth, learning, compassion, a higher love.
- Anya (princess): her mother, kindness, a higher love.

⚠️ The chart places Varya on the right between Lopakhin and Anya, so this example has five positions rather than four; Truby doesn't explain the extra one.

**Key point:** list each character's values in as much detail as possible. Don't settle for one value per character; give each a **cluster** of values that are distinct yet related to one another (Truby, PDF p. 45).

**Key point:** find the **good and bad forms of each value**. A belief can be a strength and also a source of weakness; seeing both sides shows where each character will probably stumble in the fight for his convictions. Truby's pairs: honest turning insensitive, determined turning aggressive, patriotic turning domineering (Truby, PDF p. 45).

#### Rule 4: push everyone into the corners
Sketch hero and three opponents into the four corners of a box, then "push" each into his corner, so that every one of them is **maximally unlike** the rest (Truby, PDF p. 46).

#### Rule 5: repeat the pattern at every level
Once the basic opposition is set, consider repeating it elsewhere: inside a society, an institution, a family, or one person. Epic stories in particular show four-corner opposition on several levels. Truby then charts three stories that use it on two levels (Truby, PDF pp. 47-49, charts), each with an inner level and an outer "world" level:
- ***The Iliad.*** Among the Greeks: Achilles (warrior, artist and rebel) against Agamemnon (king), with Odysseus (trickster-warrior) and Ajax (warrior) below. In the wider world: Achilles against Hector (warrior-prince), with Agamemnon (king) and Paris, grouped with Helen (lover), below.
- ***Seven Samurai.*** Within the samurai band: the lead samurai and the others (warrior-king) against the master swordsman (artist-warrior), with the apprentice (student) and the Mifune character (farmer-warrior) below. In the wider world: the lead samurai and his team (killer-king) against the bandits (killers), with the farmers (growers) and the Mifune character (grower-killer) below.
- ***The Godfather.*** Within the family: the Godfather, grouped with Tom (king), against Sonny (warrior), with Fredo, later Kay (lover), and Michael (trickster, warrior and king) below. In the wider world: the Corleone family (king plus warriors) against Sollozzo (warrior), with Barzini (king) and Carlo, grouped with Tessio, the driver and the bodyguards (tricksters), below.

The point is that the same four-corner logic can organize a group from inside and also set that group against the larger world.

## Exercises in this chapter

### Writing Exercise 3: Creating Your Characters (Truby, PDF pp. 49-50)
The exercise takes the writer through the chapter's techniques in order:
- **Web by function and archetype:** list every character with his story function (for instance hero, ally, main opponent, subplot character, fake-ally opponent) and, where one fits, his archetype.
- **Central moral problem:** state it.
- **Comparison:** compare all characters on the six points from the individualizing method (weaknesses; psychological and moral need; desire; values; power, status and ability; stance on the moral problem), starting with hero against main opponent.
- **Variations:** check that each character approaches the hero's central moral problem differently.
- **Hero's requirements:** check the four requirements (constantly fascinating; identification but not too much; empathy rather than sympathy; moral as well as psychological need).
- **Character change:** write the self-revelation first, then the need, and check that the revelation actually resolves the need, so that the falsehoods and props the hero relies on at the start must be faced and beaten at the revelation.
- **Changed beliefs:** note the beliefs the hero challenges and changes.
- **Desire:** confirm it is one specific goal running through the story, and identify when the audience learns whether he achieved it.
- **Opponents:** describe how the main and lesser opponents each attack the hero's great weakness differently.
- **Opponents' values:** list a few per opponent; say how each is a double of the hero; give each some power, status and ability; name each one's similarities to the hero; and state in a line each character's moral problem and how he justifies pursuing his goal.
- **Minor characters:** say how any of them vary the hero's particular weakness and moral problem.
- **The four corners:** map hero and main opponent on top, at least two secondary opponents below; tag archetypes only where they genuinely fit (Truby warns that many characters aren't archetypes and not to force it); push all four to the corners, chiefly by making their values differ.

### Worked example: *A Streetcar Named Desire* (Truby, PDF pp. 50-51)
Truby runs the play through the exercise. In summary:
- **Web:** Blanche DuBois is the hero (artist archetype); Stanley Kowalski the main opponent (warrior-king); Mitch, Stanley's friend, and Stella, Blanche's sister (mother archetype), are fake-ally opponents; there is no ally, fake-opponent ally or subplot character.
- **Central moral problem:** whether using lies and illusion to win love can ever be justified.
- **Blanche:** worn down, leaning on fading looks, with no real sense of self; flees into delusion when life gets hard and uses sex and other people to keep up the illusion that she is still a belle. Psychologically she must value her heart over her looks and stop waiting for a man to rescue her; morally she must be truthful when seeking love. She first wants a refuge, then mainly wants Mitch to marry her so she can feel safe.
- **Stanley:** spiteful, suspicious, hot-tempered, brutal. Psychologically he must outgrow the petty need to beat everyone and prove his manhood; morally, his cruelty toward anyone weaker, like a selfish child who must spoil others' happiness. He wants Blanche gone and his old life back, then wants to stop Mitch marrying her.
- **Stella:** naive, simple, dependent on Stanley. Psychologically, she must gain independence and see her husband as he truly is; morally, she must own her part in enabling his brutality. She wants Blanche happily married to Mitch.
- **Mitch:** timid, feeble, incapable of independent thought or action. He needs to break free of Stanley and of his mother (psychological) and to treat Blanche as a person, honoring her decency and her pain (moral). He first wants to marry her, then, on learning her past, wants only sex.
- **Variations on the moral problem:** Blanche lies to herself and others to get love. Stanley's brutal honesty in exposing others' lies tears people apart; his view of the world as harsh and underhanded makes it worse than it is, and his aggressive, self-righteous "truth" does more damage than her lies. Stella sins by omission, indulging her sister's small delusions while blind to her husband's lies after he assaults Blanche. Mitch falls for Blanche's surface lies and misses her deeper beauty.
- **Blanche's change:** from loneliness, false hope, bravado and lies to madness, despair and a broken spirit. Her changed belief: she gets past thinking she must deceive a man, in looks and words, to be loved, but her new honesty is wasted on the wrong man. Her desire fails at a clear moment, when Mitch brutally rejects her.
- **Attacks on her weakness:** Stanley aggressively makes her confront the "truth" of who she is; Stella, mostly unaware of her role, is too simple and too devoted to Stanley to protect Blanche, and refuses to believe Stanley raped her; Mitch, decent but weak and cowardly, courts her, then retreats and abuses her, destroying her last real hope.
- **Values:** Blanche prizes her sister, kindness, refinement, manners, appearances and beauty. Stanley prizes his buddies, his wife, money, sex, women, power and strength. Stella prizes her coming baby, sex, her sister, her marriage and Stanley. Mitch prizes Blanche, good manners, his friends and his mother.
- **Similarities to the hero:** Stanley and Blanche share a scheming, tactical intelligence and a deeper read of the world that Stella lacks, and each recognizes it in the other; Stella shares Blanche's past in the graceful world of the old Southern aristocracy and her hunger for love and kindness; Mitch responds to her love of manners and courtship and values her gentility and what remains of her beauty.
- **Power, status, ability:** Blanche has lost all status and clings to her power to charm men; Stanley rules his circle of pals and knows how to get his way, above all with his wife; Stella has only the status Stanley grants her but is skilled at pleasing him; Mitch has little standing anywhere and is a natural follower.
- **Moral problem and justification:** Blanche believes her lies harm no one and are her last chance at happiness; Stanley sees her as a deceitful prostitute who has defrauded him and believes he is only protecting his friend by telling Mitch her past; Stella isn't sharp enough to recognize that she is helping to destroy Blanche; Mitch feels a woman who has acted like a prostitute may be treated as one.
- **Minor-character variation:** the upstairs couple, Eunice and Steve, fight over his infidelity; when she walks out, he chases her and brings her back.
- **Four corners** (Truby, PDF p. 51, a chart): Blanche (artist) as hero, Stanley (warrior-king) as main opponent, and below them Stella (mother) and Mitch (no archetype).

## Terms introduced

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Character web | The whole cast seen as a network in which each character helps define the others. |
| Story function | A character's structural job in serving the designing principle (hero, opponent, ally and so on). |
| Hero | The character with the central problem who drives the action to solve it. |
| Opponent | Whoever is most bent on stopping the hero, ideally a rival for the identical goal. |
| Ally | The hero's helper and sounding board. |
| Fake-ally opponent | Seems a friend, is an opponent; usually torn by a dilemma; adds power and twists. |
| Fake-opponent ally | Seems an enemy, is a friend; less useful because it supplies less conflict. |
| Subplot character | Handles the hero's problem in a parallel, slightly different way with a different result; usually not the ally. |
| Two leads | The love-story and buddy forms, where one lead must still be more central and the other functions as main opponent. |
| Snag (buddy story) | The recurring sore point in the friendship that supplies ongoing opposition. |
| Multihero story | A story with several main characters, each needing all seven steps, with special devices to keep drive. |
| Extraneous character | A character who only adds texture or color; a candidate for cutting. |
| Archetype | A basic, cross-cultural psychological pattern or social role that gives a character quick weight. |
| Shadow | An archetype's negative tendency, the trap of playing that role badly. |
| Central moral problem | The moral issue at the premise's core, which every character approaches differently. |
| Variation on the theme | Each character's distinct way of handling the central moral problem. |
| Switch comedy | A premise where the hero suddenly becomes someone or something else. |
| Double pairs | Dickens's device of two parallel relationships of the same kind that differ crucially. |
| Identification | The audience's attachment to a hero through his desire and moral problem, not his traits. |
| Empathy vs. sympathy | Understanding a character's motive versus approving of or liking his actions. |
| Range of change | The span of who the hero could become, set at the start; the basis of character change. |
| Light switch school | Truby's name for flipping the hero into "changed" in the last scene. |
| Character change | Testing and revising core beliefs, which leads to new moral action; distinct from changes of circumstance. |
| Learning story | A story whose audience must figure out events and identities, and so takes part in the change. |
| Child to adult, adult to leader, cynic to participant, leader to tyrant, leader to visionary, metamorphosis | Truby's common types of character change. |
| Moses structure | A visionary-change pattern of climbing toward a moral vision for a whole society. |
| Switch tragedy | Truby's term for Kafka's reversal of the switch comedy, with the change at the start. |
| Double reversal | Giving both hero and opponent linked self-revelations, each learning from the other. |
| Necessary opponent | The person uniquely suited to exploiting the hero's central flaw. |
| Opponent-double | The opponent as a version of the hero, with his own weakness, need, desire and power. |
| Building conflict | Applying steady pressure through how attacks on the hero are distributed. |
| Web of oppositions | Multiple opponents in conflict, needed for depth. |
| Four-corner opposition | Hero, main opponent and two or more lesser opponents, each pushed to a different corner. |
| Value cluster | A set of related values held by one character. |
| Positive and negative versions of a value | The strength and weakness sides of one belief (for example, honest and insensitive). |
