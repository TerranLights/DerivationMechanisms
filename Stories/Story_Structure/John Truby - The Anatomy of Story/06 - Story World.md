# Story World (Truby, ch. 6)

Source: John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 6 "Story World," PDF pp. 67-99 (the chapter starts partway down p. 67, after ch. 5's worked example, and ends partway down p. 99, where ch. 7 begins).

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

---

## 1. Why the story world matters (pp. 67-68)

Truby pairs *Ulysses* and the *Harry Potter* books: about as different as two works can be, yet both authors understood that building a distinctive world for a story, and binding it organically to its characters, is every bit as vital as plot, character, dialogue and theme (Truby, PDF pp. 67-68).

### 1a. The "visual medium" slogan misleads (p. 68)

Truby says the familiar claim that film is "a visual medium" misleads. What actually affects an audience is not spectacle but the **story world**: a detailed web in which every element means something for the story and gives bodily form to the web of characters, the hero above all. He says this holds in every medium, not only film (Truby, PDF p. 68).

### 1b. Story reverses real life (p. 68)

In life we arrive in a world already in place and have to fit ourselves to it. In a good story the order is reversed: the characters exist first, and the world is built afterward as an endlessly detailed manifestation of them (Truby, PDF p. 68).

### 1c. The objective correlative and texture (p. 68)

Truby links this to T.S. Eliot's term "objective correlative." Whatever you call it, the world is where a story gains the rich texture that marks great storytelling. He compares a great story to a tapestry woven from many coordinated threads, many of which come from the world. A story can be told without that texture, but the loss is large (Truby, PDF p. 68).

### 1d. The world as condenser-expander (p. 68)

Writers have little time to deliver a huge amount: characters, plot, symbols, moral argument, dialogue. The physical world is a **condenser-expander**: it packs meaning into limited space and time, and the more you pack in, the more the story swells in the audience's imagination as its elements bounce off one another (Truby, PDF p. 68).

### 1e. Bachelard: intimate and exterior space (p. 68)

Truby draws on Gaston Bachelard's *The Poetics of Space*, which finds meaning built into every sort of shape and space: shells, drawers, houses. Bachelard's central point, as Truby uses it, is that inner (intimate) space and outer space keep feeding each other's growth. Truby reads this as organic storytelling: the right world plants seeds in viewers that grow and stir them profoundly (Truby, PDF p. 68).

### 1f. The process in brief, and why it is hard (p. 68)

Summing up: begin with a basic story line (the seven steps) plus your cast, then create the outer forms and spaces that express them, and those forms and spaces produce the intended effect in the audience. The meanings people take from physical forms seem to run deeper than culture or education, part of the human psyche, which is why they affect audiences so strongly. The difficulty is that the writer is working in **two languages at once**, words and images, and has to keep them exactly matched across the whole story (Truby, PDF p. 68).

### 1g. The five-step sequence for building the world (p. 68)

Truby's order of work (the first three steps build the story's space; the last two handle the world over time):
1. Start from the **designing principle** to define the overall **arena**.
2. Split the arena into **visual oppositions** drawn from the ways the characters oppose each other.
3. Detail the world with three of its four building blocks, **natural settings, technology and man-made spaces**, paying attention to what each typically means to an audience.
4. Tie the world to the **hero's overall arc of development**, and add the fourth building block, **time**.
5. Build a **visual seven steps** that tracks the world's development through the structure (Truby, PDF p. 68).

## 2. Deriving the world from the designing principle (pp. 68-70)

**Why start there.** Since the world belongs to an organic story, it takes its shape from the designing principle, just as premise, characters and theme do (Truby, PDF p. 68).

**Why it is harder.** Beyond the two-languages problem, the designing principle and the world run in contrary directions. A designing principle usually describes linear movement, such as one main character developing; the world is everything around the characters at once, a set of simultaneous elements and actions (Truby, PDF pp. 68-69).

**How to connect them.** Take the rough sequence the designing principle gives you and extend it into three dimensions. Start simply: try to find one visual idea that expresses the story's line (Truby, PDF p. 69).

**Worked examples (pp. 69-70).** Truby revisits his eleven ch. 2 stories and adds a one-line story world to each designing principle and theme line. In our paraphrase:

| Story | Gist of the one-line story world |
|---|---|
| Moses (Exodus) | A journey out of a city of bondage, across wilderness, up to a mountain summit. |
| *Ulysses* | One city over a full day, each district a modern version of a mythic trial. |
| *Four Weddings and a Funeral* | The idealized world and ceremonies of weddings. |
| *Harry Potter* | A wizards' school inside a huge, magical medieval castle. |
| *The Sting* | A sham business front in a shabby city of the Depression years. |
| *Long Day's Journey into Night* | A dark house full of hiding places for family secrets. |
| *Meet Me in St. Louis* | A big house whose character shifts with each season and each change in its family. |
| *Copenhagen* | A house shaped like a courtroom (Truby marks this one with a question mark). |
| *A Christmas Carol* | A London counting-house plus three homes (rich, middle-class, poor) glimpsed across three time frames. |
| *It's a Wonderful Life* | Two versions of one small American town. |
| *Citizen Kane* | A tycoon's mansion and private "kingdom." |

(Truby, PDF pp. 69-70)

## 3. The arena (pp. 70-71)

**What it is.** Once you have a one-line world, find a single **arena** marking its physical limits. Truby calls the arena the basic space of drama: one unified place enclosed by some sort of wall. Everything inside belongs to the story; everything outside does not (Truby, PDF p. 70).

**The mistake to avoid.** Many writers, novelists and screenwriters especially, think that because they *can* go anywhere, they should. Truby calls this a serious error: break the single arena and the drama dissipates; too many arenas make a story fragmented and inorganic (Truby, PDF p. 70).

**By medium.** Theater keeps a single arena easily thanks to the stage frame. Film and the novel widen the arena, which makes unity of place all the more needed for building drama (Truby, PDF p. 70).

### 3a. Four ways to create a single arena (pp. 70-71)

Truby is not asking for strict classical unity of place (all action in one spot). He offers four ways to keep one arena while allowing variety of place and action:

1. **A large umbrella, then crosscut and condense.** Establish the story's widest scope, the big world and its dividing wall, near the start, then focus on smaller worlds inside it as the story goes on. The umbrella can be vast (the Western plains, a city, space, the ocean) or small (a small town, a house, a bar). *Examples include* *Casablanca*, *Alien*, *The Matrix*, *Star Wars*, *Groundhog Day* and *It's a Wonderful Life* (Truby, PDF p. 70).
2. **A journey through broadly the same kind of area, developing along one line.** Journey stories often feel fragmented because the hero visits very different, unconnected places, each an episode. A sense of one arena survives if the terrain stays basically the same (desert, ocean, river, jungle), the route is one recognizable line, and the area develops simply from start to finish. *Examples include* *Titanic*, *The African Queen* and *The Wild Bunch* (Truby, PDF p. 70).
3. **A circular journey through broadly the same area.** Like the second way, but the hero ends up back home. You lose the one-directional line, but going from home to home spotlights how the character has changed against a world that has not. *Examples include* *Finding Nemo*, *Heart of Darkness*, *The Wizard of Oz* and *Ulysses* (Truby, PDF p. 70).
4. **The fish out of water.** Begin the hero in a first arena long enough to show the talents special to that world, then jump him, without a journey, to a second world where those seemingly misplaced talents work just as well. Strictly this gives two arenas, so such stories often feel like two halves; what holds them together is the hero relying on the same talents in each place, so the audience senses that the two worlds, different on the surface, are the same underneath. *Key:* don't linger in the first arena; once it has shown the hero's talents, its job is done. *Examples include* *Beverly Hills Cop* and *Crocodile Dundee*, and to a lesser degree *Witness* and *Dances with Wolves* (Truby, PDF pp. 70-71).

### 3b. Oppositions within the arena (pp. 71-72)

**Principle.** You don't make characters to populate a world, however wonderful; you make a world to express the characters, especially the hero (Truby, PDF p. 71).

**Method.** Just as the character web is defined by oppositions among characters, the world inside the arena is defined by **visual oppositions**. Go back to the character web, list every way the characters fight, and focus on the clash of **values**, since values are what they are really fighting over. Visual oppositions will start to emerge from these. Tease them out and identify the three or four central ones (Truby, PDF p. 71).

**Examples:**
- *It's a Wonderful Life*: the whole town, shown in two versions, directly expresses the George-Potter opposition; Pottersville is one-man rule and greed made physical, Bedford Falls is democracy, decency and kindness made physical (Truby, PDF p. 71).
- *Sunset Boulevard*: Joe's poky apartment against Norma's decaying mansion; bright, open, modern Los Angeles against a dark Gothic house; youth against age; struggling outsiders against the secure, ruthless studio; ordinary industry workers against star royalty (Truby, PDF p. 71).
- *The Great Gatsby*: the main oppositions form a four-corner set (Gatsby-Tom, Gatsby-Daisy, Gatsby-Nick, Nick-Tom). From these come the flat Midwest against the towers and mansions of the East; old-ish East Egg against flashier West Egg; Tom and Daisy's restrained opulence against Gatsby's gaudiness; Nick's rented cottage overlooking Gatsby's hollow parties; Tom's mansion against his mistress's gas station; the valley of ashes as the hidden waste of the capitalist machine; and, finally, modern New York against the untouched, promising land it once was (Truby, PDF p. 71).
- *King Kong*: the Denham-Kong opposition becomes New York (man-made, over-civilized, harsh, ruled by the image-maker) against Skull Island (raw nature, ruled by brute force), with a three-way contrast inside it among city dwellers, islanders and prehistoric beasts, each in its own struggle to survive (Truby, PDF p. 71).
- *Dances with Wolves*: the oppositions shift as the values shift. First, a slavery-corrupted Civil War East against the fresh Western plains; then, on the plains, apparently Dunbar the nation-builder against the Sioux. Blake undercuts that through the subworlds: Dunbar's outpost is a lifeless mud hole, the Sioux village a small utopia by the river. The deeper opposition turns out to be an expansionist America that regards animals and Indians as things to destroy, against an Indian world living with nature (Truby, PDF pp. 71-72).
- *L.A. Confidential*: the apparent opposition (cops against killers) is really detectives with competing ideas of justice against a murderous captain and corrupt DA. So the first visual opposition, in voice-over, is Los Angeles as seeming paradise against Los Angeles as racist and corrupt; it then subdivides with the three lead cops, and the investigation plays out across rich, white, corrupt L.A. (which commits the crime) and poor black L.A. (which is blamed) (Truby, PDF p. 72).

## 4. Detailing the story world: the building blocks (p. 72)

You detail the world and its visual oppositions by combining three main elements: natural settings (the land), man-made spaces (the people) and tools (technology). Time, the fourth, is how the world develops across the story, treated later (Truby, PDF p. 72).

---

## 5. Natural settings (pp. 72-75)

**Rule.** Never pick a natural setting by chance; each carries many meanings for an audience. Truby quotes Bachelard on how the cosmos shapes people and the house reshapes them. Know the typical meanings so you can choose the setting that fits your characters, theme and story line best (Truby, PDF p. 72).

### 5a. Ocean (p. 72)
The imagination splits the ocean into **surface** and **deep**. The surface is the purest flat, two-dimensional landscape, natural yet abstract, like a giant game board, which sharpens the sense of a life-and-death contest. The deep is the most fully three-dimensional landscape, where everything floats at every level; weightlessness is a common feature of imagined utopias, so the deep often hosts dream-worlds. But the deep is also a vast graveyard, an impersonal force pulling things down, and a cavern where lost worlds, ancient creatures, secrets and treasure lie waiting to be found. *Examples include* *Moby-Dick*, *Jaws*, *Titanic*, *The Hunt for Red October* and *Finding Nemo* (Truby, PDF p. 72).

### 5b. Outer space (pp. 72-73)
Space is the ocean "out there," an infinite black emptiness concealing countless other worlds. Like the deep it has three dimensions; like the surface it feels both natural and abstract. Because everything moves through blackness, each thing stands out in its essential quality: the ship, the human, the robot, the alien. That is one reason science fiction often uses the myth form, which deals in journeys and in humanity's most basic distinctions. Its promise of endless other worlds makes it a place of endless adventure, both thrilling and frightening; Truby says it is now the one natural setting left where limitless adventure still feels possible (the sea remains mostly unexplored, but we can't picture real communities living there outside fantasy). *Examples include* *2001*, *Dune*, *Star Wars*, *Blade Runner*, *Alien* and *Star Trek* (Truby, PDF pp. 72-73).

### 5c. Forest (p. 73)
The forest's core quality is a **natural cathedral**: tall, sheltering trees like ancient sages assuring us that all will work out in time; a place for contemplatives and secret lovers. Its inward gaze also carries foreboding: people get lost there, ghosts and past lives hide there, hunters stalk prey, often human. Compared with the jungle the forest is tamer; when it frightens, it attacks the mind first, and it works more slowly than the jungle, though it can still kill. *Examples include* many fairy tales, *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, *The Lord of the Rings*, *Miller's Crossing* and *The Blair Witch Project* (Truby, PDF p. 73).

### 5d. Jungle (p. 73)
The jungle stands for nature in its raw state, felt above all as suffocation; everything grabs at you. It gives the strongest sense of nature's power over humans, who are reduced to beasts there. Oddly, so primitive a setting is also one of two that convey evolution, today's theory of change. *Examples include* *Heart of Darkness*, *Apocalypse Now*, *King Kong*, *The African Queen* and *Jurassic Park* (Truby, PDF p. 73).

### 5e. Desert and ice (p. 73)
Desert and ice mean death and dying, always; even stories struggle to grow there. Both feel impersonally brutal. If anything of value emerges from them, it is because strong-willed people went there to be hardened and to grow through isolation. A rare ice utopia is the village in Mark Helprin's *Winter's Tale*, whose community grows stronger when winter cuts it off and freezes its lake for play. *Examples include* *Lawrence of Arabia*, *Fargo*, *The Call of the Wild*, *Dune* and *The Gold Rush* (Truby, PDF p. 73).

### 5f. Island (pp. 73-74)
Ideal for stories with a social dimension. Like ocean and space it is both abstract and wholly natural: a miniature earth, land ringed by water, separate by definition. So in stories it is **humanity's laboratory**, a solitary paradise or hell, a place where a unique world can be constructed and new ways of living tried; hence its frequent use for utopias and dystopias, and, more so than the jungle, for showing evolution at work. *Examples include* *Robinson Crusoe*, *The Tempest*, *Lord of the Flies*, *Cast Away*, *Lost* and, Truby says with a wink, the greatest island story of all, *Gilligan's Island* (Truby, PDF pp. 73-74).

Truby calls the island the richest natural setting for story possibilities and says its meaning is best expressed through structure. His pattern, in summary, with the structure step he attaches to each: establish the ordinary society and the characters' places in it (need); send them to an island (desire); set up a new society with its own rules and values (desire); recast the characters' relationships so they differ sharply from before (plan); use conflict to show which things succeed and which fail (opponent); show them trying out something new when things break down (revelation or self-revelation) (Truby, PDF p. 74).

### 5g. Mountain (p. 74)
The highest place becomes, in human terms, the **land of greatness**: where the strong prove themselves through seclusion, meditation, discomfort and extreme encounters with nature; home of the natural philosopher who must understand nature's forces to live with or even control them. Structurally it is tied to the **reveal**, which Truby calls the most mental of his twenty-two steps (ch. 8): revelations are discoveries that turn the plot and lift it to a "higher" intensity. The mountain thus links space and person directly: height equals insight. The same link works negatively: the mountain as seat of hierarchy, privilege and tyranny, an aristocrat lording it over those below (Truby, PDF p. 74).

**Key point:** storytellers usually pit the mountain against the **plain**. No other pair of major natural settings contrasts visually, so writers often compare them to bring out each one's opposing qualities. *Mountain examples include* the Moses story, the Olympian gods, *Brokeback Mountain*, *The Magic Mountain*, *The Shining* and *Lost Horizon* (Truby, PDF p. 74).

### 5h. Plain (p. 74)
The flat plain is open to everyone; unlike the pressing jungle, it is completely free, so in stories it stands for **equality, freedom and the common man's rights**. That freedom brings cost and conflict: like the ocean surface, its flatness turns abstract and heightens the sense of a life-and-death contest. Negatively, it is where the mediocre live: the herd below, unlike the great few on the mountain, unthinking and easily led, usually to their harm. *Examples include* *Days of Heaven*, *Field of Dreams*, *In Cold Blood*, and most Westerns, such as *Shane* (Truby, PDF p. 74).

### 5i. River (p. 74)
Possibly the most powerful natural setting for storytelling. It is a **path**, ideal for journey-based myth. It is not only a path but a road leading into or out of some place, which makes the route feel like a developing, organic line rather than a string of episodes. Going upriver in *Heart of Darkness* means going deeper into jungle and from civilization toward barbaric hell; going downriver in *The African Queen* reverses that, from a hellscape of madness, isolation and death toward commitment and love. *Other examples:* *Huckleberry Finn*, *Deliverance*, *Apocalypse Now* and *A River Runs Through It* (Truby, PDF p. 74).

### 5j. Caution: visual clichés (pp. 74-75)
It is easy to use settings by formula (Truby's mock example: a hero due for a big revelation gets sent up a mountain). Any natural setting you choose must be essential to the story and, most of all, used freshly (Truby, PDF pp. 74-75).

## 6. Weather (p. 75)

Weather, like setting, can physically represent a character's inner experience or stir strong feelings. Truby lists classic pairings, which we summarize: thunder and lightning go with passion, terror or death; rain with sadness, loneliness, boredom or snugness; wind with destruction and desolation; fog with confusion and mystery; sun with happiness, fun and freedom, but also with rot hidden under a pleasant surface; snow with sleep, serenity, and slow, inevitable death. The instruction is not to just repeat these pairings but to make weather work in unexpected, ironic ways (Truby, PDF p. 75).

## 7. Man-made spaces (pp. 75-80)

**Why they matter even more than natural settings.** They solve one of a writer's hardest problems: how to express a society. Every man-made space in a story is a condenser-expander, a physical microcosm of the hero and his society. The task is to render that society so the audience understands the hero's deepest relationship with other people (Truby, PDF p. 75).

### 7a. The house (p. 75)
For storytellers, man-made space begins with the house: a person's first enclosure, whose physical features shape the growing mind and its present well-being. It is also home to the family, the core unit of social life and of drama, so every fiction writer should weigh what role a house plays. It is unmatched for **intimacy**, for characters and audience alike, but it contains visual oppositions the writer must know to use it fully (Truby, PDF p. 75):

- **Safety versus adventure.** Above all, the house protects; Truby quotes Bachelard on finding the original shell and on the house as a large cradle where life begins warm and enclosed. But the cocoon is exactly what makes its opposite possible: home is the base from which we go out into the world, alternating security and adventure, both cell and world (Bachelard again). In stories, the longing for adventure often begins at the **window**, where a character looks out, maybe hears a distant train whistle, and dreams of leaving (Truby, PDF p. 75).
- **Ground versus sky.** The house is rooted and squat, telling everyone it is solid and trustworthy. It also reaches upward, like a small proud cathedral wanting to raise its inhabitants toward their best; Bachelard notes that even very earthbound beings feel the pull of the air, the house wanting a branch that feels the breeze or a garret that hears the leaves (Truby, PDF p. 75).

### 7b. The warm house (pp. 75-76)
The warm house is large (seldom a mansion) and full of rooms, corners and hidey-holes, so each resident's individuality can flourish. It contains two more opposites: the snug security of the shell, and the variety that only a big space permits (Truby, PDF p. 75).

**Technique: the buzzing household.** Writers heighten warmth with what Truby calls the "buzzing household," Pieter Brueghel's busy-scene method (as in *The Hunters in the Snow*) applied to a home: every member of an extended family busy in a separate pocket, people and little groups coming together for a moment, then drifting apart again. It is perfect community at household scale; everyone is both a separate person and a member of a nurturing family, and even scattered through the house they seem linked by a gentle spirit the audience can feel. *Examples include* *Meet Me in St. Louis*, *The Royal Tenenbaums*, *You Can't Take It with You*, *Mary Poppins* and TV's *The Waltons* (Truby, PDF pp. 75-76).

**Why it works.** The warm house speaks to the audience's memory of childhood, real or imagined: every house seemed big and cozy when we were small, and those who grew up in hovels can still see the childhood they wished for. Hence its use in memory stories such as Jean Shepherd's *A Christmas Story*, and American storytellers' fondness for rambling Victorian houses full of gables and corners (Truby, PDF p. 76).

**The bar as a house.** A bar works as a kind of house, and may be either warm or frightening. In *Cheers* it is a utopia where everyone is known; regulars keep the same seats, make the same mistakes, hold the same quirky relationships; it feels warm partly because nobody has to change (Truby, PDF p. 76).

**Extended example: *Casablanca* (p. 76).** Truby says the world is as vital to *Casablanca* as to any advanced fantasy or science fiction, and it is centered on Rick's Café Américain. What makes it unique is that it is both **dystopia and utopia**, home of the king of the underworld:
- *Dystopia:* everyone wants out of Casablanca and kills time here waiting; there is no exit; it runs on money-grubbing and bribery, mirroring Rick's despair, selfishness and cynicism.
- *Utopia:* Rick is master here, a king in his lair receiving his courtiers; the café is a big warm house full of nooks, each filled by a character who knows and enjoys his place (Carl, Sascha, Abdul, Emil, Sam, Berger), down to the ideal hiding spot for the transit letters, under the lid of Sam's piano.
- In a land full of contradictions it is the home of cool, embodied in the impeccably dressed, ever-witty Rick, yet it lives at night, and its brooding king, who calls two murdered couriers the "honored dead," is Hades.
- By sealing a world that is both, the writers create what Truby calls a **Möbius-strip** world that never stops: forever open, refugees forever gathering, the captain forever gambling, the Germans forever strutting. It is the perfect community nobody in the audience wants to leave, though every character in it wants an exit visa (Truby, PDF p. 76).

### 7c. The terrifying house (pp. 76-77)
**What it is.** The warm house's mirror image: usually a house that has turned from **cocoon into prison**. The strongest examples terrify because the house grows out of the character's great weakness and need; it gives physical form to the hero's greatest fear. Taken to the limit, the character's mind has decayed and the house lies in ruins, yet it imprisons just as powerfully (Truby, PDF p. 76).

*Examples:* Miss Havisham in *Great Expectations*, enslaved in her decaying mansion after martyring herself to unrequited love, her house a picture of her bitter mind; *Wuthering Heights*, where the house is a horrible prison since it's where Cathy abandoned true love, and where Heathcliff's bitterness drives him to terrible acts against its people in her name (Truby, PDF p. 76).

**In horror.** The haunted house is so central to horror that it counts as one of the form's distinctive beats. Structurally it stands for the **power of the past over the present**; the house becomes an instrument of vengeance for the parents' sins. It needn't be a creaking mansion with secret passages; it can be an ordinary suburban house (*A Nightmare on Elm Street*, *Poltergeist*) or a grand mountaintop hotel (*The Shining*), where solitude and the hotel's sinful past drive the hero mad instead of inspiring great thoughts (Truby, PDF pp. 76-77).

**The Gothic hulk.** If the terrifying house is a great Gothic pile, an aristocratic family often lives there, living off the labor of others in the valley purely by birthright. Either the house is far too empty for its size (no life in it) or it is crammed with costly, outdated furnishings that oppress by sheer quantity. The house preys on its parasitic owners just as they prey on others; in the end the family falls and, in the extreme version, the house burns, swallows them or falls in on them. *Examples include* Poe's "Usher," *Rebecca*, *Jane Eyre*, *Dracula* and *Sunset Boulevard* (Truby, PDF p. 77).

**The modern cramped house.** In more modern stories the house imprisons by being **small**: thin walls or none, the family jammed together, no private corners where each person can become himself. Here the family, drama's basic unit, becomes a unit of endless conflict: a pressure cooker with no escape, which finally explodes. *Examples include* *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*, *American Beauty*, *Death of a Salesman*, *The Glass Menagerie* and *A Streetcar Named Desire* (Truby, PDF p. 77).

### 7d. Cellar versus attic (p. 77)
Inside the house, the key opposition is **cellar versus attic**.
- **Cellar:** underground, the house's graveyard, where corpses, the dark past and awful family secrets lie buried, though never for long; when they surface into the living spaces they usually destroy the family (shocking in *Psycho*, darkly comic in *Arsenic and Old Lace*). The cellar is also where schemes get hatched, coming from the darkest corner of both house and mind; the natural workshop of criminals and revolutionaries (*The Silence of the Lambs*, *M*, *Notes from Underground*, *The Lavender Hill Mob*).
- **Attic:** a cramped half-room at the top, where house meets sky. When lived in, it is where great art and ideas are created before the world knows them (*Moulin Rouge*); it also gives height and perspective, a small window onto a Brueghel-like street of community below. Like the cellar it hides things; because it is the house's "head," its terrors involve **madness** (*Gaslight*, *Jane Eyre*); more often, though, its hidden things are positive: treasures and memories, such as an old chest that opens a window onto who a character, or his ancestors, once were (Truby, PDF p. 77).

### 7e. The road (p. 77)
Among man-made spaces, the road is the house's opposite. The house invites nesting and timelessness; the road summons you outward to explore and reinvent yourself. In story terms, the house belongs to simultaneous storytelling, with everything occurring together; the road belongs to linear storytelling, events following one another along a line of growth. Truby quotes George Sand praising the road as the emblem of a busy, varied life. The road is always fragile, a thin human mark across rough wilderness, so it demands courage, but it promises nearly endless possibilities of who the traveler might become, and a destination worth reaching (Truby, PDF p. 77).

**House and road in myth.** Myth is built on this opposition. The classic myth begins at home; the hero travels, meets many testing opponents, and returns having discovered what lay within him all along. The opening home is poorly used: the hero hasn't become himself there, or felt enslaved. The road tests him, but in myth the road does not turn him into a new person; he has to come home to realize, more deeply, who he always was (Truby, PDF p. 77).

### 7f. Story world technique: the vehicle (pp. 77-78)
Besides too many arenas, journey stories feel fragmented because the hero meets one opponent after another along the way. A key fix is the **vehicle**. Truby's rule of thumb: the larger the vehicle, the more unified the arena, because a big vehicle can carry opponents along for the whole ride; these ongoing opponents, together with the hero, form a single arena inside the vehicle. *Examples:* ships (*Ship of Fools*, *Titanic*), trains (*Twentieth Century*, *Murder on the Orient Express*), a bus (*Almost Famous*) (Truby, PDF pp. 77-78).

### 7g. The city (p. 78)
The city is the biggest man-made microcosm, so big it overflows the idea of a microcosm, with its millions of residents and countless buildings, yet a distinct human experience the writer must somehow convey. Storytellers shrink it to a smaller microcosm. A favorite is the **institution**: an organization with a particular function, boundaries, rules, power hierarchy and way of operating. This metaphor turns the city into something like a military operation in which masses of people are identified, and deal with one another, purely through their role. Typically the writer builds one huge building with many floors and rooms, including one vast room of desks in perfect rows. *Examples include* *The Matrix*, *Double Indemnity*, *Network*, *The Hospital* and *The Incredibles* (Truby, PDF p. 78).

### 7h. Technique: the city pictured as a natural setting (pp. 78-79)
Fantasy takes the opposite route from the institution: instead of locking the city into a regulated organization, it opens it out by picturing it as a natural setting. This turns the overwhelming city into one unit the audience can recognize and, more importantly, hints at its huge potential for good and ill (Truby, PDF p. 78).

- **City as mountain.** Common, especially for very vertical cities like New York: the richest and most powerful in the top towers, the middle class in the middle, the poor in tenements at the base. Stylized crime fantasies such as Batman stories use it (Truby, PDF p. 78).
- **City as ocean.** Truby rates it more powerful than the predictable mountain. The writer typically starts on gabled rooftops that feel like floating on waves, then "dips" under the surface to collect characters living at different levels, mostly unaware of others "swimming" in the same sea (*Under the Roofs of Paris*, *Wings of Desire*, *Yellow Submarine*). It is also the key metaphor for showing the city at its best, a playground of love, style and freedom; in fantasy the main way is to make city dwellers literally **float**, so ceilings become floors, nothing is fixed, and people share the freedom of imagining together. Floating stands for the possibilities hidden in the everyday city. Non-fantasy films create floating with the camera, as when *Under the Roofs of Paris* glides over the rooftops, dips into one window, then "swims" out and into another, a structure the writer designed to evoke a far-flung community (Truby, PDF p. 78).
  - *Mary Poppins* (p. 78): built on the ocean metaphor. Mary drifts down from above; next door a ship's captain stands on his roof as if on deck; the children learn that laughter makes you float; Bert and the sweeps dance across the rooftops (the "waves") until the captain's cannon sends them below the surface.
- **City as jungle.** The opposite of the ocean: the city's three dimensions bring not freedom but death, with enemies everywhere and attack from any side. Typically packed, steamy and wet, its residents shown as animals differing only in how they kill. Cop and detective stories have leaned on it so heavily that it became a cliché long ago. More original uses: *Pépé le Moko*, *Spider-Man*, *Batman Begins*, *The Jungle*, *Blade Runner*, *M* and *King Kong* (Truby, PDF pp. 78-79).
- **City as forest.** The positive version of the jungle: buildings scaled down and humanized, as if people lived in trees, a neighborhood or town amid impersonal towers. Usually utopian: city life's richness with tree-house coziness (Truby, PDF p. 79).
  - *Ghostbusters* (p. 79): a boys' adventure set in New York; its three "musketeers" begin as professors at a cozy, townlike university, then go into business getting paid to wear cool uniforms, drive a souped-up ambulance and fire gadgets, living in a **firehouse**, which Truby calls the ultimate boys' tree house, complete with a pole to slide down. Lots of "floating" goes on in this city.

### 7i. Miniatures (pp. 79-80)
**What it is.** A society shrunk down. Truby likens miniatures to chaos theory applied to story: they reveal **levels of order**, making the larger world's order, too big to see whole, suddenly clear (Truby, PDF p. 79).

**Why it matters.** Every man-made space in a story is a miniature of some sort; only the scale differs. The miniature is a fundamental world technique because it is an excellent condenser-expander: it displays many things simultaneously, with all their interrelations, rather than one after another (Truby, PDF p. 79).

**Three main uses:** (1) letting the audience see the story world as a whole; (2) letting the writer show different facets of a character; (3) showing the exercise of power, often tyranny (Truby, PDF p. 79).

**The Eameses' *Powers of Ten*.** Truby uses Ray and Charles Eames's documentary to explain: it pulls back from a picnicking couple, power of ten by power of ten, until deep space fills the frame, then zooms back and into cells, molecules and atoms. Each level is a complete subworld explaining how that level operates. Story miniatures do the same, but they reveal more than how the pieces fit; they show **what matters**. Truby quotes Bachelard: in miniature, values become condensed and enriched (Truby, PDF p. 79).

*Examples:*
- *Citizen Kane* is built on miniatures: the dying Kane's snow globe with its cabin (his lost childhood); the newsreel (his life in miniature from a distant, pseudo-historical angle); Xanadu (a whole world rebuilt behind walls for his pleasure and dominance). Each gives a picture loaded with values of this wealthy, lonely, often tyrannical man, and their sheer number supports a theme: no number of perspectives or storytellers can let us know another person (Truby, PDF p. 79).
- *The Shining*: procrastinating Jack looks down on a model of the hedge maze, taking a "God's-eye" view, and sees his wife and son as tiny figures; it foreshadows (a miniature in time, Truby says) his attempt to kill his son in the real maze (Truby, PDF pp. 79-80).

### 7j. Shrinking and growing characters (p. 80)
**Why change a character's size.** It spotlights the relationship between character and world, forcing the audience to rethink both radically; suddenly the underlying principles of what they took for granted are laid bare and the world's foundations are entirely different (Truby, PDF p. 80).

**Getting small.** One main reason fantasy exists is to let us see things as if for the first time, and shrinking a character does that best of all. A shrunken character becomes like a little child again: on the negative side, he abruptly loses power and may be terrified of a looming world; on the positive side, he and the audience feel the wonder of seeing the world anew (Truby quotes Bachelard on the magnifying glass restoring a child's enlarging gaze). At the moment of shift the world's principles leap out while the world stays vividly real; the mundane becomes sublime. *Examples:* the backyard turns into a jungle in *Honey, I Shrunk the Kids*; the body becomes inner space in *Fantastic Voyage*; Alice's tears become an ocean she nearly drowns in; to Kong, a subway train is a snake and the Empire State Building the tallest tree he knows. The chief benefit of shrinking a character: he at once becomes more heroic, having to use brain over brawn: Jack against the giant; Odysseus escaping the Cyclops by clinging under a sheep and calling himself "Noman." *Others:* *Gulliver's Travels*, *Stuart Little*, *The Borrowers*, *The Incredible Shrinking Man* (Truby, PDF p. 80).

**Getting big.** Always less interesting, because it removes subtlety and plot: the giant becomes a bull in a china shop, all straight-line dominance. That is why Alice is giant only briefly near the start (Wonderland's wonder would vanish if she stomped through it), and why the best part of Gulliver in Lilliput is the early stretch when he is still captive. Once he towers over the warring factions he makes an abstract point about the absurdity of national conflict, but the story essentially stops; nothing can happen unless he allows it. *Big* looks like an exception but isn't: it is a twist on getting small, a boy waking up as a man, and its charm is the adult body behaving with a boy's mind and enthusiasm (Truby, PDF p. 80).

### 7k. Passageways between worlds (pp. 80-81)
**What it is.** Whenever the arena holds two or more subworlds, you can use a **passageway** between them. It is normally used only when the subworlds are extremely different, most often in fantasy, crossing from the mundane to the fantastic. *Classic passageways:* *Alice*'s rabbit hole, keyhole and mirror; *Oz*'s cyclone; *Narnia*'s wardrobe; *Mary Poppins*'s painting and chimney; *Tron*'s computer screen; the TV set of *Pleasantville* and *Poltergeist* (Truby, PDF p. 80).

**Two uses.** It moves the character physically from one location to the next. More importantly, it serves as a **decompression chamber** that lets the audience shift from realism into fantasy, signaling that the world's rules are about to change a great deal and that they should loosen their usual idea of reality. That matters especially in fantasy, a strongly symbolic and allegorical form whose themes concern seeing life from new angles and finding possibility in ordinary things (Truby, PDF p. 80).

**How to use it.** Take the character through it slowly; the passageway is a world of its own, and should be stocked with objects and beings both strange and organic to the story; let the character linger. Truby calls it one of the most popular story techniques of all: invent a unique one and the story is halfway there (Truby, PDF pp. 80-81).

## 8. Technology (tools) (p. 81)

**What tools are.** Tools extend the human body, taking a simple ability and magnifying it. They are one of the basic links between character and world. Whatever tool a character wields becomes part of who he is, revealing how his power has been magnified and how well he can manipulate and move through the world (Truby, PDF p. 81).

**Where technology matters most.** It counts most in world-centered genres (fantasy, science fiction) and in ambitious stories that set the hero inside a larger social system. In science fiction, since the writer invents the world, the technology created spotlights the aspects of humanity that most trouble the writer; and because Truby holds that all great science fiction concerns the writer's vision of how everything evolves, the human-technology relationship is always central. In fantasy, an object like a magic wand symbolizes a character's self-mastery and whether he uses knowledge for good or evil (Truby, PDF p. 81).

**Systems and modernization.** When characters are caught inside a system, tools reveal how the system wields power, especially in **modernization stories**, where a whole society moves to a more complex, technological stage. *Examples:* the automobile's rise in *The Magnificent Ambersons*; the movie house torn down for parking in *Cinema Paradiso*; aging cowboys meeting their first car and machine gun in *The Wild Bunch*; the bicycle salesman pitching to reluctant posse recruits in *Butch Cassidy* (Truby, PDF p. 81).

**Even in small-scale forms.** Action stories stress how the hero converts ordinary objects into weapons or advantages. In drama, everyday tools are nearly invisible but still help define who a character is and where he stands. Willy Loman, in *Death of a Salesman*, sees his small commissions eaten up by payments on the fridge, the water heater and the car; he is always behind on "the machine" (Truby, PDF p. 81).

## 9. Tying the world to the hero's arc (pp. 81-84)

**The second step.** After identifying the key visual oppositions from characters and values, look at the **endpoints of the hero's development**. This mirrors the character process: first the web, then the hero's overall range of change, starting at the endpoint (self-revelation), returning to the start (weakness, need, desire), then building the steps between, because every story is a learning journey whose end the writer must know first. Do the same with the world: decide what it is like at the start and end (Truby, PDF p. 81).

### 9a. Slavery to freedom (pp. 81-82)
In most stories the hero goes from **slavery to freedom**; when that holds, the visual world will probably do the same. A character is enslaved mainly by his own psychological and moral flaws. Whether a world enslaves or frees depends on how its three major elements (land, people, technology) relate and how they affect the hero; your particular combination defines the world (Truby, PDF p. 81):
- **Beginning (slavery):** land, people and technology out of balance; everyone for himself, reduced to an animal fighting over scarce resources or a cog serving a machine. At its extreme, a **dystopia**, hell on earth.
- **Endpoint (freedom):** the three in balance (as the writer defines balance); a **community** where individuals grow in their own fashion, with others' support. At its extreme, a **utopia**, heaven on earth (Truby, PDF p. 81).

**The apparent utopia.** A third kind of world, usable at the start or end: it looks perfect, but only on the surface; underneath it is corrupt and enslaving, and everyone is frantically keeping up appearances over a psychological or moral disaster. *Examples:* the openings of *Blue Velvet* and *L.A. Confidential* (Truby, PDF p. 82).

### 9b. The world as the hero made physical (p. 82)
These world types exist to be tied to the hero. In most stories the link is one-to-one: an enslaved hero lives in an enslaving world; a free hero inhabits a free world, often creating it as he frees himself (Truby, PDF p. 82).

**Key point:** in most stories, the world gives physical shape to the hero's identity and his growth. Through structure it shows his needs, values, desires (good and bad) and obstacles. Since most heroes start enslaved somehow, focus on the slavery (Truby, PDF p. 82).

**Key point:** always ask how the enslaving world expresses the hero's **great weakness**. It should embody, spotlight or magnify that weakness, or draw it out at its worst. Thrillers, crime and detective stories frequently tie the hero's weakness, when he has one, closely to the "mean streets" he works in (Truby, PDF p. 82).

*Examples:*
- *Vertigo*: the opening rooftop chase leaves Scottie dangling, overcome by vertigo, and a fellow cop dies trying to help, leaving lasting guilt. The world repeats this when his vertigo keeps him from climbing the tower to stop the woman he loves from killing herself. Truby calls this the film's greatest strength: the murderer exploits the detective's own weakness as the central trick of the crime (Truby, PDF p. 82).
- *Sunset Boulevard* (drama/melodrama): the hero's weakness is a taste for money and fine things, and he lands in a decaying mansion with a rich aging star who will pay as long as he serves her wishes; like vampires, she and the house feed on him and revive as he sinks into luxurious slavery (Truby, PDF p. 82).
- *A Streetcar Named Desire*: fragile, self-deceiving Blanche longs to shelter in a fantasy of romance and lovely things, but finds herself shoved into a sweltering, cramped flat ruled by a brutish brother-in-law; the space crowds her until she shatters (Truby, PDF p. 82).
- *Casablanca*: the opening world keeps jabbing at Rick's weakness. His bar reminds him of the love lost in Paris; it runs on money he can make only by paying off a traitorous police captain; every corner shows how far he has sunk into cynicism while the world needs leaders (Truby, PDF p. 82).

**In fantasy.** Fantasy in particular matches the enslaving world to the hero's flaw: good fantasy starts the hero in a mundane world and sets up his moral or psychological weakness in that ordinary world. That weakness is why he can't see the real potential of his home and himself, and it is what pushes him into the fantasy world (Truby, PDF p. 82).
- *Field of Dreams*: Ray farms near a book-banning town, and builds a ball field despite the scorn of neighbors and a brother-in-law who values the land only for money. His need is to follow a passion and make peace with his dead father; the field creates a utopia and a final reunion with his father (Truby, PDF pp. 82-83).
- *Mary Poppins*: the home is restrictive, ruled by a clock-worshipping father. Mary, the apparent lead, is what Truby calls a **traveling angel**: flawless, so without weaknesses, her purpose is to reveal to others what they could become and how their confining world holds them down. The children are self-destructively rebellious and blind to the enchantment outside the door and inside their minds; the father, the main opponent, has the bigger weakness, seeing the world as a business. He never enters the fantasy worlds but benefits from them, and by the end his business world has turned into somewhere he can fly a kite with his children. *Other traveling-angel comedies:* *Crocodile Dundee*, *The Music Man*, *Amélie*, *Chocolat*, *Good Morning, Vietnam* (Truby, PDF p. 83).

### 9c. How world and hero develop together (pp. 83-84)
**Orchestration.** Every major element covered so far (premise, seven steps, designing principle, moral argument, characters) connects with all the others to form one textured, organic unit; Truby calls this orchestration essential to great storytelling. At the start, all elements say the same thing: the hero, most likely, inhabits an enslaving world that magnifies his great weakness, and faces the opponent most capable of exploiting it. (In ch. 8 the hero's **ghost** will be shown to express the weakness too.) Because hero and world express each other, they usually develop together; when the hero stays the same, as often in Chekhov, so does the world (Truby, PDF p. 83).

**Classic patterns of hero and world change (pp. 83-84).** Truby lists paired paths for hero and world:

| Hero path / world path | What happens | Examples Truby gives (selection) |
|---|---|---|
| Slavery → greater slavery → freedom (both) | Hero starts enslaved, declines as the world closes in, then through self-revelation meets his need and wins freedom, in a world his actions have improved. | *It's a Wonderful Life*, *The Verdict*, *The Lord of the Rings*, *Star Wars* (4-6), *The Shawshank Redemption* |
| Slavery → greater slavery or death (both) | Hero trapped by his own flaw and by a world closing in; the rot in his soul means the world relying on him rots too; a negative self-revelation destroys both, or he is crushed by a world he can't understand. | *Death of a Salesman*, *Three Sisters*, *Oedipus the King*, *The Conversation*, *Heart of Darkness* |
| Hero: slavery → greater slavery or death; world: slavery → greater slavery → freedom | Used in some tragedies; the hero-world link breaks at the end. His self-revelation comes too late to free him, but a sacrifice before his fall frees the world after he is gone. | *Hamlet*, *The Seven Samurai*, *A Tale of Two Cities* |
| Slavery → temporary freedom → greater slavery or death (both) | Mid-story, the hero enters a **subworld of freedom** where he would live if he realized his true self; moving on, or seeing its rightness too late, destroys him. | *Dances with Wolves*, *The Wild Bunch*, *Butch Cassidy*, *The Treasure of the Sierra Madre* |
| Freedom → slavery or death (both) | A utopia where the hero is content yet exposed; a newcomer, social change or a character flaw brings decline and fall. | *How Green Was My Valley*, *King Lear*, King Arthur stories |
| Freedom → slavery → freedom (both) | A free world hit by an attack from inside the family or from outside; hero and world decline, but he overcomes it and builds a stronger utopia. | *Amarcord*, *Meet Me in St. Louis*, partly *Cinema Paradiso* |
| Apparent freedom → greater slavery → freedom (both) | An apparent utopia that is really hierarchical and corrupt; ruthless fighting, many deaths; the hero fights through to a juster society, or is simply among the last standing. Truby singles out *Goodfellas* (gangster plus black comedy) as a brilliant variation that runs from the mob's apparent freedom to greater slavery for the hero and death for his friends. | *Jurassic Park*, *Blue Velvet*, *L.A. Confidential*, *The Magnificent Ambersons* |

(Truby, PDF pp. 83-84)

## 10. Time in the story world (pp. 84-87)

**Time as the fourth building block.** With world and hero connected, consider how the world itself develops. Time is the fourth building block, alongside tools, natural settings and man-made spaces (Truby, PDF p. 84).

### 10a. Two fallacies about time (p. 84)
- **The fallacy of the past.** Found often in historical fiction: the notion that the past was a different world with its own values, so we shouldn't judge its people by ours. Truby says it rests on the mistaken belief that the historical novelist is mainly writing history. A storyteller always writes fiction and uses the past like a lens that helps today's audience see itself more clearly; refusing to judge the past is absurd, since we show the past so we can measure ourselves against it. Negatively, show past values that still hurt people now (the Puritan values in *The Scarlet Letter* and *The Crucible*); positively, show past values worth reviving (duty, honor and loyalty at a frontier outpost in *She Wore a Yellow Ribbon*) (Truby, PDF p. 84).
- **The fallacy of the future.** Common in science fiction: the belief that it predicts what the future will actually be (Truby recalls the late-1983 debate over whether Orwell got *1984* right). Stories set in the future aren't about the future; they are another lens, abstracting the present to understand it better. A key difference from historical fiction: future stories emphasize less the values than the forces and decisions facing us now, and the consequences of choosing badly (Truby, PDF p. 84).

**Natural time.** True story time is "natural" time: how the world evolves and thereby pushes the story along. Truby's top techniques: the seasons, holidays and rituals, the one-day story, and the time endpoint (Truby, PDF p. 84).

### 10b. Seasons (pp. 84-85)
Set the whole story, or one part of it, in a given season; like settings, each season carries meanings about hero or world. Showing the seasons change expresses the growth or decay of hero or world in detail (Truby, PDF pp. 84-85).

**All four seasons = circular story.** Covering a full cycle tells the audience you've moved from a linear story (about development) to a **circular** one (about how things ultimately stay the same). Positively, it stresses humans' bond with the land: we are animals and glad of it; the round of birth, death and renewal deserves celebration (*Walden*). Negatively, it stresses that humans are bound by nature like other animals; this is tricky because it can turn dull, as in nature documentaries whose season-by-season plots are entirely predictable (Truby, PDF p. 85).

**The classic one-to-one pattern** (done beautifully, Truby says, in *Amarcord* and *Meet Me in St. Louis*): **summer**, the characters are troubled and vulnerable, or free but open to attack; **fall**, they begin to decline; **winter**, they hit bottom; **spring**, they overcome and rise. Use it, or deliberately cut against it to avoid cliché (decline in spring, rise in winter), which both upends expectations and asserts that humans, though part of nature, aren't enslaved by its patterns (Truby, PDF p. 85).

### 10c. Holidays and rituals (pp. 85-86)
**What they give you.** Another way to express meaning, pace the story and show development. Truby defines a **ritual** as a philosophy translated into actions repeated at set intervals, so any ritual is already a dramatic, visual event you can insert. A **holiday** widens a ritual to national scale, letting you express its political as well as personal and social meaning (Truby, PDF p. 85).

**Rule.** Before using one, examine the philosophy built into it and decide where you agree or disagree; the story can endorse or challenge that philosophy in whole or in part (Truby, PDF p. 85).

*Examples:*
- **Jean Shepherd** (*A Christmas Story* and his Fourth of July TV film): he pairs a holiday with a narrator reminiscing about his family, creating a **utopia of childhood**; the holiday becomes a **time passageway** back to the viewer's own childhood. The narrator recalls the comic mishaps that recurred each year (the oversized snowsuit, Dad's gift that always angered Mom, the neighborhood bullies, Flick's tongue frozen to the flagpole). Shepherd backs the holiday not straightforwardly but by pretending to mock it; the silly rituals warm him because they recur every year and the people in his memory never age. Truby calls this the strength of the **perennial story**. To use it, understand how ritual, holiday and season relate, then orchestrate them to express change in hero or world (Truby, PDF p. 85).
- ***Hannah and Her Sisters***: Thanksgiving, a holiday of community forming to give thanks and of a nation's beginnings. Woody Allen doesn't use its philosophy in the usual way; he builds community through **structure**. The film opens at a Thanksgiving where everyone is with the wrong partner, fractures into crosscutting among six people, regathers at a second Thanksgiving with new but still wrong partners, fractures again, and ends at a third Thanksgiving where each is with the right partner, a real community. Story and holiday become one; the characters never discuss Thanksgiving; they embody it (Truby, PDF pp. 85-86).

### 10d. The single day (p. 86)
**First effect: simultaneous movement with narrative drive.** Instead of one character over a long span, you show many acting at once, today, while the ticking hours keep things moving and compress the story (Truby, PDF p. 86).
- **Twelve-hour clock** (one day or one night): a **funnel effect**; every strand will be settled by the end, and urgency climbs as the end approaches (*Ferris Bueller's Day Off*, *Smiles of a Summer Night*, *American Graffiti*).
- **Twenty-four-hour clock:** less urgency, more circularity; we return to where we began, everything the same. Some writers use that to make change stand out: most things are unchanged, so the one or two that did change in a day matter all the more (*Ulysses*, *Groundhog Day*). The TV series *24* reverses this, stretching the twenty-four hours over a season to heighten suspense (Truby, PDF p. 86).

The circular day shares the thematic effects of the four seasons; both are often tied to **comedy** (circular, focused on society over the individual, ending in communion or marriage) and to **myth**, built on circular space (home, journey, home) (Truby, PDF p. 86).

*Example:* *Long Day's Journey into Night* uses a single day but only about eighteen hours, morning into night, giving a downward line from hope into despair, unlike *Ulysses*'s nearly full, positive circle (Truby, PDF p. 86).

**Second effect: everyday quality.** Rather than cutting dead time to show only big moments, you show the small events and dull details of an ordinary life (Solzhenitsyn's *Ivan Denisovich*). The "day in the life" approach implies drama belongs to the ordinary person as much as to royalty, if not more (Truby, PDF p. 86).

### 10e. The perfect day (p. 86)
A variant of the single day: the utopian moment expressed in time, almost always used for a section rather than a whole story, since total harmony with no conflict would kill the story if prolonged. It usually ties a **communal activity** (the crucial element of any utopian moment) to a single day or night of twelve hours; grounding the harmony in a natural rhythm such as dawn to dusk deepens the sense that everything works together. *Example:* *Witness*, where the perfect day joins the Amish barn-raising with the leads falling in love (Truby, PDF p. 86).

### 10f. Time endpoint (ticking clock) (pp. 86-87)
**What it is.** Telling the audience at the outset that the action must be finished by a set time. Common in action (*Speed*), thrillers (*Outbreak*), suicide-mission stories (*The Dirty Dozen*, *The Guns of Navarone*) and capers (*Ocean's Eleven*) (Truby, PDF pp. 86-87).

**Trade-off.** Strong narrative drive and speed, at the cost of texture and subtlety. It funnels even faster than the twelve-hour day, so it is often used to give action stories epic scope: hundreds of characters acting at once, urgently, without stalling. In such stories (e.g., *The Hunt for Red October*) the deadline is usually tied to **one place** where all forces must converge (Truby, PDF p. 87).

**Comic journeys.** A less common but effective use. Journey stories already wander, and comedy fragments them further, since every gag moves the story sideways while a character is knocked down or diminished. Announcing a deadline gives the audience a forward line to hold onto, so they relax and enjoy the detours (*The Blues Brothers*, Tati's *Traffic*) (Truby, PDF p. 87).

## 11. The visual seven steps (pp. 87-95)

**Why.** Having made the world develop over time, link the world to each step of the hero's growth. The overall arc (e.g., slavery to freedom) is the big picture; structure details it. Structure is what lets you express theme without preaching, and also how you show a richly textured world without losing narrative drive (Truby, PDF p. 87).

**What it is.** A **visual seven steps**: each key structure step tends to have its own story world, a distinct visual subworld inside the arena. The big advantage: the world gains texture *and* changes along with the hero. Onto these steps you hang the world's other physical components (technology, time, natural settings and man-made spaces), producing complete orchestration between world and story (Truby, PDF p. 87).

**The steps that tend to get their own subworld.** Truby notes that two items on this list, the visit to death and the apparent defeat/temporary freedom step, are not part of his seven key steps (Truby, PDF p. 87):

| Step | Its subworld |
|---|---|
| **Weakness and need** | Opening subworld giving bodily form to the hero's fear or weakness. |
| **Desire** | A subworld where the hero voices or pursues his goal. |
| **Opponent** | The opponent lives or works in a distinctive place expressing his power and his capacity to strike at the hero's great weakness. It should also push the hero's world of slavery to an extreme. |
| **Apparent defeat / temporary freedom** | The hero's mistaken belief that he has been beaten (apparent defeat, ch. 8). Its world is usually the **narrowest space so far**, every enslaving force literally closing in. In the uncommon stories that end with the hero dead or enslaved, this point often brings instead a brief taste of **temporary freedom**, usually in a utopia that would be perfect for him if only he could see it soon enough. |
| **Visit to death** | (ch. 8) The hero goes to the underworld or, in modern stories, suddenly senses he may die. He should meet his mortality in a place embodying decline, aging and death. |
| **Battle** | Should happen in the **most confined place in the story**; physical compression works like a pressure cooker, so the last conflict heats to its peak and blows up. |
| **Freedom / slavery** | By the close, the world becomes either free or more enslaving and deadly, physically representing the character's final maturing or decline. |

(Truby, PDF pp. 87-88)

### 11a. Worked examples (pp. 88-95)
Truby then walks through several films, marking for each step the subworld and the attached building blocks. In brief:

- ***Star Wars*** (p. 88): arena is outer space. *Weakness/need and desire:* the desert, where Luke feels stuck; his desire is triggered by a miniature, the Leia hologram. *Opponent:* the Death Star, an abstract shape (a sphere) made real, which fantasy permits. *Combining visit to death with apparent defeat:* the collapsing trash compactor with a creature in the water, not just the narrowest space so far but one that keeps narrowing, compressing space and time. *Battle:* the trench run; a dogfight would realistically be in open space, but Lucas knows the best battle is in the tightest space, so the epic funnels down to one small target at the trench's end, with Vader in pursuit. *Freedom:* the hall of heroes, where the warriors are publicly celebrated (Truby, PDF p. 88).
- ***The Wild Bunch*** (pp. 88-89): a single-line journey through ever more barren land, in a society shifting from village to city, with cars and machine guns the Bunch can't adapt to. *Problem:* a dystopian town where outlaws dressed as soldiers and worse-than-outlaw lawmen massacre townspeople. *Weakness/need:* a bare cantina where the gang nearly splits. *Desire:* a campfire where Pike wants one last score, and Dutch's question about what they'd retire to foreshadows the slide to death. *Temporary freedom:* Angel's village under the trees, the story's one communal, arcadian place, where these men should live but don't. *Visit to death:* a bridge already wired with lit dynamite, narrowing both space and time. *Battle:* Mapache's walled courtyard packed with hundreds of enemies; walls and confinement create maximum compression. *Slavery/death:* a windblown ghost town, with wind added to deepen the devastation (Truby, PDF pp. 88-89).
- ***Meet Me in St. Louis*** (p. 89): small-town America centered on one big house, a society moving from town to city, structured on the four seasons in the classic one-to-one pattern. *Freedom:* summer, the warm house, with land, technology and people in balance (carriage beside horseless carriage), the title song passed through the house from one family member to another like a baton, introducing the minor characters and the house's corners, until the hero Esther arrives singing it; she is happy, without weakness yet, but vulnerable. *Weakness/need, problem, opponent:* autumn, the warm house turned frightening at Halloween, the family starting to break up as the father (opponent) plans a move to New York; the girls' rumor-mongering exposes small-town life's darker side. *Apparent defeat:* winter in a bleak, packed-up house, the sad Christmas song. *New freedom:* spring, the father decides to stay, two marriages, and the World's Fair as a short-lived utopia and a model of America's future, showing individual opportunity need not destroy community (Truby, PDF p. 89).
- ***It's a Wonderful Life*** (pp. 89-91): a social fantasy built to compare two versions of a whole town, itself a miniature of America with two sets of American values. The arena is bustling Bedford Falls; the film rests on Christmas but really follows Easter's logic of death and rebirth. *Weakness/need:* the night sky over the town, seen from God's vantage point, framing the angel Clarence (who needs his wings) and suicidal George. *Desire:* George's buzzing family home, where he announces his wish to build things, a case where the subworld deliberately **conflicts** with the step (usually they match), since the warm house shows a loving family while his urge to leave shows small-town oppression under a tyrant; and the deserted house he stones while vowing to leave, the terrifying house he later lives in and still feels as a tomb. *Opponent:* Potter's bank, his lair; Clarence even asks if he is a king. *Apparent defeat:* the bridge in a snowstorm, a narrow passage where George decides to die. *Visit to death:* Pottersville, a physical image of Potter's values: bars and pool halls, no community, every minor character living out his worst potential, which suggests people are ranges of possibility shaped by their world and values; it ends at the graveyard and back to the bridge. *Freedom:* George running joyfully down the same street, now a winter wonderland; the drafty house turned warm and filled with everyone he helped (Truby, PDF pp. 89-91).
  - **Technique: playful borrowing (p. 91).** Truby notes the film borrows from Twain and Dickens (an angel named after Twain's *Connecticut Yankee* ally, reading *Tom Sawyer*; an American *Christmas Carol* with some *David Copperfield*). Borrow lightly: those who catch it enjoy it, others still feel the texture. You can borrow even another story's designing principle, but must change it enough to be unique; audiences sense the artistry of the change. He also notes the film flopped at first, perhaps as too dark a satire for its day, and won audiences over time (Truby, PDF p. 91).
- ***Sunset Boulevard*** (pp. 91-92): a satire of a modern kingdom whose royalty sell beauty; Hollywood as a court with peasant laborers, narrated by a screenwriter so the world is laced with story references. *Problem:* Joe's shabby apartment and repossessed car. *Weakness/need, opponent:* Norma's decaying mansion and empty pool, the terrifying house that seems a refuge but is the opponent's subworld he will never leave, because it feeds his hunger for money; Joe himself compares it to Miss Havisham's house, and passes through overgrown thorns like a fairy-tale prince. *Opponent, apparent defeat:* the house and Norma revive as he is ensnared; the pool is clean and full, and Norma dries him like a baby. *Battle, death:* she shoots him and he falls into the pool. *Opponent's slavery:* Norma descends the grand staircase into madness, into a sleep no prince will break (Truby, PDF pp. 91-92).
- ***Ulysses*** (pp. 92-95): Truby argues Joyce's techniques apply to popular storytelling in any medium, even if you avoid his complexity. The form combines myth, comedy and drama; the arena is Dublin, mostly on the road, and as a mock-heroic myth the hero returns home with little apparent learning. Dublin sits at the town-to-city shift, oppressive less from technology than from the dead weight of the past (English rule and the Church). The twenty-four-hour day matches the circular space of myth and comedy, and the time overlap (Stephen's morning, then Bloom's same morning) invites constant comparison of the two heroes. Minor characters get their own weaknesses and needs, usually variations on the world's slavery, and the web mixes real Dubliners of 1904 with characters from Joyce's other works. Joyce ties each Dublin subworld (each keyed to an *Odyssey* episode) to one or two structure steps, which anchors readers in a vast epic. Truby's step-by-step list runs, in outline: Martello Tower (Stephen's weakness, need, opponent, ghost); the school; the strand; Bloom's kitchen and the butcher's; the street, post office and chemist; the carriage to the cemetery; the newspaper office; the walk through central Dublin with its restaurant, pub and museum; the National Library (Stephen's revelation about Mulligan); the "Wandering Rocks" section as the whole world in miniature at the book's center; the Ormond bar (Bloom's apparent defeat); Kiernan's pub, a darkening cave where Bloom faces the "Citizen"; the strand again (a false drive); the maternity hospital and pub (Bloom's new drive, to find a son); the brothel (Stephen's moral decision and self-revelation; Bloom's moral act, ironically by blackmail); the cabman's shelter (limited self-revelation for both; Bloom's not purely altruistic decision); Bloom's kitchen and bedroom (the thematic revelation, lifted to a cosmic view); and Molly's bed (her partial self-revelation, and the hint of a renewed marriage) (Truby, PDF pp. 92-95).

## 12. Exercises: Writing Exercise 5, "Creating the Story World" (pp. 95-99)

The exercise walks the writer through the whole chapter. In summary, it asks the writer to:
- write a **one-line story world** from the designing principle;
- define the **overall arena** and say how a single arena will be kept, using one of the four methods;
- identify the **value oppositions** in the character web and assign **visual oppositions** that express them;
- describe the story's particular mix of **land, people and technology** (Truby contrasts a lush wilderness with a few nomads using simple tools against a high-tech city where nature has nearly vanished);
- if the hero lives in a **system**, explain its rules, power hierarchy and his place in it, and, if it enslaves him, why he can't see that;
- consider which **natural settings** serve the story, avoiding predictable or implausible use;
- consider **weather**, focused on dramatic moments like revelations and conflicts, avoiding clichés;
- show how the **man-made spaces** express the structure;
- decide whether to use a **miniature** and exactly what it represents;
- decide whether a character should **grow or shrink**, and what that reveals;
- invent a unique **passageway** if a character crosses into a very different subworld;
- describe the key **technology**, however mundane;
- decide whether the **world changes with the hero**, and how;
- decide whether any **season** matters and connect it freshly to the dramatic line;
- if the philosophy of some ritual or holiday lies at the story's center, decide where you agree or disagree with it and place it at the right story points;
- detail the **visual seven steps** (the subworlds for the steps listed in §11), attaching settings and spaces, and concentrating on three: the **weakness subworld** (how it expresses the hero's weakness), the **opponent subworld** (how it expresses the opponent's power to attack that weakness), and the **battle subworld** (the most confined space in the story) (Truby, PDF pp. 95-96).

**Worked example: *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone* (pp. 96-99).** Truby fills in the exercise. Highlights, paraphrased:
- **World and arena:** a wizard school in a huge magical castle; the series blends myth, fairy tale and the school story, starting in the mundane world and moving to Hogwarts, a bounded place with seemingly endless subworlds, over one school year.
- **Value and visual oppositions:** wizards against Muggles (bland identical suburbia, no magic or community, nature tamed away, against a castle in wild nature that teaches values as well as magic); Harry against Voldemort (Harry's bright "city on the hill" against the Dark Forest and the underworld below the school); Harry against Draco (rival house with its own colors).
- **Land, people, technology:** a present-day story that is really a throwback, a modern prep school in a medieval world, with magic given a high-tech sheen (the newest broom has spec-sheet numbers).
- **Systems:** the fusion of prep school and magic, which Truby calls the gold of the idea, with detailed rules, houses and even a sport; Harry starts at the bottom and learns the system alongside the audience.
- **Settings and weather:** castle by a mountain lake ringed by the Dark Forest; weather used predictably (rain when Hagrid arrives, lightning at the troll attack, snow at Christmas).
- **Man-made spaces:** a step-by-step journey back in time from bland suburb to Dickensian Diagon Alley (with its goblin bank like a hall of the mountain king) to a steam train into the fairy-tale castle; the castle as the ultimate warm house, its cathedral-like great hall the center of community; shifting stairs and passwords as a labyrinth of diversity; and its terrifying places, the forbidden corridor and the trapdoor to the cellar-like underworld with its life-or-death chess game.
- **Miniature:** Quidditch, a hybrid of several sports plus flying and jousting, mirrors the school's hybrid nature and lets rival houses stage mock battle; Harry's position, Seeker, names his whole quest.
- **Big and small:** little used, though the friends are effectively small against the troll, the giant dog and gentle giant Hagrid.
- **Passageways:** the brick wall into Diagon Alley, the platform wall onto the Hogwarts Express, and the trapdoor into the underworld.
- **Technology:** "magic tech," modern power plus the charm of spells and animals (owl mail, wands that choose their owners, the mind-reading sorting hat). Truby notes tools of false value: the wish mirror, which shows a double of the self and a false desire one could waste a life on; the invisibility cloak, which lets you act on desire without cost but with great risk; and the stone itself, which offers unearned growth.
- **Change:** Harry overcomes the ghost left by his parents' deaths and learns love's power, but the timeless school does not change; the school year's seasons tie the students' maturing to nature's rhythms; Halloween and Christmas punctuate the year without comment on their philosophy.
- **Visual seven steps:** the room under the stairs (problem, ghost; Harry as foundling, like Moses and Oedipus); the zoo's snake exhibit and the great hall (weakness and need: tamed, caged nature; the sorting hat naming his potential); the hut, great hall and trapdoor (three nested **desire lines**: the series-long goal of becoming a great wizard, this book's goal of winning the house cup, which turns a loose school year into one measurable track that begins and ends in the same hall, and the second-half goal of solving the trapdoor mystery, which turns the world-heavy book into a detective story with a strong spine); the suburban house, classes, stadium and bathroom (opponents); the Dark Forest (opponent and apparent defeat: Voldemort, kept weak because he must last seven books, drinking unicorn blood); the underworld below the trapdoor (opponent and battle: an enclosed room reached by a long descending stair, like the point of a vortex, ringed with fire, where Voldemort attacks Harry's weakness, his longing for his parents); the room of fire and the infirmary (self-revelation: Harry stands for good and learns he is protected by his mother's sacrificial love); and the train station (new equilibrium: the passageway home, with Hagrid's photo album of his parents) (Truby, PDF pp. 96-99).

## 13. Terms introduced

| Term | Meaning in Truby |
|---|---|
| **Story world** | The detailed web of physical elements, each carrying story meaning, that expresses the character web and especially the hero. |
| **Objective correlative** | Eliot's term, borrowed for the idea that the physical world embodies characters' inner lives. |
| **Condenser-expander** | Any device that packs meaning into little space and time so the story grows in the audience's mind. |
| **Arena** | The single, walled space of the story; inside is story, outside is not. |
| **Umbrella (crosscut and condense)** | Establishing the widest arena early, then narrowing to subworlds within it. |
| **Fish out of water** | Moving a hero, without a journey, to a second arena where his first-world talents still work. |
| **Visual oppositions** | Physical contrasts in the world drawn from the characters' value conflicts. |
| **Subworld** | A distinct visual world inside the arena, often tied to a structure step. |
| **Land / people / technology** | The three spatial building blocks: natural settings, man-made spaces, tools. |
| **Natural cathedral** | Truby's image for the forest. |
| **Laboratory of man** | Truby's sense of the island as a separated place for testing new ways of living. |
| **Land of greatness** | Truby's sense of the mountain; height linked to insight (the reveal). |
| **Warm house** | A big, nook-filled house where each member can flourish within a supportive family. |
| **Buzzing household** | Brueghel-style technique: many family members busy in separate pockets, loosely linked. |
| **Terrifying house** | A house turned from cocoon to prison, embodying the hero's weakness or fear. |
| **Möbius-strip world** | A sealed, self-renewing world that seems to run forever (Truby's term for Rick's café). |
| **Cellar / attic** | The house's central vertical opposition: buried past and plots below; mind, art, madness or treasure above. |
| **The road** | The house's opposite: the linear story of going out and becoming. |
| **Vehicle rule** | A larger vehicle makes for a more unified arena. |
| **City as institution** | Rendering the city as an organization defined by function, rules and hierarchy. |
| **City as mountain / ocean / jungle / forest** | Fantasy's ways of picturing the city as a natural setting. |
| **Miniature** | A society shrunk down to reveal levels of order and, above all, values. |
| **Passageway** | The transition device between very different subworlds; a decompression chamber for the audience. |
| **Traveling angel** | A flawless lead who exists to reveal others' potential and their world's limits. |
| **World of slavery / world of freedom** | Unbalanced, enslaving world (dystopia at the extreme) versus balanced community (utopia at the extreme). |
| **Apparent utopia** | A world perfect on the surface and corrupt beneath. |
| **Subworld of freedom (temporary freedom)** | A mid-story utopia where the hero belongs if he realizes it in time. |
| **Orchestration** | All story elements working together as one organic unit. |
| **Fallacy of the past / of the future** | Mistaking historical fiction for history, or science fiction for prediction. |
| **Natural time** | Story time shaped by the world's own development: seasons, holidays, days, deadlines. |
| **Ritual** | A philosophy turned into actions repeated at set intervals. |
| **Perennial story** | A story tied to a recurring holiday, whose rituals and people never age. |
| **Time passageway** | A holiday used to carry the audience back to childhood. |
| **Funnel effect** | The rising urgency of a short, fixed time span. |
| **Perfect day** | The utopian moment rendered as a span of time: communal activity tied to a day or night. |
| **Time endpoint (ticking clock)** | An announced deadline that drives the story and focuses it on one place. |
| **Visual seven steps** | Assigning a distinct subworld to each main structure step so the world changes with the hero. |
