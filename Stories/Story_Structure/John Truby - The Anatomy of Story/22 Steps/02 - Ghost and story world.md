# Ghost and story world (Truby, step 2)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 121-122 (step text and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example pp. 140-141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. For the story world in depth Truby refers to ch. 6.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

Two elements that are both in place from the story's first moment: the hero's **ghost** and the **story world** he lives in. In Truby's table the ghost belongs to the **character** thread and the story world to the **story world** thread (PDF p. 120).

### Ghost

**Why not "backstory."** Truby avoids "backstory" (the hero's entire life before page 1) as too wide a term to help. The audience doesn't want everything, only what matters, so he prefers the narrower term "ghost" (PDF p. 121).

**Kind 1, the common ghost.** A past event that keeps haunting the hero in the present (PDF p. 121):
- It is an open wound, and often the root of his psychological and moral weaknesses.
- It lets the writer stretch the hero's development back in time, to before page 1, so it is a large part of the story's foundation.
- It works as the hero's **internal opponent**: the great fear that keeps him from acting.
- Structurally it is a **counterdesire**. Desire pushes the hero forward and the ghost holds him back. Truby borrows Ibsen's image of a ship carrying a corpse in its hold (PDF p. 121).

**Kind 2, the rare case: no ghost.** Sometimes the hero's world is a paradise, so there is nothing to haunt him. He starts free rather than enslaved, but an attack will soon end that. Examples: *The Deer Hunter* and *Meet Me in St. Louis* (PDF p. 121). This is the "community start" opening described under step 3.

### Story world

The world is where the hero lives, and it too is present from the start. It is built from the arena, the natural settings and weather, man-made spaces, technology, and time, and it ranks among the chief tools for defining the hero and the other characters, who in turn define the world through their values (PDF p. 122).

Two key points (PDF p. 122):
- The world should **express the hero**, showing his weaknesses, needs, desires and obstacles.
- If the hero starts out **enslaved** in some way, the world should be enslaving too, putting his great weakness on display or making it worse.

The hero is set in a world from the first page, yet many later steps get a **subworld** of their own (PDF p. 122).

## How it works

**Hold the ghost back.** Don't overload the opening with exposition. Writers often try to tell everything about the hero on the first page, including how and why the ghost came about, and that flood of information pushes the audience away. Withhold much of it, ghost included; the audience senses something is hidden and leans in to work it out (PDF p. 121).

**When the ghost comes out.** Now and then the ghost event itself happens in the first few scenes. More often another character explains it somewhere in the first third. Rarely, it is held back until the self-revelation late in the story, which Truby calls usually a mistake: the power of the past then dominates the story and keeps dragging it backward (PDF pp. 121-122).

**Build the world in detail.** Truby rejects the screenwriting advice to sketch the world quickly (unless writing fantasy or science fiction) in order to get to the desire line. Every story needs a unique, detailed world; audiences love being inside a special world, don't want to leave it, and come back to it (PDF p. 122).

**Examples of the ghost:**
- *Hamlet:* before the play begins, the uncle has murdered Hamlet's father and married his mother; then Shakespeare adds a literal ghost, the dead king, demanding revenge, and Hamlet laments that it falls to him to set the broken time right (PDF p. 121).
- *It's a Wonderful Life:* George longs to travel and to build, but fear of what Potter would do to his family and friends in his absence keeps him home (PDF p. 121).

**Casablanca** (PDF p. 122):
- *Ghost:* Rick fought Franco's side in Spain and smuggled weapons to Ethiopia; why he left America is never explained; he is haunted by Ilsa abandoning him in Paris.
- *World:* the film spends a long time on a complex world. A narrator and a map show refugees streaming out of Nazi Europe to a remote North African outpost, all hunting for visas to Portugal and America, penned in like animals. A Nazi officer's arrival, met by the French police chief, shows a limbo of power: Vichy officials nominally run things while the Germans really do. Inside it, Rick's café is his small kingdom, where he rules like a king at court and every minor character knows his place; Truby notes the irony that a film about freedom fighters enjoys such a hierarchy. The bar is also venal, mirroring Rick's own cynical selfishness.

**Tootsie** (PDF p. 122):
- *Ghost:* no single past event, but a long record of being unbearable to work with, which is why nobody will hire him.
- *World:* New York show business, which prizes looks, fame and money and is steeply ranked, a few stars on top and a crowd of unknowns waiting tables below. Once Michael lands the soap role as Dorothy, the world shifts to daytime TV: theater run by commerce, silly melodrama shot at speed, and a chauvinist set ruled by an arrogant male director. Its spaces are cramped actors' apartments and the studio, a place of make-believe that suits a man passing as a woman; its tools are the actor's kit (voice, body, hair, makeup, costume), with a neat parallel between stage makeup and the makeup of his disguise. This world both expresses and worsens his weakness: a chauvinist willing to lie and break faith to win a role.

**The Godfather** (PDF pp. 140-141):
- *Ghost:* not an event but an inherited tradition of crime and murder that Michael despises.
- *World:* the family's Mafia system, hierarchical and run like an army with strict rules, the Don an absolute ruler dispensing his own justice and using murder as a tool. It is laid out at the sister's wedding, where every character, including the hidden opponent, is present; the family's national reach is shown when a Hollywood producer who defies the Don wakes beside his prize horse's severed head.

## Placement

At the very beginning: both the ghost and the world exist from the opening (PDF pp. 121-122). The ghost may be explained later, most often by another character within the first third (PDF p. 121). Together with weakness, need and problem (step 3), these form the story's opening, which Truby treats as one of three types (see step 3) (PDF p. 123).
