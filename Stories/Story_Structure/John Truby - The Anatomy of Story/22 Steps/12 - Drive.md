# Drive (Truby, step 12)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 128-129 (step text and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example p. 141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. The moral side is developed in ch. 5.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

The **chain of actions** by which the hero tries to beat the opponent and come out on top. It is usually the **largest section** of the plot, beginning with the plan (step 10) and lasting until the apparent defeat (step 14) (PDF p. 128).

In Truby's table it is a **plot** step (PDF p. 120). He also gives it as his example of a step that serves more than one subsystem: it is the hero's action toward the goal, and it is also where he may act immorally to win, which belongs to the moral argument (PDF p. 119).

**The moral slide.** Through most of the drive the opponent has the upper hand and the hero is losing. He grows desperate and often starts doing immoral things to win. These actions are part of the story's moral argument (ch. 5) (PDF p. 128).

## How it works

**Key point: development, not repetition.** The hero's actions must change at a basic level rather than striking one plot beat (action or event) over and over. Truby's illustration: two people falling in love go to the beach, a movie, a park and a dinner. Those are four different actions, but only one beat repeated four times (PDF p. 128).

**How to make it develop.** Have the hero react to fresh discoveries about the opponent (revelations once more) and change his strategy and course to match (PDF p. 128).

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF pp. 128-129):
  - **A postponed drive.** Rick's drive is delayed, and Truby insists this is not poor writing. It follows from Rick's character: bitterness has paralyzed him, he believes nothing in the world matters anymore, and the woman he wants is his opponent and with another man. So through the early and middle parts he talks with Ilsa without trying to win her, and at first even drives her off.
  - **The cost.** The delay creates lulls where interest drops. Laszlo's scenes (seeking visas from Ferrari, at the police station, from Rick; with Ilsa; escaping the underground meeting) all pull away from the hero's line.
  - **Two benefits.** First, Laszlo's actions build the epic, political dimension, which gives Rick's final reveal and decision importance on a world scale even though they aren't part of his drive. Second, starting the quest so late lets the climaxes and revelations land in rapid succession.
  - **Ignition.** When Ilsa comes to his room and says she loves him, Rick at last springs into action and the story ignites. The irony is that his burst of action aims at making sure he does *not* end up with her. His goal and motive flip, from winning Ilsa to getting her onto the plane with Laszlo, just after his quest begins, and much of the last quarter's excitement comes from not knowing which goal he is really after.
  - **Key point:** this doubt about which goal he serves succeeds only because it is brief and feeds the final battle's big reveal.
  - **Drive steps.** Truby lists nine. They run from Rick reliving Paris, insulting Ilsa, and failing to make peace with her at the market; through refusing the letters to Renault, rigging the roulette so the Bulgarian couple can pay Renault off, and turning down first Laszlo and then Ilsa (who confesses she still loves him); to promising to get Laszlo out alone, and finally having Carl spirit Ilsa away while Laszlo is arrested.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 129): eighteen drive steps. They include Michael buying women's clothes, lying to Sandy again and again, taking over his own makeup and hair, improvising his way out of an on-screen kiss with a man, befriending Julie and coaching her lines, rewriting Dorothy's scenes on the fly to make her tougher, asking his agent for meatier roles now that playing a woman has taught him so much, being turned down when he approaches Julie as a man, rebuking the director for calling him "Tootsie," and falling in love with Julie on the farm. They end with the producer wanting to extend Dorothy's contract.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): a ten-step drive sequence. Clemenza coaches Michael for the double killing, which he carries out at the restaurant; a montage of headlines follows; Sonny and Tom argue over revenge; in Sicily Michael spots, courts and marries Apollonia; Sonny beats Carlo in the street; Tom refuses to pass on Kay's letter; and while teaching Apollonia to drive, Michael learns Sonny is dead. An added revelation sits inside this sequence (the girl on the road; see step 9).

**Variations.** The drive can be delayed for character reasons (*Casablanca*), packed with many small deceptions (*Tootsie*), or interrupted by an exile and a romance (*The Godfather*).

## Placement

From the plan (step 10) to the apparent defeat (step 14); usually the biggest block of the plot (PDF p. 128). The attack by the ally (step 13) happens during it, and the hero's moral decline begins here and continues into step 15 (PDF pp. 129-130).
