# Opponent's plan and main counterattack (Truby, step 11)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF p. 128 (step text and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example p. 141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

The opponent's mirror of step 10. The hero plans and acts to win, and the opponent does the same: he forms a **strategy** for getting the goal and launches a **campaign of attacks** on the hero (PDF p. 128). In Truby's table it is a **plot** step (PDF p. 120).

Truby stresses that he can't overstate how much this step matters, and yet most writers barely know it exists (PDF p. 128).

## How it works

**Hidden attacks make reveals.** Plot comes mostly from reveals, and reveals come from concealing how the opponent is attacking the hero. So the writer should work out the opponent's plan in detail and pack it with concealed attacks; every one that lands on the hero becomes one more reveal (PDF p. 128).

**Key point:** the plot improves as the opponent's plan grows more intricate and better concealed (PDF p. 128).

**Two parts in the examples.** Truby labels the opponent's **plan** and, separately, his **main counterattack**, the biggest blow that plan delivers.

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 128):
  - *Plans:* Ilsa sets out to convince Rick that she had good reasons for leaving him at the station and that Laszlo has to get out of Casablanca. Strasser leans on Renault to keep Laszlo in the city and to frighten off anyone, Rick included, who might help him leave.
  - *Main counterattacks:* after Rick refuses to sell Laszlo the letters, Ilsa turns up at his place with a gun and threatens him. Strasser's big move follows Laszlo's rousing of the French customers with the band's "La Marseillaise": he has the café shut and tells Ilsa the couple must return to German-held France, or else Laszlo faces prison or death; and that night has Renault arrest Laszlo.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 128): as romantic comedy and farce, each opponent has a plan built on who they believe Michael-Dorothy to be, and the plot piles up their attacks in a rising series. Dorothy must share Julie's room and bed and look after Julie's screaming baby; Julie decides Dorothy is a lesbian; Les asks Dorothy to marry him; John assaults her with his advances; Sandy is furious at Michael's lies. Truby calls this whirlwind one of farce's pleasures, and praises *Tootsie* for giving it emotional weight that most farces lack: each gender switch toys with people's love and tangles it, faster and more intricately.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): Barzini, the main opponent, plans to use Sollozzo as his front in the attempt on Don Corleone. Once the Don is out of action, Barzini pays Carlo to draw Sonny into an ambush, and bribes the Sicilian bodyguard to murder Michael.

**Variation: many opponents, many plans.** In farce each opponent runs a separate plan, and the step becomes an accelerating series of attacks rather than a single counterattack (*Tootsie*). In a hierarchy of opponents, the hidden top opponent works through fronts and fake allies (*The Godfather*; compare the iceberg technique in step 7).

## Placement

After the hero's own plan (the previous step), and it plays out during the drive (step 12). Truby gives no page or percentage. In *Casablanca* the main counterattacks fall in the middle stretch, before the third revelation (PDF p. 128).
