# Plan (Truby, step 10)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 127-128 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the plot technique "Training"); *The Godfather* example p. 141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. The plan is also one of the seven key steps in ch. 3.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

The **guidelines and strategies** the hero means to use to overcome the opponent and win the goal (PDF p. 127). In Truby's table it is a **plot** step (PDF p. 120), and it is the fourth of the **seven key steps**.

## How it works

**Key point: the first plan should fail.** If the hero simply carries out his plan, the plot is predictable and the hero shallow. In good stories the hero's first plan nearly always fails, because at this stage the opponent is too strong. The hero has to dig deeper and devise something smarter, a strategy that reckons with the opponent's strength and weapons (PDF p. 127).

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 127): Rick's first plan for getting Ilsa back is arrogant and passive at once. Certain she'll end up at his door, he says so to her face. His real plan arrives fairly late: use the exit visas Ugarte left with him to get Ilsa and Laszlo away from the Nazis. A late plan has an advantage: the twists (reveals) near the end come in quick, breathtaking succession.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 127): Michael has to keep up his female disguise while persuading Julie to leave her boyfriend, Ron; keep Les and John at arm's length without either finding out Dorothy is a man; and fool Sandy about both his feelings for her and his soap-opera job.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): plan one is to murder Sollozzo along with the police captain guarding him; plan two is to wipe out every rival family's boss in a single stroke.

**Variation.** The examples show a hero with two plans: an initial plan, and a later, better one (Rick, Michael Corleone). This fits the key point that the first plan usually fails.

### Plot technique: training (PDF pp. 127-128)

- Most heroes are already trained for what they must do. Their early failures come from not having looked inward and faced their weaknesses, not from a lack of skill.
- In some genres, though, training matters a great deal and is often the audience's favorite part: sports stories, war stories (*The Dirty Dozen* and other suicide-mission tales) and heist or caper stories (*Ocean's Eleven*).
- When training is used, it usually follows the plan directly, ahead of the main lines of action and conflict.

## Placement

After the first revelation and decision (step 9). The plan opens the **drive**, which Truby says runs from the plan (step 10) to the apparent defeat (step 14) (PDF p. 128). Training, if any, sits between the plan and the start of the main action (PDF p. 128). A story may hold back the hero's main plan until late, as *Casablanca* does (PDF p. 127).
