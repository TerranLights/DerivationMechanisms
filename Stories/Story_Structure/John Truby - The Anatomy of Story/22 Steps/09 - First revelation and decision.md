# First revelation and decision (changed desire, changed motive) (Truby, step 9)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 126-127 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the technique "Added revelations"); *The Godfather* examples pp. 141 (step 9) and 141 (added revelation inside the drive); step list p. 119; thread table p. 120.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

At this point the hero receives a **revelation** (a "reveal"): a surprising piece of information he didn't have. It forces him to **decide** something and set off in a new direction, and it makes him adjust both his **desire** and his **motive**, where motive means the reason he wants the goal. Truby wants all four (revelation, decision, changed desire, changed motive) to happen together, in the same moment (PDF p. 126).

In Truby's table the revelation and decision belong to the **plot** thread and the changed desire and motive to the **character** thread (PDF p. 120).

## How it works

**Reveals are the keys to plot.** They are usually what average stories lack, and a plot is, to a large degree, only as good as its revelations (PDF p. 127).

**Techniques for revelations** (PDF p. 127). ⚠️ This PDF loses the first technique and most of the second; the page picks up mid-sentence.
- *End of technique 2 (fragment):* when a revelation changes the desire, the change should adjust and intensify the original desire line and make it grow. A completely new desire would mean starting a new story.
- *Technique 3:* each revelation should be **explosive** and **stronger than the one before**. The information must matter, or it won't jolt the story, and every reveal should grow out of the previous one. This is what people mean when they say a plot "thickens."

**The gearbox image.** Truby compares the revelations to a car's gears: each reveal shifts the story up, until by the last one it is racing, and the audience, unsure how it got going so fast, is enjoying every moment. Revelations that fail to grow stronger make the plot stall or slide backward, which he calls deadly and says to avoid at all costs (PDF p. 127).

**Against three-act structure again.** Hollywood has grown more plot-conscious, which makes relying on three-act structure riskier still. That method asks for just two or three plot points, which is to say reveals; Truby calls this simply wrong and a recipe for a plot that can't compete professionally. A typical Hollywood hit carries seven to ten big reveals, and detective stories and thrillers have more. The sooner a writer drops three-act structure for advanced plotting techniques, the better (PDF p. 127).

**Examples** (PDF pp. 127, 141):
- *Casablanca:* *revelation:* later that same night, Ilsa comes back to the bar. *Decision:* Rick resolves to hurt her as badly as he can. *Changed desire:* before Ilsa came, all he wanted was to keep his business going, earn money and be left in peace; from now on he wants her to suffer as he has. *Changed motive:* she has earned it by what she did to him in Paris.
- *Tootsie:* *revelation:* Michael discovers his own power the moment "Dorothy" turns fierce at the audition and tells the director exactly what she thinks. *Decision:* as Dorothy, he will play a tough, no-nonsense woman. *Changed desire:* none; the job is still the goal. *Changed motive:* he now sees a way to keep it on his own terms.
- *The Godfather:* *revelation:* the hospital where his father lies has no guards and is nearly deserted, and Michael realizes killers are on their way. *Decision:* he moves the bed to another room and keeps watch outside. *Changed desire:* rather than staying apart from the family, he now wants to guard his father and rescue the family. *Changed motive:* his deep love for his family, plus a competitive drive that won't let him lose.

### Technique: added revelations (PDF p. 127)

Truby labels this a "twenty-two steps technique." The more revelations a story has, the richer and more complex its plot; whenever the hero or the audience learns something new, that counts as a revelation.

**Key point:** a revelation should be weighty enough to make the hero decide something and change course.

**Examples of added revelations:**
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 127): Michael notices he is attracted to Julie, decides to become her friend, now wants Julie, and his motive is that he is falling in love.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): inside the drive, Michael sees a beautiful girl on a Sicilian road, decides to meet her, wants her, and is falling in love.

## Placement

After the fake-ally opponent (step 8) and before the hero's plan (step 10) in Truby's typical order (PDF pp. 119, 126). He gives no page or percentage. Added revelations can appear anywhere; in the *Godfather* example one sits inside the drive (step 12) (PDF p. 141).
