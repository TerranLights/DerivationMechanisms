# Gate, gauntlet, visit to death (Truby, step 18)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 131-132 (step text and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example p. 142; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

Late in the story, hero and opponent clash so fiercely that the strain on the hero nears the breaking point. His options shrink, and often the space he moves through physically narrows too. In the end he has to squeeze through a **narrow gate**, or run a long **gauntlet** while being attacked from every side (PDF p. 131). In Truby's table it is a **plot** step (PDF p. 120).

**The visit to death.** This is also when the hero visits "death" (PDF pp. 131-132):
- **In myth,** the hero goes down into the underworld and sees his own future among the dead.
- **In modern stories,** the visit is psychological: the hero suddenly grasps that he is mortal, that life is limited and could end very soon.

**Why it leads to the battle.** One might expect this awareness to make him run, since the fight could kill him. Instead it pushes him to fight. His reasoning runs: if my life is going to mean anything, I have to stand up for what I believe, here and now. So the visit to death works as a **test** that frequently launches the battle (PDF pp. 131-132).

## How it works

**The most movable step.** Of all twenty-two, this one moves around the most and often turns up elsewhere in the plot (PDF p. 132):
- The brush with death can come at the **apparent defeat** (step 14).
- The gauntlet can be run **inside the battle itself**: the trench run in *Star Wars*, the tower in *Vertigo*.
- Or it can come **once the battle is over**: Terry Malloy's walk at the close of *On the Waterfront*.

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 132): the step covers the drive to the airfield, Rick with Ilsa, Laszlo and Renault, and Strasser racing to catch them.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 132): a gauntlet of ever-worse nightmares. Michael has to babysit Julie's screaming baby, Amy; take Julie's rebuff after an attempted kiss; dance with Les, now smitten with Dorothy; shake off John, the fellow actor pursuing her too; and fend off Sandy's accusations after he hands her Les's candy.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 142): there is no gate or gauntlet, because Michael fights so well that he fools even the audience. His visit to death is watching the car bomb meant for him kill his wife, which is also part of his apparent defeat.

**Variations.** Physical (narrowing space, a passage under fire) or psychological (facing mortality); before, during or after the battle; or merged with the apparent defeat (*The Godfather*). A gauntlet can also be social, a run of escalating confrontations (*Tootsie*).

## Placement

Near the end, typically after the third revelation (step 17) and just before the battle (step 19), which it often triggers (PDF pp. 131-132). But no step moves around more than this one (PDF p. 132).

## Equivalents in other frameworks

- **Snyder:** All Is Lost's "whiff of death" matches the visit to death. The Finale's Storming the Castle and High Tower Surprise resemble the gate and gauntlet. ⚠️ Partial; spread across two of Snyder's beats.
- **Bell:** the Mirror Moment's "I'm probably going to die" realization (with its physical, social, professional and psychological kinds of death, as the master records it) parallels the psychological visit to death, though Bell places it at the midpoint. ⚠️
- **Campbell:** the descent into a realm of death is a core monomyth image. Of the seventeen stages, the Belly of the Whale (early) and the trials of the return are the closest. ⚠️ No exact stage.
- **Harmon:** Find/Take, the low point of the circle's descent. ⚠️
- **Weiland:** no named beat. ⚠️
