# Opponent and/or mystery (Truby, step 7)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 125-126 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the plot technique "The iceberg opponent"); *The Godfather* example p. 141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. The full treatment of the opponent, including four-corner opposition, is in ch. 4.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

**The opponent** is whoever is trying to stop the hero from getting his goal (PDF p. 125). In Truby's table the opponent belongs to the **character** thread and the mystery to the **plot** thread (PDF p. 120). The opponent is the third of the **seven key steps**.

**The most important relationship.** The bond between hero and opponent matters more than any other in the story. Set it up correctly and the plot will unfold as it should; set it up wrongly and no amount of rewriting will save it (PDF p. 125).

**The necessary opponent.** The best opponent is the one best equipped to strike at the hero's central weakness, which forces the hero either to overcome that weakness and grow or to be destroyed. Truby refers back to ch. 4 for everything a great opponent needs (PDF p. 125).

**Why opponent and mystery go together** (PDF p. 125):
1. **A hidden opponent is harder to beat.** In ordinary stories the hero's only job is to beat the opponent. In good ones he must first find out who or what the opponent really is, and then defeat him, which doubles the difficulty and makes success far more impressive. Hamlet can't be sure the king killed his father, since the news came from a ghost; Othello has no idea Iago means to ruin him; Lear doesn't know which daughter truly loves him.
2. **A mystery can stand in for an absent opponent.** Detective stories and thrillers keep the opponent concealed until the final pages, so the audience needs something in place of an ongoing fight between hero and opponent. In such stories, bring in a mystery roughly where the main opponent would usually enter.

## How it works

**Questions to answer before bringing in the main opponent** (PDF p. 125):
- Who is trying to block the hero, and for what reason?
- What is the opponent after? Ideally the **same goal** the hero is chasing.
- What values does the opponent hold, and where do they clash with the hero's? Truby says most writers skip this question, a serious mistake: a story needs a clash of values, not only of people, or it can't build.

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF pp. 125-126): it is at heart a love story, so the first person opposing Rick is Ilsa, his lover, a mysterious woman who has kept from him that she was Laszlo's wife then and still is. His second opponent is that rival suitor, Laszlo, a great man admired across half the world. Both men hate the Nazis, but they stand for two kinds of greatness, Laszlo's political and public, Rick's personal. Strasser and the Nazis supply outside opposition and danger that raise the love story's stakes. Strasser isn't mysterious at all and doesn't need to be; in this world his power is total.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 126): because it is built on farce (along with romantic comedy), it doesn't use the hidden-opponent technique. Farce carries more opponents than any other form, and it works by sending many of them at the hero at an ever-quicker rate. Four main opponents go after Michael's weaknesses: Julie, who makes him face how he has mistreated women; Ron, the arrogant director, who never wanted Dorothy cast and stays hostile; Les, Julie's father, whose crush on Dorothy unwittingly shows Michael what his lying does to people; and John, a fellow actor who pursues Dorothy uninvited.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): Michael's first opponent is Sollozzo, but the real one is Barzini, the stronger power hidden behind Sollozzo, who wants to destroy the whole Corleone family. The two compete over the family's survival and over control of crime in New York.

### Plot technique: the iceberg opponent (PDF p. 126)

Whatever the genre, making the opponent mysterious matters a great deal. Picture the opponent as an iceberg: a little shows on the surface, while the bulk, and the most dangerous part, lies underneath. Truby gives four techniques for making the opposition as dangerous as possible:
1. **Build a hierarchy of opponents linked by alliances.** They are all connected and all working together to bring the hero down, with the main opponent at the top of the pyramid and the others ranked below. (He points to ch. 4's four-corner opposition and to the *Godfather* example closing ch. 8.)
2. **Hide that hierarchy** from hero and audience, along with each opponent's real agenda (true desire).
3. **Reveal it in pieces, faster and faster.** This puts more reveals near the end. How information reaches hero and audience decides whether a plot succeeds.
4. **Consider opening with an obvious opponent.** As the conflict heats up, let the hero find he is also under attack from a stronger opposition he couldn't see, or from the concealed part of the opponent he already knows.

## Placement

After the ally (step 6) in Truby's typical order. In detective fiction and thrillers, the mystery comes in at about the time the main opponent otherwise would (PDF p. 125). The iceberg technique spreads the opponent's reveals across the rest of the story, densest near the end (PDF p. 126).
