# Desire (Truby, step 5)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF p. 124 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the plot technique "Levels of desire"); *The Godfather* example p. 141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. The basic rules for desire come from ch. 3.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

The hero's **particular goal**. Truby calls it the spine that holds up the entire plot (PDF p. 124). In his table it is a **character** step (PDF p. 120), and it is the second of the **seven key steps**.

## How it works

**Rules carried over from ch. 3.** Good stories tend to have a single goal, a specific one, lasting through most of the story (PDF p. 124).

**The rule added here: start low.** One way a story builds is by making the desire matter more and more as it goes. If the desire starts too high, it has nowhere to climb and the plot feels flat and repetitive. So begin with a modest version and leave room to grow (PDF p. 124).

**Grow it; don't replace it.** As the desire builds, don't invent a wholly new one. Deepen the one you began with, and raise what rides on it (PDF p. 124). (Step 9 makes the same point about changed desire: an entirely new desire means a new story.)

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 124): Rick wants Ilsa, but because she is also his first opponent, that desire is muffled; bitter about Paris, what he wants first is to hurt her. While Rick's desire is stalled, the film shifts attention to Laszlo's goal of getting exit visas for himself and Ilsa. Because the writers made Rick's desire plain early, the audience puts up with Laszlo's scenes, trusting Rick's line will take over soon; the delay lets the desire simmer. Late in the story Rick forms a second desire that conflicts with the first: helping Ilsa and Laszlo get away. Introduced early, that would split the story into two spines; arriving late and kept secret until the final moment, it works both as a revelation and as part of his self-revelation.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 124): Michael first wants an acting job, which he gets early. The goal that actually carries the film is his wish to win Julie, one of the show's actresses.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): to avenge the attack on his father and so protect the family.

### Plot technique: levels of desire (PDF p. 124)

- How high the hero's desire sits is part of what makes a story succeed.
- A desire that stays low the whole way shrinks the hero and makes a complex plot close to impossible.
- The lowest desire line is bare **survival**: the hero, attacked, just wants to get away. That reduces him to an animal, and an escape plot just repeats one beat, running.
- Truby then announces a ranking of classic desire lines, climbing from the lowest. ⚠️ **The ranking is missing from this PDF;** the page goes straight from the announcement to step 6. It needs checking against a print copy.

## Placement

After the inciting event (step 4), which produces it. It then runs through most of the story as the plot's spine, and it changes form at each revelation and decision (steps 9, 15, 17) while staying the same basic goal (PDF p. 124).

## Equivalents in other frameworks

- **Truby, seven key steps:** step 2 of the seven (ch. 3; see `03 - The Seven Key Steps.md`).
- **Bell:** the Objective in LOCK (Lead, Objective, Confrontation, Knockout).
- **Weiland:** the Thing the Character Wants.
- **Harmon:** Need (the character wants something). ⚠️ Harmon's step can be a want or a lack, so it overlaps both Truby's desire and his problem.
- **Snyder:** the hero's goal, which he commits to at Break into Two. ⚠️ Snyder has no beat named for the desire itself.
- **Campbell:** the quest's object; the Ultimate Boon is the prize won. ⚠️ A loose match.
