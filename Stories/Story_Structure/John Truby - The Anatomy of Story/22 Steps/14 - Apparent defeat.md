# Apparent defeat (Truby, step 14)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 129-130 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the variant "Apparent victory"); *The Godfather* example p. 142; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. Subworlds of temporary freedom are discussed in ch. 6.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

The hero has been losing through the drive. Somewhere between the **two-thirds and three-quarters** marks, he suffers what looks like defeat: he believes the goal is gone and the opponent has won. It is the hero's **lowest point** (PDF p. 129). In Truby's table it is a **plot** step (PDF p. 120).

**Why it matters.** It punctuates the overall structure, because it is the moment the hero hits bottom, and it heightens the drama because he must now climb back from defeat to win. A sports match is more thrilling when the home team comes from behind, and a story is too when a beloved hero claws back from what looks like sure loss (PDF pp. 129-130).

## How it works

**Key point: make it devastating.** It is not a minor or passing setback but an explosive, crushing moment. The audience must truly believe the hero is finished (PDF p. 130).

**Key point: only one.** Setbacks can and should be frequent, but just one moment should plainly look like the end. Otherwise the story loses its shape and dramatic force. Truby's image: a car racing downhill can jolt over a few rough bumps, or it can hit a brick wall head-on (PDF p. 130).

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 130): Rick's apparent defeat comes fairly early in the drive, the night Ilsa visits after closing. Drunk, he relives their Paris romance and its end, when she never appeared at the station. As she attempts an explanation, he lashes out bitterly and drives her away.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 130): his agent says the soap-opera contract cannot be broken; he is stuck living this nightmare as a woman.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 142): a one-two punch. news of Sonny's murder reaches Michael, and shortly afterward he watches his wife die in an explosion intended for him.

**Variation: two blows.** *The Godfather* fits the one-defeat rule by joining two blows into a single collapse.

### Variant: apparent victory (PDF p. 130)

- When a story's hero finishes **more enslaved, or dead**, this step becomes an **apparent victory**.
- The hero hits his peak of success or power, and from there everything runs downhill.
- It is also often where the hero steps into a **subworld of temporary freedom** (ch. 6).
- *Goodfellas:* the gang pulls off the Lufthansa robbery, thinking it is the biggest haul they'll ever make. Instead that success starts the process that will destroy them all.

## Placement

Between roughly the two-thirds and three-quarters marks (PDF p. 129). It ends the drive, which runs from the plan to this point (PDF p. 128), and is followed immediately by the second revelation (step 15), without which the defeat would be real and the story over (PDF p. 130). Truby's own *Casablanca* reading puts it "fairly early" in the drive, so the position flexes (PDF p. 130).
