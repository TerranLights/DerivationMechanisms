# Self-revelation, need, and desire (Truby, step 1)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 120-121 (step text and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example p. 140; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

Step 1 is not an event that happens in the story. It is the **frame**: the full span of the hero's change from the first page to the last. Truby builds it by joining three other steps, step 20 (self-revelation), step 3 (weakness and need) and step 5 (desire), and says this frame gives the writer the structural "journey" the hero will travel (PDF p. 120).

It repeats the method of ch. 4 (Character). There, the writer begins with where the hero ends up, his self-revelation, and only then goes back to the opening to find his weakness, need and desire. Truby applies the same order to plot: fix the end point first, and every later step will lead straight to the place you want to arrive (PDF p. 120).

In his table of the four story threads, step 1 belongs to the **character** thread (PDF p. 120).

## How it works

**Three framing questions.** Truby asks the writer to answer these as specifically as possible (PDF p. 121):
- What will the hero have learned by the end?
- What does he already know or believe at the start? No hero begins as a blank slate.
- Where is he mistaken at the start? A hero can't learn something at the end unless he was mistaken about something at the beginning.

**The parts of the frame.** His worked examples break the frame into five labeled parts (PDF p. 121):
- **Self-revelation:** what the hero finally sees.
- **Psychological need:** the inner fault he must overcome for his own sake.
- **Moral need:** the way he must stop hurting others.
- **Desire:** the outward goal he pursues.
- **Initial error:** the false belief he holds at the start, which the self-revelation will correct.

**Examples** (PDF p. 121):
- *Casablanca:* Rick comes to see that being hurt in love doesn't entitle him to stay out of the fight for freedom. His psychological need is to get past his bitterness toward Ilsa, find a reason to live and recover his ideals; morally, he needs to quit putting himself first at others' expense; his desire is to win Ilsa back; his initial error is to treat himself as a dead man passing time, with no stake in world affairs.
- *Tootsie:* Michael comes to see that he has regarded women only as sexual objects, and that this made him a lesser man. His psychological need is to drop his condescension toward women and learn to love honestly, giving and receiving; morally, he needs to quit lying to women and using them; his desire is Julie, who acts on the same soap; his initial error is believing he behaves decently toward women and that lying to them is fine.
- *The Godfather:* the hero, Michael, gets no self-revelation. He becomes a ruthless killer, and only his wife, Kay, sees how far he has fallen. His need is to avoid becoming such a killer; his desire is to avenge the shooting of his father; his initial error is thinking he stands apart from his family, untouched by its crimes (PDF p. 140).

**Variation.** The *Godfather* example shows that the frame still applies when the self-revelation is absent: the writer fixes the end point as "no self-revelation" and lets another character see what the hero cannot (PDF pp. 140, 142).

## Placement

Step 1 has no position on the story's timeline. It is the first thing the writer works out, before anything else, and Truby states that only from step 2 onward does he follow the order in which steps usually appear in a story (PDF p. 121). Exercise 7 repeats the point: describe the steps starting with step 1, so the rest fall into place (PDF p. 140).

## Equivalents in other frameworks

- **Truby, seven key steps (ch. 3):** the frame spans the first two of the seven (weakness and need, desire) and the sixth (self-revelation). See `03 - The Seven Key Steps.md`.
- **Weiland:** her character-arc foundation (the Lie He Believes, the Thing He Wants, the Thing He Needs, and the Truth he arrives at) is the closest match. Initial error ≈ the Lie; self-revelation ≈ embracing the Truth; desire ≈ Want; need ≈ Need. ⚠️ Close in function, not identical in definition.
- **Snyder:** no single beat. The Opening Image and Final Image pair frames the before and after of the hero's change, and Theme Stated hints at the need. ⚠️
- **Harmon:** the circle as a whole, from You and Need to Change. ⚠️
- **Bell:** LOCK's Lead and Objective cover the hero and his desire; Bell's Transformation is the end point. ⚠️
- **Campbell:** no stage; the whole departure-and-return cycle frames the change. ⚠️
