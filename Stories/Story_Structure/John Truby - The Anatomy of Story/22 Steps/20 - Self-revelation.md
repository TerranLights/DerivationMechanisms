# Self-revelation (Truby, step 20)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 132-133 (step text, the plot technique "Double reversal", and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example p. 142; storyteller techniques 1 and 8, pp. 137 and 139; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. Ch. 10 covers how to write it in dialogue.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

Having gone through the **crucible of the battle**, the hero usually changes. Now, at last, he discovers his real identity. He rips away the front he has been living behind and sees his true self, and the sight shocks him (PDF p. 132). In Truby's table it is a **character** step (PDF p. 120), and it is the sixth of the **seven key steps**.

**Two outcomes.** The truth about himself can **destroy** the hero (*Vertigo*, *Oedipus the King*, *The Conversation*), or it can make him **stronger** (PDF p. 132).

**Psychological and moral.** When the revelation has a moral side too, and not only a psychological one, the hero further discovers how he ought to treat others (PDF p. 132).

**Three qualities of a great self-revelation** (PDF p. 132):
- **Sudden,** for the strongest dramatic effect.
- **Shattering** for the hero, for good or ill.
- **New:** something about himself the hero didn't know until that moment.

## How it works

**Everything leads here.** Much of a story's quality rests on how good its self-revelation is; the whole story has been heading toward it, and it has to work (PDF p. 132).

**Two pitfalls** (PDF p. 132):
1. What the hero learns must be **genuinely meaningful**, not a fine-sounding slogan or a cliché about life.
2. The hero must **not announce** his lesson outright; Truby calls that a sign of bad writing. Ch. 10 explains how to convey the self-revelation in dialogue without preaching.

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 133): *psychological:* Rick gets back his ideals and a sure sense of himself. *Moral:* he sees he must give himself up for the couple's sake and take up the struggle for freedom again. *Revelation plus double reversal:* Renault declares that he too has turned patriot and will join Rick on his new road.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 133): *psychological:* Michael sees he has never truly loved, because he never looked past a woman's body. *Moral:* he sees how his arrogance and contempt for women have harmed both himself and the women in his life; he tells Julie that being a woman taught him more about manhood than being a man ever did.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 142): *psychological:* none; Michael still thinks his superiority and self-righteousness are justified. *Moral:* none; he has turned into a merciless murderer. Truby credits the writers with an advanced device: they hand the moral self-revelation to Kay, who sees what her husband has become as the door shuts in her face.

**Variations.**
- **Positive or destructive** (*Oedipus*, *Vertigo*, *The Conversation* destroy their heroes).
- **Absent in the hero, given to another character** (*The Godfather*).
- **Shared with the opponent:** see the double reversal below.
- **With a storyteller,** the telling as a whole works structurally as a single large self-revelation, split in half: the teller looks back at the start and gains the insight by the end (storyteller techniques 1 and 8; PDF pp. 137, 139). In *Goodfellas* the first-person teller ironically never gets the self-revelation he plainly should (PDF p. 139).

### Plot technique: double reversal (PDF pp. 132-133)

Let the **opponent** have a self-revelation alongside the hero's. Hero and opponent learn from one another, so the audience leaves with a pair of insights about how to live rather than a single one.

**How Truby builds it** (five steps in the book, summarized):
- The groundwork is laid early: the main opponent, like the hero, needs his own weakness and need, and must be a real person who can learn and change.
- The payoff comes in the battle or right after it, when the opponent also sees the truth about himself.
- The two revelations are linked, so that each character's lesson comes from the other.
- The author's own moral vision is the best of what the two of them learn.

In *Casablanca*, Renault's turn at the end is labeled the double reversal (PDF p. 133).

## Placement

After the battle, as its result (PDF p. 132); in the double reversal, the opponent's revelation comes in the battle or right after it (PDF p. 133). It is the end point fixed in advance by the step 1 frame (PDF p. 120), and it is followed by the moral decision (step 21), which proves it (PDF p. 133).

## Equivalents in other frameworks

- **Truby, seven key steps:** step 6 of the seven (ch. 3; see `03 - The Seven Key Steps.md`).
- **Weiland:** the protagonist fully embracing the Truth, around the Third Plot Point and Climactic Moment. ⚠️ Weiland spreads the realization over several beats.
- **Bell:** the Transformation; also the Mirror Moment, an earlier, midpoint self-confrontation. ⚠️
- **Snyder:** Break into Three (the hero realizes who he must become) and the Finale's Dig Deep Down. ⚠️
- **Campbell:** Apotheosis. ⚠️
- **Harmon:** Change (and Find, where the character finds what he needed). ⚠️
