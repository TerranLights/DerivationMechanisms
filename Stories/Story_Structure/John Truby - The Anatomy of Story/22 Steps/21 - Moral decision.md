# Moral decision (Truby, step 21)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF p. 133 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the plot technique "Thematic revelation"); *The Godfather* example p. 142; storyteller technique 10, p. 139; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. Thematic revelation is introduced in ch. 5.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

Once the self-revelation has shown the hero the right way to act, he has to **choose**. At this moment he chooses between **two paths**, each of them embodying certain values and a manner of living that touches other people (PDF p. 133). In Truby's table it is a **moral argument** step (PDF p. 120).

**Proof of the change.** This choice is the evidence of what the self-revelation taught him. By acting on it, he shows the audience who he has become (PDF p. 133).

## How it works

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 133): Rick gives the transit letters to Laszlo, sends Ilsa off with her husband, and assures Laszlo of her love for him. Then Rick sets off to risk his life fighting for freedom.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 133): Michael sacrifices the role and asks Julie and Les to forgive his lies.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 142): Michael's great moral decision comes **just before** the battle: having become godfather to his brother-in-law's baby, he decides to have all his rivals killed, and the brother-in-law too. Here the decision is a choice for evil, matching his missing self-revelation and his fall (step 22).

**The storyteller link.** When a story uses a storyteller, the telling itself should produce a final dramatic event, most often the moral decision, made on the strength of the self-revelation. Examples: Nick leaving New York in *The Great Gatsby*, George choosing life (*It's a Wonderful Life*), the boxer refusing to throw the fight in *Body and Soul*, Red refusing to give up in *The Shawshank Redemption* (storyteller technique 10; PDF p. 139).

### Plot technique: thematic revelation (PDF p. 133)

- **What it is (from ch. 5):** a revelation that comes not to the hero but to the **audience**. They glimpse how human beings at large ought to live and behave, which lets the story reach beyond its particular characters and touch the audience's own lives.
- **Why writers avoid it:** many shy away for fear of sounding preachy at the very end. Done well, Truby says, the effect can be overwhelming.
- **Key point:** the craft lies in drawing the abstract and the universal out of the concrete, specific reality of your characters. Look for one specific gesture or act able to carry symbolic weight for the audience.
- **Example, *Places in the Heart* (Robert Benton, 1984):** a 1930s Midwestern widow (Sally Field) whose husband, the sheriff, dies by accident at the hands of a drunk Black teenager; the Klan lynches the youth and later drives off a Black man who has been helping her farm, and a subplot follows an adulterous husband. The last scene is a church service. While the sermon turns to love, the wife of the unfaithful husband reaches for his hand, her first gesture of the kind since his affair almost ended the marriage. The communion plate travels along the pews, each person wishing the next God's peace, and gradually the audience realizes that everyone in the story is taking part: the banker who opposed the heroine, the man who was driven away, the heroine, her dead husband beside her, and beside him the youth who killed him and died for it. A realistic portrait turns into forgiveness extended to everyone, and the audience is drawn into it. Truby urges writers not to avoid the technique out of fear of seeming pretentious.

## Placement

In Truby's sequence: after the self-revelation (step 20), which it proves, and before the new equilibrium (step 22) (PDF p. 133). His own *Godfather* breakdown places it **just before the battle** (PDF p. 142), which shows the position can move. The thematic revelation sits at the story's final moment with the audience (PDF p. 133).

## Equivalents in other frameworks

- **Weiland:** the Climactic Moment, where the protagonist's choice decides the conflict. ⚠️
- **Snyder:** within the Finale, Executing the New Plan, after the hero has dug deep. ⚠️ Snyder has no beat named for a moral choice.
- **Bell:** the choices inside the Final Battle; Bell's Q-Factor is a different idea (renewed capacity to act). ⚠️
- **Campbell:** Refusal of the Return and Master of Two Worlds involve choosing between worlds. ⚠️ Weak match.
- **Harmon:** Return/Change. ⚠️
- **The thematic revelation** loosely parallels Snyder's Final Image as a closing statement of meaning. ⚠️
