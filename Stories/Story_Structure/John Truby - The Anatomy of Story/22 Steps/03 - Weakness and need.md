# Weakness and need (Truby, step 3)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 122-123 (step text, the Casablanca and Tootsie examples, and the plot technique "Openings"); *The Godfather* example p. 141; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120. The concepts are introduced earlier, in ch. 3 and ch. 4.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

Three linked elements of the hero's starting condition. In Truby's table this is a **character** step (PDF p. 120), and it is the first of his **seven key steps** (ch. 3).

**Weakness.** One or more character flaws serious enough to be wrecking the hero's life (PDF pp. 122-123). They come in two forms, which can coexist:
- **Psychological:** every weakness is psychological, because something inside the person is damaged.
- **Moral:** a weakness is moral as well when it causes someone else to be hurt. A morally weak hero always has a direct bad effect on another person.
- **The test (key point):** writers often believe a weakness is moral when it is merely psychological. Ask whether, at the start, the hero is plainly harming someone besides himself (PDF p. 123).

**Need.** What the hero has to fulfill for his life to improve. It nearly always means overcoming his weaknesses by the story's end (PDF p. 123). In the examples Truby splits it, like the weakness, into a psychological need and a moral need.

**Problem.** The trouble or crisis the hero faces right at the start. He knows about it but sees no way out. It usually grows out of his weakness and is built to reveal that weakness fast. It belongs at the opening, but it matters far less than weakness and need (PDF p. 123).

## How it works

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 123): Rick seems to want and need nothing; the need is there but concealed. He looks stronger and more self-sufficient than anyone, and although his cynicism reveals a deeply troubled man, he rules his little world, running the club as a kindly dictator and keeps women under his control. He is full of contradictions: bitter, cynical and frequently immoral today, though not long ago he fought for good causes. Truby finds it unusual that a hero so firmly in control starts as an observer who only reacts: a man of power and history who has retreated to a forgotten corner of the world and into himself, "a lion" in a cage he built. *Weaknesses:* cynical, disillusioned, reactive, selfish. *Needs:* as in step 1. *Problem:* he is stuck in Casablanca and stuck in his own bitterness.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 123): *weaknesses:* selfish, arrogant, a liar. *Needs:* as in step 1. *Problem:* he is desperate for acting work.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 141): *weaknesses:* youth, inexperience, being untested, overconfidence. *Psychological need:* to get past feeling superior and self-righteous. *Moral need:* to protect his family without becoming as ruthless as the other bosses. *Problem:* rival gangsters shoot his father.

### Plot technique: openings (PDF p. 123)

Together, the ghost, the world, the weakness, the need and the problem make up the story's opening. Truby names three structural kinds of opening that set these up:
- **Community start.** The hero's home is a paradise where land, people and technology fit together in harmony. He has no ghost and is happy, with a minor problem at most, but he is open to an attack that will soon come from outside or inside. *The Deer Hunter* opens this way, as does *Meet Me in St. Louis*.
- **Running start.** The classic opening, built to hook the audience within the first pages. It combines several structural elements: a strong ghost, a world that enslaves, several serious weaknesses, needs of both kinds (psychological, moral), and at least one problem. This is how most good stories begin.
- **Slow start.** Not a running start with steps left out, but a story whose hero has no purpose. Such people exist, but their stories drag: since the self-revelation is discovering what he truly wants, the first three-quarters lacks a goal and so lacks forward drive. Few stories survive this structural flaw; Truby names *Rebel Without a Cause* and *On the Waterfront* as two that do.

## Placement

At the opening, together with step 2. The problem should appear at the very beginning (PDF p. 123). Weakness and need also form part of the step 1 frame, and the need is what the self-revelation (step 20) resolves.
