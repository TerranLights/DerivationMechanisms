# Audience revelation (Truby, step 16)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*, ch. 8 "Plot", PDF pp. 130-131 (step text and the Casablanca and Tootsie examples); *The Godfather* example p. 142; the *Alien* and Borges notes in the revelations-sequence section, pp. 135-136; step list p. 119; thread table p. 120.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is

The point where the **audience alone, with the hero still in the dark,** gains some important new knowledge. Often what they learn is who the **fake-ally opponent** really is: the character they took for the hero's friend turns out to be an enemy (PDF p. 131). In Truby's table it is a **plot** step (PDF p. 120).

## How it works

**Why it is valuable, whatever is revealed** (PDF p. 131):
- It gives an exciting jolt to what is often a sluggish stretch of the plot.
- It shows the audience how strong the opposition really is.
- It lets the audience watch certain hidden plot elements play out dramatically and on screen.

**A shift between hero and audience.** Until now, in most stories (farce is the big exception), the audience has discovered each thing exactly when the hero did. That creates a one-to-one bond, an identification, between them. Here, for the first time, the audience is told something *ahead of* the hero. This opens a distance and puts the audience above the hero. Truby gives several reasons this helps; chief among them, the audience can stand back and take in the whole of the hero's change, which peaks at the self-revelation (PDF p. 131).

**Examples:**
- *Casablanca* (PDF p. 131): at gunpoint, Rick makes Renault phone the airport, supposedly the control tower, but we see that the call actually goes to Strasser.
- *Tootsie* (PDF p. 131): the step is absent, chiefly because Michael is the one running a con on everyone else. Since he is the deceiver, he is in control, and the audience learns things alongside him.
- *The Godfather* (PDF p. 142): the audience watches Luca Brasi, the Don's most dangerous ally, being killed at his meeting with Tattaglia and Sollozzo.

**Variations and absences:**
- *Alien* (PDF p. 135): there is effectively no audience revelation. The creature stays an unknown, terrifying force, so the audience learns things about when Ripley and the crew do; denied any superiority over the characters, they stay more afraid.
- Borges's traitor-and-hero story (PDF p. 136): the story ends on an audience revelation, when the narrator hides his final discovery and publishes a book that glorifies his ancestor instead.
- *Tootsie* shows the step can be dropped when the hero is the deceiver.

## Placement

After the second revelation and decision (step 15) and before the third (step 17), in Truby's typical order (PDF pp. 119, 131). It often lands in a part of the plot that drags (PDF p. 131). The hero usually catches up at step 17, often by discovering the fake ally's identity (PDF p. 131).
