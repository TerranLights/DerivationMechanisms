## Why structure is the hidden chain
Truby opens with three very different successes: *The Godfather* (long and complex), *Tootsie* (a tightly choreographed farce of unrequited love and mistaken identity) and *Chinatown* (a twisting chain of surprises and reveals). What they share, deep under the surface of each, is the same unbreakable organic sequence: seven key steps of structure (Truby, PDF p. 20).

## Structure means development over time
Talking about a story's structure means talking about how it develops through time. Living things seem to grow in one smooth flow, but close up you can see stages in that growth, and a story is the same (Truby, PDF p. 20).

## The seven steps
At minimum, a story passes through seven steps from start to finish (Truby, PDF p. 20):
1. Weakness and need (the hero's inner lack)
2. Desire (his outer goal)
3. Opponent (his rival for that goal)
4. Plan (his strategy)
5. Battle (the deciding clash)
6. Self-revelation (what he learns about himself)
7. New equilibrium (the changed normal)

## Why these seven are organic, not imposed
- They are **not** pressed onto the story from outside, the way a mechanical template such as the three-act model is. They are **already inside** the story (Truby, PDF pp. 20-21).
- They are the story's nucleus or DNA, and the basis of a storyteller's success, because they are grounded in **what people do**: they are the stages any person has to go through to solve a problem in life (Truby, PDF p. 21).
- Because they are organic, already implied by the one-line premise, they need to be **connected properly** for the story to hit the audience hardest (Truby, PDF p. 21).

## Step 1: Weakness and need

### What it is
When the story opens, the hero already carries one or more **great weaknesses** that restrain him. Some lack inside him runs so deep that it is wrecking his life. (Truby notes he calls the hero "he" only for ease of writing.) The **need** is the inner fulfillment he must reach to live a better life; typically that means getting past his weaknesses and changing or maturing somehow (Truby, PDF p. 21).

Examples (Truby, PDF p. 21):
- *Tootsie:* Michael is arrogant, selfish and dishonest; he needs to drop his arrogance toward women and stop lying to and using them.
- *Silence of the Lambs* (step 1): Clarice is green, haunted by her early years, and female in a male-run profession; she needs to lay her past to rest and earn professional respect in that world.

Truby says he can't overstate the need's importance: the whole story flows from it as from a **wellspring**, and every later step depends on it (Truby, PDF p. 21).

### Rule: the hero doesn't know his need at the start
**Key point:** the hero should be unaware of his need when the story begins. If he already knows it, the story is over. He should discover it only late, at the self-revelation, and only after much pain (in drama) or struggle (in comedy) (Truby, PDF p. 21).

### Rule: give the hero a moral need as well as a psychological one
**Key point:** give the hero a need of each kind (Truby, PDF p. 21).
- **Psychological need:** overcoming a serious flaw that hurts no one but the hero. Average stories stop here.
- **Moral need:** overcoming a moral flaw and learning to treat other people properly. Better stories add this. At the start, a hero who has a moral need is always **harming other people** somehow; that harm is his moral weakness.

*The Verdict:* Frank must, psychologically, conquer his alcoholism and recover his self-respect; morally, he must quit exploiting people for cash and learn to act justly. The audience sees the moral need when he talks his way into a stranger's funeral to drum up business, indifferent to upsetting the grieving family (Truby, PDF pp. 21-22).

Two reasons to add a moral need (Truby, PDF p. 21):
1. **Scope.** The hero's actions now affect others, not just him, which moves the audience more powerfully.
2. **It keeps the hero from being perfect or a victim,** both of which Truby calls fatal. A perfect hero isn't believable. And when the hero has no moral flaws but the opponent does, the opponent usually dominates him, and the story turns **reactive and predictable**.

### The problem (an aspect of step 1, not a step of its own)
Also there from page one, though much less central than step 1, is the **problem**. Good stories open with a jolt, the hero already in trouble. The problem is that opening **crisis**. He knows full well he's in it but doesn't know how to get out (Truby, PDF p. 21).

The problem doesn't count as a step, but it is a facet of weakness and need, and a useful one (Truby, PDF p. 21):
- A crisis defines a character very fast.
- It should be the **outward expression of the hero's weakness,** highlighting that weakness for the audience.
- It gives the story a quick start.

**Key point:** make the problem simple and concrete (Truby, PDF p. 21).

Examples (Truby, PDF pp. 21-22):
- *Sunset Boulevard:* Joe's weakness is his taste for money and comfort, for which he'll trade his artistic and moral integrity. His problem: he's broke, the repo men come for his car, and he bolts.
- *Tootsie:* Michael's weakness is arrogance, selfishness and lying. His problem: he is a fine actor but so overbearing that nobody will hire him, and he is desperate.

### Technique: building the moral need
A common error is thinking you've supplied a moral need when what you've supplied is purely psychological. The test: a moral need requires the hero to be **harming someone else** when the story opens (Truby, PDF p. 22).

Two ways to find the right moral need (Truby, PDF p. 22):
- **Grow it from the psychological need.** In good stories the moral need typically springs from the psychological one: the hero's inner weakness spills over into mistreating others. Method: start with the psychological weakness, work out what immoral act would naturally follow from it, then name the deep moral weakness and need behind that act.
- **Push a strength until it becomes a weakness.** Either take a virtue and make the hero so fervent about it that it starts to oppress people, or take a value he believes in and find its negative form.

## Step 2: Desire

### What it is
Once weakness and need are set, the hero needs a **desire**: his particular goal within the story (Truby, PDF p. 22).

The audience doesn't get interested until desire arrives. Truby pictures desire as the track the audience rides: the audience climbs aboard the train beside the hero and they pursue the goal as one. Desire powers the story, the line everything else hangs from (Truby, PDF p. 22).

### Desire and need are linked
In most stories, reaching the goal also fulfills the need. Truby's nature example: a hungry lion (a physical need) spots a young antelope in a passing herd and wants it (the desire); catch it, and the hunger is gone, end of story (Truby, PDF p. 22).

### Desire and need are different steps
Truby ranks mixing them up, or merging them into one step, among the worst errors a writer can commit. They are two distinct steps that together form the story's beginning, and each has its own function (Truby, PDF p. 22):

| | Need | Desire |
|---|---|---|
| Where it lies | inside the hero: overcoming a weakness | outside the hero: a goal |
| What it does to the hero | at the start, his weakness leaves him paralyzed in some way | once he has it, he moves in a definite direction and acts to reach it |
| What it does for the audience | shows how he must change to live better; it unlocks the entire story | gives them something to want alongside him, through every twist and digression |
| Visibility | hidden, below the surface | on the surface; what viewers *believe* the story concerns |

**Truby's chart** (PDF p. 22, an image): a horizontal line marks the story's surface. Desire runs as an arrow along the top of that line. Need runs as a second arrow underneath it, and the need arrow ends at the self-revelation. In other words, the visible goal travels on the surface while the hidden need travels below it toward the moment the hero finally recognizes it.

Examples (Truby, PDF pp. 22-23):
- *Saving Private Ryan:* Captain Miller needs to do his duty despite his fear (psychological and moral); he wants to find Ryan and bring him home alive.
- *The Full Monty:* each man needs his self-respect back (psychological); they want to make big money stripping in front of a room full of women.
- *The Verdict:* Frank needs self-respect (psychological) and justice toward others (moral); like every courtroom hero, he wants the verdict.
- *Chinatown:* Jake needs to shed his cocky arrogance and learn trust (psychological), and to quit exploiting people for profit and see a murderer punished because that is right (moral); like every detective, he wants to solve the mystery, here who killed Hollis and why.

### Rule: the desire belongs to this story, not to his life
**Key point:** the real desire is the hero's goal inside this particular story, not his general wish for his life. Miller in *Saving Private Ryan* would like to quit fighting and go home to his family, but that doesn't drive this story. What drives it is the specific goal, demanding a series of concrete actions, of bringing Ryan back (Truby, PDF p. 23).

### Seven-steps technique: don't start with desire
Writers who know the audience perks up only when desire kicks in are sometimes tempted to skip weakness and need and open on desire. Truby calls this a pact with the devil. It buys a quick start but kills the payoff at the end. Weakness and need are the story's foundation: they make the hero's final change possible, give the story personal meaning, and earn the audience's concern. His verdict: never skip the first step (Truby, PDF p. 23).

## Step 3: Opponent

### What it is
Writers often picture the **opponent** (the antagonist) as whoever looks, sounds or acts evil, and Truby warns that this view will keep a writer from ever producing a good story. See the opponent **structurally**, by his function: a true opponent doesn't merely block the hero's desire; he **wants that very goal too**, and the two are rivals for it (Truby, PDF p. 23).

### Why the same goal matters
Defining the opponent this way ties step 3 organically to step 2. Only rivals for one goal are forced into direct conflict, again and again, across the story. Give them different goals and both can succeed without ever colliding, which leaves no story at all (Truby, PDF p. 23).

### Look for the hidden shared goal
In many good stories hero and opponent seem at first to want different things. Look again for what they are really fighting over. In a detective story the hero appears to be after an arrest and the killer after escape, yet the real contest is over **which account of reality the world will believe** (Truby, PDF p. 23).

The method: dig down to the **deepest level of conflict** between them by asking what, above everything else, their fight is about, and center the story there (Truby, PDF p. 23).

**Key point:** the right opponent is found by starting from the hero's specific goal; whoever is trying to keep it from him qualifies (Truby, PDF p. 23).

### Warning: the hero is not his own opponent
Writers often say their hero is battling himself. Truby calls this a mistake that causes many structural problems. A hero "fighting himself" is really a hero with an inner **weakness** (step 1), not an opponent (Truby, PDF p. 23).

Examples (Truby, PDF p. 23):
- *The Godfather:* Sollozzo is Michael's first opponent, but the main one is Barzini, the stronger hidden power behind Sollozzo, who wants to destroy the Corleones. The stakes are whether the Corleones survive and who controls the New York underworld.
- *Star Wars* (opponent): Luke against Darth Vader, competing for control of the universe: the Empire's tyranny against the good side of the Jedi and the Republic.
- *Chinatown:* as in any good detective story, the opponent is tricky and stays hidden until the end: Noah Cross. His water scheme to control the city's future isn't what he competes with Jake over; they compete over which account of the truth wins. Cross wants the official story to be an accidental drowning and a girl who is merely his granddaughter; Jake wants the world to learn that Cross killed Hollis and fathered the girl by raping Evelyn, his own daughter.

## Step 4: Plan

### What it is
Neither in life nor in stories can anyone act without some plan. The **plan** is the hero's set of guidelines or strategies for defeating the opponent and winning the goal (Truby, PDF p. 24).

### How it works
- It grows organically out of both desire and opponent, and should always be pointed squarely at beating the one and gaining the other (Truby, PDF p. 24).
- It can be vague. In some genres, such as the caper and the war story, it is so complex that characters may write it out so the audience can see it (Truby, PDF p. 24).

Examples (Truby, PDF p. 24):
- *Chinatown:* Jake will interview people who knew Hollis and follow the physical evidence tied to his death.
- *Hamlet:* Hamlet will stage a play that re-enacts his father's murder by the new king, and use the king's reaction to prove his guilt.
- *The Godfather* (plan): Michael first sets out to murder Sollozzo and the police captain guarding him; his second, near the end, is to wipe out the heads of the rival families in one blow.

---

## Step 5: Battle

### What it is
Through the middle of the story, hero and opponent trade blows, punch and counterpunch, each trying to win the goal, and the fight intensifies. The **battle** is their **last** clash, which settles who gets the goal. It can be fought with violence or with words (Truby, PDF p. 24).

Examples (Truby, PDF p. 24):
- *The Odyssey:* Odysseus kills the suitors who tormented his wife and wrecked his household.
- *Chinatown:* a policeman kills Evelyn, Noah escapes with her daughter, and Jake walks away in despair.
- *The Verdict:* Frank beats the opposing lawyer through brilliant courtroom work and persuasion.

---

## Step 6: Self-revelation

### What it is
The battle is an intense, painful ordeal, and this **crucible** forces the hero into a major discovery about his true self. How good the story is depends largely on how good this discovery is. Like need, it has **two varieties: psychological and moral** (Truby, PDF p. 24).

### Psychological self-revelation
The hero rips away the **facade** he has hidden behind and, for once, sees himself truthfully. This is neither passive nor easy: it is the most active, hardest and bravest thing he does in the whole story (Truby, PDF p. 24).

**Warning:** don't have the hero announce what he learned. That is obvious and preachy and alienates the audience. Let his actions in the run-up to the revelation imply the insight (Truby, PDF p. 24).

Examples (Truby, PDF p. 24):
- *Big:* Josh sees he must give up his girlfriend and his toy-company life and return to childhood so that he can later have a good, loving grown-up life.
- *Casablanca:* Rick drops his cynicism, recovers his idealism and gives up Ilsa to become a freedom fighter.
- *Chinatown:* a **negative** revelation. After Evelyn's death Jake mutters "As little as possible," apparently seeing his life as useless and even destructive, since he has once again hurt someone he loves.
- *Dances with Wolves:* through his new wife and his adopted Lakota kin, Dunbar gains a purpose and a fresh model of manhood, yet that culture is about to vanish, so the revelation cuts **both ways**.

### Moral self-revelation
A hero given a moral need should get a moral revelation as well. He doesn't just see himself anew; he gains insight into the **right way to treat others**. In effect he realizes he was wrong, that he has hurt people, and that he must change, and he then proves the change with a **new moral action** (Truby, PDF p. 24).

Examples (Truby, PDF p. 24):
- *Tootsie:* Michael grasps what being a man really means, saying in effect that he was a better man as a woman than he had ever been as a man (Truby quotes the line), and apologizes to the woman he hurt. He does say what he learned outright, but so wittily that it avoids preaching.
- *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn:* Huck realizes he was wrong to think of Jim as something less than a person, and swears he'd sooner go to hell than betray Jim's whereabouts to his owner.

### Self-revelation is the partner of need
Structurally, self-revelation connects most closely to need; together the two express the hero's change (developed in chapter 4) (Truby, PDF p. 25):
- **Need** is where the change starts: the sign of his immaturity at the outset, what is missing, what holds him back.
- **Self-revelation** is where it ends: the moment he grows as a person (unless what he learns is unbearable enough to destroy him); it is his lesson and his gain, what lets him live better from then on.

## Step 7: New equilibrium

### What it is
At the **new equilibrium** things return to normal and all desire has gone, with one big exception: the ordeal has carried the hero **up or down** to a new level. A fundamental, permanent change has happened (Truby, PDF p. 25).
- A **positive** revelation, in which he grasps his true self and how to live well, makes him **rise**.
- A **negative** one (discovering that he has done something terrible that reveals a rotten flaw in him), or if he is **incapable** of self-revelation, he **falls** or is destroyed.

Rises (Truby, PDF p. 25):
- *Die Hard:* John has defeated the gang, rescued his wife and rekindled their marriage.
- *Pretty Woman:* Vivian is out of prostitution and with the man she loves (conveniently, a billionaire).
- *Silence of the Lambs* (step 7): Clarice has caught Buffalo Bill, become an excellent agent and apparently beaten her nightmares.

Falls (Truby, PDF p. 25):
- *Oedipus the King:* Oedipus blinds himself on discovering he murdered his father and married his mother.
- *The Conversation* (Coppola): the surveillance-expert hero learns he helped bring about a murder and ends up shattered, ripping his apartment apart in search of a bug.
- *Vertigo:* the hero forces the woman he loves up a tower to make her confess to a murder, then watches in horror as she, consumed by guilt, falls to her death.

## Exercises in this chapter

### Writing Exercise 2: using the seven steps (Truby, PDF pp. 25-26)
The exercise has the writer work out the seven steps from the writer's own material, in this order:
- **Story events.** List possible events, each in a single sentence: at least five, better ten to fifteen. Because the steps are embedded in the idea rather than imposed on it, this is the first move. The events that pop into mind on first thinking of a story, usually actions by hero or opponent, are valuable even if none survives to the final draft; detail doesn't matter yet, only the gist. More events make the steps easier to see.
- **Order of events.** Arrange the events roughly from start to end, knowing the order will change; the aim is a glimpse of how the story might develop.
- **Seven steps.** Find the seven steps among the events.
- **Key point: start at the end.** Settle the self-revelation first, then return to the opening for need and desire. Truby will use this end-first method repeatedly for character, plot and theme, because it guarantees the hero and story always head for the true structural end-point, the self-revelation.
- **Both kinds of revelation.** Aim for a psychological and a moral one; be specific about what the hero learns, and stay ready to revise as the other six steps and the rest of the process come into view. Truby likens the work to a crossword: use the easy answers to crack the hard ones, and go back and change earlier entries when later material gives a new angle.
- **Both kinds of weakness and need.** Return to the start and aim for one of each; psychological ones affect only the hero, moral ones affect others. Find many weaknesses, not one, each serious enough to be ruining, or truly threatening to ruin, the hero's life.
- **Problem.** Name the crisis at the start, ideally growing out of the hero's weakness.
- **Desire.** Make it very specific: a goal that carries the hero to the end and forces many actions.
- **Opponent.** Build one who wants the same goal and is exceptionally skilled at attacking the hero's greatest weakness. Of the hundreds possible, find the best by returning to the deepest conflict. The main opponent should be just as obsessed with the goal as the hero, and should hit the hero's chief weakness relentlessly while pursuing it.
- **Plan.** It should require many actions and need adjusting when it fails at first. Since it largely determines what follows, it needs many steps (or the story will be very short) and must be distinctive and complex enough to break down.
- **Battle and new equilibrium.** The battle pits the hero against the main opponent and settles the goal for good. Choose action and violence or words, but make it an intense ordeal that tests the hero to the limit.

### Worked example: *The Godfather* (Truby, PDF p. 26)
Truby breaks the film into the seven steps:
- **Hero:** Michael Corleone.
- **Weaknesses:** young, inexperienced, untested, overconfident.
- **Psychological need:** to outgrow his self-righteous sense of being above others.
- **Moral need:** to protect his family without becoming as ruthless as the other bosses.
- **Problem:** rival gangsters shoot his father, the family head.
- **Desire:** revenge on the shooters, and through it the family's protection.
- **Opponent:** first Sollozzo, then the real opponent, Barzini (as above).
- **Plan:** first, kill Sollozzo and the police captain; later, kill the other family heads in one stroke.
- **Battle:** a crosscut between Michael at his nephew's baptism, professing belief in God and renouncing Satan, and the coordinated killing of the rival heads and of the family's traitors.
- **Psychological self-revelation:** none; Michael still believes his air of superiority is warranted.
- **Moral self-revelation:** none for Michael, who has become a ruthless killer. In what Truby calls an advanced technique, the writers hand the moral revelation to his wife Kay, who grasps what he now is as a door closes in her face.
- **New equilibrium:** Michael has destroyed his enemies and "risen" to Godfather, but in moral terms he has sunk to become the "devil": the man who once wanted no part of the family's crimes now heads it and will have anyone killed who crosses him.

## Terms introduced

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Seven key steps | The minimum stages of any story's growth, listed at the top of this file. |
| Story DNA / nucleus | Truby's image for the seven steps: organic, inside the story, based on how people solve life problems. |
| Weakness | A deep flaw or lack that is holding the hero back or ruining his life. |
| Need | What the hero must fulfill inside himself, usually by overcoming his weakness, to live better; hidden, and unknown to him until the self-revelation. |
| Psychological need | Overcoming a flaw that hurts only the hero. |
| Moral need | Overcoming a flaw that hurts others; learning to treat people rightly. |
| Problem | The crisis the hero is in from page one, an outward sign of his weakness; part of step 1, not a step itself. |
| Desire | The hero's specific goal in this story; the surface track the audience rides. |
| Opponent | The hero's rival for the very same goal, defined by function, not by evil. |
| Deepest level of conflict | What hero and opponent are most fundamentally fighting about. |
| Plan | The hero's guidelines or strategies for beating the opponent and reaching the goal. |
| Battle | The last, deciding clash between hero and opponent over the goal; by violence or words. |
| Self-revelation | The hero's major insight into who he is, produced by the battle's crucible; psychological, moral, or both; can be negative. |
| Facade | The false front the hero lives behind and strips away at the psychological self-revelation. |
| New equilibrium | The return to normal, with desire gone and the hero permanently changed, higher or lower than before. |
| Starting at the end | Truby's method: settle the self-revelation, then work back to need and desire. |
