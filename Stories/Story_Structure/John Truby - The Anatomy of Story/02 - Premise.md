# Premise (Truby, chapter 2)

## Opening example: Crichton and *Jurassic Park*
Truby opens by calling Michael Crichton Hollywood's best premise writer, weaker than Chekhov on character or Dickens on plot but unmatched at the core idea. He imagines the governing idea behind *Jurassic Park* as a fight to the death, in one arena, between the two heavyweights of evolution: dinosaurs and people (Truby, PDF p. 11).

## Where to start the writing process
A writer can begin in several places. A few begin with the seven key structure steps covered in chapter 3; most begin with the shortest possible statement of the whole story, the premise line (Truby, PDF p. 11).

## What a premise is
The premise is the story in a single sentence: the most basic fusion of character and plot. It usually holds three things: an event that sets the action going, some idea of the protagonist, and some idea of how things turn out (Truby, PDF p. 11).

Truby's sample premises, summarized (Truby, PDF p. 11):
- *The Godfather:* a Mafia family's youngest son avenges the shooting of his father and ends up as the new don.
- *Moonstruck:* a woman whose fiancé is away in Italy falls for his brother.
- *Casablanca:* a hard-bitten American abroad meets his lost love again, then lets her go so he can join the war against the Nazis.
- *Streetcar:* a fading beauty tries to land a husband while her sister's crude husband attacks her.
- *Star Wars:* a young man uses his fighting skills to rescue an endangered princess and beat an evil galactic empire.

## Why the premise matters
Truby gives four reasons (Truby, PDF pp. 11-12):
1. **Marketing.** Hollywood sells films worldwide and earns much of its money on opening weekend, so producers want a "high concept": a film that boils down to a catchy line audiences grasp at once and rush to see.
2. **Inspiration.** The premise is the moment of excitement ("that would make a great story") that carries you through months or years of hard work.
3. **Prison.** Choosing one idea shuts out thousands of others, so you had better like the world you've picked.
4. **Foundation.** Every later decision, about character, plot, theme or symbol, grows out of the premise. A strong talent for character, plot or dialogue cannot save a weak premise, just as work on upper floors cannot fix a flawed foundation.

**Key point:** what you write about matters far more than any choice about how you write it (Truby, PDF p. 12).

## Why most writers fail at the premise
**Key point:** Truby claims nine writers in ten fail at this stage (Truby, PDF p. 12). Chiefly, they lack the know-how to develop an idea and extract the value hidden inside it. They miss the premise's real value: it lets you explore the whole story, and the many shapes it could take, before you write it.

### The trap of the high concept
A little knowledge is dangerous here. Screenwriters know Hollywood prizes a high-concept premise, but a sales pitch can't reveal what the story, as a living whole, requires. And every high concept has a built-in structural weakness: it supplies only **two or three scenes**, those just before and just after the twist that makes the idea unique. A feature film has roughly forty to seventy scenes and a novel two or three times as many. Only full command of the craft lets a writer overcome those limits and fill out the rest (Truby, PDF p. 12).

## Take time: weeks, not hours
The first way to find the value in an idea is time. Spend weeks on the premise, not hours or days. The amateur mistake is to grab a hot premise and start writing scenes at once, which leads to a dead end twenty or thirty pages in (Truby, PDF p. 12).

The premise stage is where you work out the story's **grand strategy**: the big picture, its general shape and development. Because you have almost nothing to go on yet, this is the most tentative stage of the whole process, a matter of groping for which options hold together as a living whole and which collapse. Stay flexible and open, and lean hardest on an organic method here (Truby, PDF p. 12).

## Developing the premise: the ten steps

### Step 1: A story that could change your life
Truby calls this a very high bar and possibly the most valuable advice a writer can get; in his experience, no writer who followed it has gone astray. A story that important to you may be equally important to many in the audience, and whatever happens after, writing it will have changed your life (Truby, PDF p. 12).

How can you know in advance? Through **self-exploration**, which Truby says most writers never do. They settle for a rough imitation of another writer's work that seems commercial but isn't personal, and such a story stays generic and fails. To explore yourself you need data about who you are, put down on paper outside yourself, where you can examine it at arm's length (Truby, PDF p. 12). Two exercises:
- **The wish list:** everything you would love to see on screen, in a book or on stage, whatever fascinates and entertains you, such as imagined characters, plot twists, lines of dialogue, themes you care about and genres that draw you. No rejecting, no budget worries, no organizing while you write; let each idea spark the next (Truby, PDF p. 12).
- **The premise list:** every premise you've ever had, whether five or fifty, each written as **one sentence**. The one-sentence rule forces clarity and lets you see all of them together (Truby, PDF p. 12).

Then lay the two lists side by side and look for **core elements that recur** on both: repeated characters or character types, a quality of voice in the dialogue, one or two genres, or a theme, subject or period you keep returning to. These patterns are your **vision** in its rawest form, a picture of yourself as both artist and person. Return to it often. The exercises can't guarantee a life-changing story, but they make any premise you then devise more personal and original (Truby, PDF pp. 12-13).

### Step 2: Look for the possibilities
A major cause of failure is not seeing an idea's true potential, which takes both experience and technique. Look for where the idea could go and how it might grow, and don't seize the first good possibility (Truby, PDF p. 13).

**Key point:** explore your options. Brainstorm the many paths the idea could take, then pick the best (Truby, PDF p. 13).

Two techniques:
- **Find the promises.** Some ideas create expectations that a finished story would have to meet to satisfy its audience. Those promises can steer you toward the strongest option (Truby, PDF p. 13).
- **Ask "What if...?"** Truby rates this more valuable. The question leads both into the idea and into your own mind. It defines what the story world allows and forbids, and it lets you roam that make-believe landscape. Asking it repeatedly lets you live in that landscape, fill in its details and make it gripping. Let the mind run free with no self-censoring; ideas that seem stupid often lead to breakthroughs (Truby, PDF p. 13).

Truby illustrates by imagining how authors might have pushed past an idea's obvious promise (Truby, PDF pp. 13-14):
- *Witness:* past the thriller promise of a boy who sees a crime, toward a study of American violence, setting pacifism against force by moving the boy from the Amish world to the city, then the cop into the Amish world, and finally bringing violence into the pacifist community.
- *Tootsie:* past the fun of a man in a dress, toward a look at how men play the love game from inside; the hero is a chauvinist forced into the one disguise he least wants and most needs, and the pace is pushed toward farce.
- *Chinatown:* past the twists of a whodunit, toward a crime that keeps growing, from a small adultery case to the discovery that a whole city was built on murder, with ever-bigger revelations about America's darkest secrets.
- *The Godfather:* past a story of mob killers, toward making the family head a kind of American king who rules the underworld as the president rules official America, which opens the way to Shakespearean tragedy and a dark national epic.
- Agatha Christie's *Orient Express* mystery: past a clever detective puzzle, toward ultimate poetic justice, in which the victim deserved to die and a jury of twelve acts as both judge and executioner.
- *Big:* past a comic fantasy of a boy who wakes up grown, toward a fantasy set in a world any kid knows, a toy company as a boy's paradise plus a date with an attractive woman, and finally toward showing the ideal mix of adult and child that makes for a happy grown-up life.

### Step 3: Identify the idea's own challenges and problems
Some construction rules apply to every story, but each story also has its **own** rules, or challenges: problems deep in the idea that can't be avoided and shouldn't be. These problems are signposts to the true story, and you have to meet them head-on to execute the story well. Most writers spot them, if ever, only after finishing a draft, which is far too late (Truby, PDF p. 14).

The skill is to see them **at the premise line**. Not even the best writers see them all that early, but mastering character, plot, theme, world, symbol and dialogue makes you better at digging them out (Truby, PDF p. 14). His examples:
- *Star Wars:* introduce a large cast fast and keep them interacting across vast space and time; make a future story feel real and familiar now; give a hero who starts out morally good some kind of change.
- *Forrest Gump:* turn forty years of history into one cohesive, personal story; let a mentally challenged hero drive the plot, have believable insights and change; balance whimsy with real feeling.
- *Beloved:* tell a story of slavery without making the hero a victim; keep drive through constant jumps in time; make the distant past matter now; drive a plot with reactive characters; show slavery's effects on the mind, and how they keep punishing long after it ends.
- *Jaws:* make a fair fight against an opponent of limited intelligence; set up frequent attacks; end with hero and shark one on one.
- *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn:* render a whole nation's moral, or immoral, fabric as fiction; let a boy drive the action; sustain momentum and a strong opponent in a road story made of episodes; believably give a simple, flawed boy great moral insight.
- *The Great Gatsby:* show the American dream degraded into a race for fame and money; create drive when the narrator-hero is only an assistant to another man; make readers care about shallow people; make a modest romance stand for the whole country.
- Arthur Miller's *Salesman:* turn a small man's life into grand tragedy; mix past and present clearly; keep drive; offer hope within a desperate, violent ending.

### Step 4: The designing principle
With the idea's problems and promises in view, you need an overall strategy for telling the story. That strategy, stated in a line, is the story's **designing principle**, and it is what extends the premise into deep structure (Truby, PDF p. 15).

**Key point:** this principle is what organizes the entire story. It is the story's inner logic, binding the parts organically so the whole becomes more than their sum, and it is the source of the story's originality (Truby, PDF p. 15).

What it is, in more detail (Truby, PDF p. 15):
- It is the story's **seed** and the biggest single factor in making the story original and effective.
- Sometimes it is a symbol or metaphor (variously called the central symbol, grand metaphor or root metaphor), but often it is bigger: it traces the **fundamental process** that unfolds over the story.
- It is hard to see, and most stories lack one; they are conventional tales told in a conventional way.

**Premise versus designing principle.** Every story has a premise; only good ones have a designing principle. The premise is **concrete**: the events. The designing principle is **abstract**: the underlying process, rendered in a fresh way. Truby sums it up as a formula, designing principle equals the story's process plus an original way of executing it (Truby, PDF p. 15).

Truby calls it the story's "synthesizing idea" and "shaping cause" (with a footnote), the thing that makes the story one unit internally and sets it apart from every other story. His *Godfather* example: hundreds of writers have shown the Mafia from inside, but a strong writer might frame it with the fairy-tale pattern of the youngest of three sons who becomes the new king (Truby, PDF p. 15).

**Key point:** once you find the principle, commit to it. Search for it diligently and keep it in view through every month of drafting (Truby, PDF p. 15).

*Tootsie* contrast: the premise is an unemployed actor who poses as a woman, lands a part on a TV show and falls for a female castmate; the designing principle is to make a male chauvinist live as a woman (Truby, PDF p. 15).

**How to find it.** The common mistake is to choose a genre, lay it over the premise and make the story hit that genre's usual beats, which yields mechanical, generic fiction. Instead, coax the principle out of the one-line premise, reasoning like a detective to **induce** the story's form from it. There is no single fixed principle per idea. Many are possible, each opening different things to say and each bringing its own problems (Truby, PDF p. 15).

**Kinds of designing principle:**
- **A journey or other traveling metaphor** (Truby, PDF p. 15): Huck and Jim's raft trip down the Mississippi; Marlow's voyage up the river in *Heart of Darkness*; Alice's tumble into Wonderland; Leopold Bloom's wanderings around Dublin (*Ulysses*). For *Heart of Darkness*, Truby reads the trip upriver as heading toward three destinations at once: the truth about a mysterious, seemingly immoral man, the truth about the narrator himself, and back through civilization to the savage moral core found in every person.
- **A single symbol** (Truby, PDF pp. 15-16): Melville's whale, Hawthorne's scarlet A, Thomas Mann's mountain, Shakespeare's island in *The Tempest*.
- **Two grand symbols linked in a process:** the green countryside against the black slag in *How Green Was My Valley* (Truby, PDF p. 16).
- **Units of time** (day and night, the four seasons), **a distinctive use of the storyteller**, or **a special way of unfolding** the story (Truby, PDF p. 16).

**Premise-versus-principle pairs** (Truby, PDF p. 16), summarized:

| Work | Premise (what happens) | Designing principle (the deeper process) |
|---|---|---|
| *Citizen Kane* | the life of a wealthy press baron | several narrators prove that a person's life can never be fully known |
| Moses, Exodus | an Egyptian prince discovers his Hebrew birth and frees his people from bondage | a man ignorant of his own identity fights to free his people and receives the new moral law that will define them both |
| *The Sting* | two grifters cheat the rich man who murdered their friend | the story of a con is itself built as a con, fooling the villain and the viewer alike |
| *Ulysses* | one ordinary Dubliner's day | across one day, a modern odyssey through the city lets one man find a son and another find a father |
| *Meet Me in St. Louis* | a girl falls for the boy next door | one year in a family's growth, told through an event in each season |
| Harry Potter | a boy learns he is a wizard and enrolls at a magic school | over seven school years at a sorcerers' boarding school, a wizard prince grows into manhood and kingship |
| *It's a Wonderful Life* | an angel shows a suicidal man the world without him | one person's power, shown by how his town and his country would look had he never lived |
| *Four Weddings and a Funeral* | a man loves a woman, but each in turn is engaged to someone else | friends pass through four utopias (the weddings) and one hell (the funeral) while hunting for the right spouse |
| *A Christmas Carol* | three ghosts restore a miser's holiday spirit | a man is reborn by being made to see his past, present and future in one night before Christmas |
| *Long Day's Journey into Night* | a family copes with the mother's addiction | as daylight fades into darkness, the family confronts its old sins and ghosts |
| *Copenhagen* | three people give clashing accounts of a meeting that changed World War II | Heisenberg's own uncertainty principle used to probe his murky morality |

### Step 5: The best character in the idea
With the designing principle in hand, the focus shifts to the hero (Truby, PDF p. 16).

**Key point:** always tell the story of your **best character** (Truby, PDF p. 16). Truby stresses that "best" is not about niceness: it means the character who is most complex, most demanding and most fascinating, even an unlikable one. Both writer and audience will be drawn to that figure regardless, so he is the one who should drive the action.

To find the best character, ask **"Who do I love?"**, helped by follow-up questions: would I enjoy seeing him in action, does his mind delight me, do his obstacles matter to me? If no character you love is implied in the idea, drop the idea. If you find one who isn't the lead, rewrite the premise now to make him the lead. If the idea seems to have several main characters, you'll have a story line for each, and each line needs its own best character (Truby, PDF pp. 16-17).

### Step 6: Sense the central conflict
Next, pin down what the story is fundamentally about: its central conflict. Truby's question is **who is fighting whom, and over what**, answered in a single tight line. That answer is what the story is really about, since all the story's conflict reduces to this one issue. Keep this line, together with the designing principle, constantly in view; chapters 3 and 4 build on it (Truby, PDF p. 17).

### Step 7: The single cause-and-effect pathway
A good organic story runs on **one** causal chain, A leading to B leading to C on to Z. This is the story's **spine**. With no spine, or too many, the story falls apart (Truby, PDF p. 17).

Example of a **split premise:** a winery feud between brothers bolted onto a separate love story, which gives two separate trajectories. At the one-line stage this is easy to fix, whereas a finished draft feels set in concrete. Truby's repair merges the two: through a good woman's love, the man beats his brother for the winery (Truby, PDF p. 17).

**How to find the spine:** ask what the hero's **basic action** is. Of the many things the hero does, one outranks and ties together all the rest, and it forms the causal path (Truby, PDF p. 17). Examples:
- *Star Wars:* the basic action is using his skills as a fighter.
- *The Godfather:* the basic action is taking revenge.

**Key point:** when there are several main characters, every story line needs one causal path of its own, and together the lines should form a larger spine that contains them all. *The Canterbury Tales* is the example: each pilgrim's tale has its own spine, and all the tellers belong to one group, a small model of English society, traveling to Canterbury (Truby, PDF p. 17).

### Step 8: The hero's possible character change
Second only to the designing principle, the thing to extract from the premise is the hero's **fundamental change**. Whatever the form, this is what satisfies an audience most deeply, even when the change is for the worse, as in *The Godfather* (Truby, PDF p. 17).

**The change equation: W × A = C** (Truby, PDF p. 17).
- **W** = the hero's weaknesses, psychological as well as moral.
- **A** = the mid-story struggle to accomplish the basic action.
- **C** = the changed person.

Truby warns not to confuse its three parts with three-act structure. In most stories, a flawed character struggles to achieve something and is changed, for better or worse. The story's logic is the question of how struggling at A turns W into C. The basic action is the **fulcrum**: pass a flawed character through the right ordeal, and the ordeal hammers him into a new person (Truby, PDF p. 17).

**Key point:** choose as the basic action whatever will most strongly compel the hero to face his weaknesses and change (Truby, PDF p. 17). Truby calls this the simple geometry of every story, since it mirrors how people actually grow, which is hard to pin down but real, and which the writer must express above all, or else show why it fails to happen (Truby, PDF pp. 17-18).

**Method: go to the opposites** (Truby, PDF p. 18). Start from the basic action and work out its opposites; they tell you who the hero is at the start (his weaknesses) and at the end (his change). In order:
1. Write the simple premise line, ready to revise it once the change is found.
2. Set the hero's basic action across the story.
3. Find opposites of A for both W (psychological and moral weaknesses) and C (the changed person).

Opposites are essential because they are the only way change can happen. If the hero's weaknesses resemble the basic action, the action will only deepen them and he will stay the same (Truby, PDF p. 18).

**Key point:** write down several options for the weaknesses and the change; there are as many possibilities here as in developing the premise (Truby, PDF p. 18). Truby's illustration uses the basic action "joins a gang of outlaws," paired with three different openings and endings: a tense man ruled by his wife who ends up divorcing; a stiff, arrogant banker who ends up helping the poor; a bashful, nervous man who ends up intoxicated by fame (Truby, PDF p. 18).

Worked examples (Truby, PDF p. 18):
- *Star Wars:* Luke starts naive, rash, stuck, scattered and unsure of himself, qualities nothing like a fighter's. Repeatedly forced to fight, he becomes a confident warrior for good who has earned self-respect and a place among an elite few.
- *The Godfather:* Michael starts detached from the family, fearful, conventional and legitimate. Taking revenge turns him into the family's tyrannical, absolute ruler. Had he begun as a vengeful man, revenge would only make him more of the same, with no change. Starting him as the opposite yields a radical but fully believable transformation.

The result is only a set of *possible* changes. Premise work, especially on change, is highly tentative; stay open to other changes as you go. Chapters 3 and 4 go further (Truby, PDF p. 18).

### Step 9: The hero's possible moral choice
A story's central theme is often crystallized in a **moral choice** the hero faces, usually late in the story. Theme is your idea of how people ought to behave, your moral vision, and a chief reason for writing at all (Truby, PDF pp. 18-19).

Theme works best through the story's structure, through what Truby calls the **moral argument**: the author argues for a way of living, not through philosophy but through what characters do in pursuit of a goal (see chapter 5). The hero's final moral choice may be the argument's most important move (Truby, PDF p. 19).

**The fake choice.** Many writers give the hero a choice between a good and a bad option, such as prison versus winning the girl. The answer is obvious, so it isn't a real choice (Truby, PDF p. 19).

**Key point:** a true choice means picking between two **good** outcomes or, occasionally, escaping one of two **bad** ones (*Sophie's Choice* is his example) (Truby, PDF p. 19). Make the options as close to equal as possible, with one only slightly better. The classic pair of positives is **love versus honor**: the hero of *A Farewell to Arms* picks love; in *The Maltese Falcon*, as in most detective stories, the hero picks honor (Truby, PDF p. 19).

As with change, this yields only *possible* choices; the real one may be quite different by the final draft. The point is to start thinking practically about theme from the very beginning (Truby, PDF p. 19).

### Step 10: Gauge audience appeal
Finally, ask whether this story line is distinctive enough to interest many people besides you. This is the question of commercial appeal, and it must be answered ruthlessly. If only you and your family would want it, Truby strongly advises against building a full story on it (Truby, PDF p. 19).

Write first for yourself, about what you care about, but not *only* for yourself. A big mistake is **either-or thinking**, the belief that you must choose between writing what you care about and writing what sells. Truby calls this a false split, inherited from the romantic image of the suffering artist in a garret. Sometimes an idea simply must be written, or you can't tell whether audiences will like it, but you'll have far more ideas than you can ever develop, so aim for ones you care about **and** expect to appeal widely. Aiming at an audience also makes it easier to keep doing the work you love (Truby, PDF p. 19).

## Exercises in this chapter

### Writing Exercise 1: Creating Your Premise (Truby, PDF p. 19)
The exercise walks the writer through the ten steps in order, applied to the writer's own idea:
- write the premise as one sentence and test whether it could be life-changing;
- make the wish list and premise list and study them together for recurring core elements;
- write down options for what the premise could become;
- list the challenges and problems particular to the idea;
- state a designing principle, meaning the deeper process or form through which the story will unfold uniquely;
- find the best character and make him the premise's hero;
- state whom the hero fights and over what;
- name the basic action, which gives the single cause-and-effect path;
- derive the possible change by going from the basic action to its opposites for the starting weaknesses and the ending change;
- name a possible late moral choice, hard but plausible;
- judge whether the premise will reach a wider audience, and start over if not.

### Worked example: *Tootsie* (Truby, PDF pp. 19-20)
Truby runs *Tootsie* through every item, briefly:
- **Possibilities:** a comic look at modern dating that also dissects the immorality beneath how men and women treat each other in intimate life.
- **Challenge:** show the harm men's immoral behavior does to women without attacking one sex and painting the other as innocent.
- **Problems:** making the female disguise convincing; weaving several romantic plots into one; resolving each; and delivering a satisfying love story while farce devices let the audience know more than the characters.
- **Designing principle:** make a chauvinist live as a woman, set in show business so the disguise is believable.
- **Best character:** Michael, whose split between living as a man and as a woman gives his inner contradiction a physical, comic form.
- **Conflict:** Michael against Julie, Ron, Les and Sandy, over love and honesty.
- **Basic action:** a man impersonates a woman.
- **Change:** an arrogant, lying womanizer who, by passing as a woman, becomes a better man able to love.
- **Moral choice:** he gives up his lucrative role and apologizes to Julie for deceiving her.

## Terms introduced

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Premise | The whole story in one sentence: an inciting event, a sense of the main character and a sense of the outcome. |
| High concept | A premise reducible to a catchy line audiences grasp instantly; good for marketing, but it yields only the few scenes around its twist. |
| Grand strategy | The big-picture shape and development of the story, worked out at the premise stage. |
| Wish list | A free, uncensored list of everything the writer would love to see in a story. |
| Premise list | Every premise the writer has had, each in one sentence. |
| Vision | The recurring core elements found across the wish list and premise list; the writer's identity on paper. |
| Promises | Expectations an idea creates that a full story must satisfy. |
| "What if...?" | The brainstorming question for exploring an idea's possibilities and its story world. |
| Story challenges and problems | The difficulties built into a specific idea, to be found at the premise stage and solved. |
| Designing principle | The one-line overall strategy that organizes the whole story; story process plus original execution. |
| Central symbol, grand metaphor, root metaphor | Names for a designing principle that takes the form of a single symbol or metaphor. |
| Best character | The idea's most complex, compelling and demanding character, not the nicest; he should be the hero. |
| Central conflict | Who fights whom over what, in one line. |
| Single cause-and-effect pathway (spine) | The one causal chain from start to end that holds the story together. |
| Split premise | A premise with two separate causal tracks, to be merged into one. |
| Basic action | The hero's one most important action, which unifies all the others and forms the spine. |
| W × A = C | Weaknesses, put through the struggle of the basic action, produce the changed person. |
| Opposites method | Deriving the hero's starting weaknesses and ending change as opposites of the basic action. |
| Moral argument | Theme expressed through structure and character action rather than speeches (chapter 5). |
| Fake choice | A choice between a good and a bad option, whose answer is obvious. |
| True moral choice | A choice between two near-equal goods, or rarely between two evils. |
| Either-or thinking | The false belief that you must choose between personal writing and commercial writing. |
