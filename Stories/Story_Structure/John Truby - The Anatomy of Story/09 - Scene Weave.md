## 1. Why the weave matters
Truby opens by asking why Jane Austen and Charles Dickens still hold readers. Part of his answer is that both are master weavers of scenes (Truby, PDF p. 142).

## 2. What a scene is
A scene, in Truby's working definition, is usually a single action happening in a single time and place. It is the smallest unit of what actually happens in the story, experienced by the audience as happening now (Truby, PDF p. 142).

## 3. What the scene weave is
- **The weave is the order of those units.** Truby's image is a tapestry: a thread surfaces for a moment, slips back under the cloth, and comes up again further along (Truby, PDF pp. 142-143).
- **Other names for it:** the scene list, scene outline or scene breakdown (Truby, PDF p. 143).
- **Its place in the process:** it is the last step before drafting the full story or script. It lists every scene you expect the finished story to have, and it marks the scenes in which a structure step happens (Truby, PDF p. 143).
- **What it is for:** like the seven steps, the character web and the sequence of revelations, it lets you see how the story fits together under the surface (Truby, PDF p. 143).
- **It extends the plot.** The weave is the plot at the finest grain, and its purpose is one final look at the whole architecture before you write (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 4. Keep each entry to one line
- Too much detail hides the structure. Aim for one line per scene, naming only the one essential action (Truby, PDF p. 143).
- Truby shows four one-line entries from *The Godfather* (the hospital rescue, the confrontation with the police captain, Michael's proposal to kill the captain and Sollozzo, Clemenza's lesson in how to do it) as models of the right level of detail (Truby, PDF p. 143).
- At one or two lines per scene, the whole weave fits on a few pages (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 5. Tag the structure steps
Next to each scene description, note any structure step the scene accomplishes (his examples: desire, plan, apparent defeat). Some scenes carry tags; many carry none (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 6. Expect the weave to change
**Key point:** be ready to revise the weave once you start writing individual scenes. You only learn what a scene's real core action is when you are inside it, writing it. At this stage the goal is an overview of what you currently believe each scene's single main action will be (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 7. How many scenes
- A typical Hollywood film has roughly forty to seventy scenes.
- A novel usually has about double that, and depending on length and genre it can have far more (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 8. Label subplots and subsections
If the story is woven from several subplots or subsections, mark every scene with its plotline and subsection number. Then you can pull out each subplot's scenes, read them as a unit, and check that each one builds correctly (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 9. Three revisions to make once the full weave is in front of you
Truby lists three kinds of change to look for (Truby, PDF p. 143):
- **Reorder.** Get the large-scale sequence of the story right first. Only then look at how individual scenes sit next to each other.
- **Combine.** Writers often add a scene for no better reason than to fit in one good line of dialogue. Merge scenes where you can so each is packed, but keep each scene to essentially one action.
- **Cut or add.** Trim all fat. Pacing depends on which scenes you choose, not only on how long each one runs. Once the fat is gone you may find gaps that need a new scene; put it in the right spot.

## 10. Order scenes by structure, not chronology
**Key point.** Most writers pick the next scene by asking what comes next in time. That produces a padded story full of useless scenes (Truby, PDF p. 143).
- Instead, pick each scene by how it advances the hero's development.
- If a scene neither advances that development nor sets it up in some crucial way, cut it.
- Truby says this guarantees every scene is necessary and in the right place. The result is usually still chronological, but not always (Truby, PDF p. 143).

## 11. Juxtaposition of scenes
**Key point:** watch closely how each scene sits against its neighbors. In film and television the change of scene or storyline is instant, so the pairing of two scenes can matter more than what happens inside either one (Truby, PDF p. 143). Check two contrasts:
- **Contrast of content.** Does the next scene comment on the one before it, and if so, how?
- **Contrast of proportion and pacing.** Is the following scene or section the right weight and length compared with what came just before it? (Truby, PDF p. 143)

### "Find the line and keep the line"
Truby's rule of thumb (Truby, PDF p. 143):
- Some scenes, such as subplot scenes, exist only to set up the narrative drive, and it is fine to include them.
- But you can never stray from the main narrative line for long, or the story collapses.

## 12. Juxtaposing sight and sound
- One of the strongest juxtapositions, especially on screen, splits the picture from the soundtrack so that together they make a third meaning (Truby, PDF pp. 143-144).
- **Example, *M* (1931):** a child murderer buys a girl a balloon. Next, her mother calls her name with mounting panic while the images show only empty places (stairwell, apartment block, her empty chair and setting at the table) and end on the balloon caught in wires and drifting off. Truby counts it among film's most heartbreaking moments (Truby, PDF p. 144).

## 13. The crosscut
The most common juxtaposition technique in a weave is the crosscut: alternating among two or more lines of action. Truby gives it two main effects (Truby, PDF p. 144):
1. **Suspense,** especially when the cuts speed up, as in a race to save someone in danger.
2. **Comparison.** Crosscutting puts two lines of action, two pieces of content, side by side and makes them equal. That widens the thematic pattern: the story moves from the simple linear development of one character to a deeper pattern running through the whole society.

### Examples
- **The content crosscut in *M*:** the film alternates between police and criminals, both trying to work out how to catch the child killer. The audience sees that two groups it thinks of as opposites are in many ways the same (Truby, PDF p. 144).
- **The battle in *The Godfather*:** the battle has to express what Michael has become as the new Godfather.
  - Crosscutting among his men as they kill the heads of the five families gives a dense run of plot punches. It also shows Michael as a kind of corporate chief of crime, who has specialists on staff do the killing rather than doing it himself in a crime of passion (Truby, PDF p. 144).
  - A second crosscut sets the killings against the baptism, where Michael renounces Satan while standing as godfather to the baby of a man he is about to have murdered. In one movement he becomes Satan and reaches the peak of his power (Truby, PDF p. 144).

## 14. Case study: two drafts of *The Godfather*
Truby compares an early draft's weave with the final one for the stretch just after Michael shoots Sollozzo and McCluskey (Truby, PDF pp. 144-145).
- **The early draft** grouped the material in two blocks: first all the Sonny scenes, through Sonny's death and the end of the war between the families, then all of Michael's Sicily scenes, ending in his wife's murder (Truby, PDF pp. 144-145).
- **The problems** with that order (Truby, PDF p. 145):
  - The plot-heavy, more dramatic material (Sonny's killing, the discovery about Barzini) came first, so the move to Sicily felt like a letdown.
  - The long, slow Sicily section brought the story to a dead stop, and restarting it afterward was very hard.
  - Running all the Apollonia scenes together exposed how sudden and hard to believe Michael's marriage was. The "thunderbolt" explanation in the dialogue could not cover it.
- **The final draft** crosscuts the Sonny line with the Michael line (Truby, PDF p. 145):
  - The slow Sicily line is never on screen long enough to kill the narrative drive.
  - Both lines narrow to one point, the hero's apparent defeat: Sonny's murder followed almost immediately by Apollonia's, a one-two punch.
  - That double blow is then topped by the reveal that Barzini was behind everything. Exposing Barzini as the true opponent drives the rest of the plot to its ending.

## 15. Learning the weave by case study
Truby says the scene weave, more than any of his other techniques, is best learned from cases. He starts with television, because TV drama is built on weaving many juxtaposed storylines (Truby, PDF p. 145).

## 16. The multistrand plot weave (television drama)
**Form.** A TV multistrand episode crosscuts three to five major storylines, each with its own hero. In about forty-five minutes of airtime no single line can go deep. The writers count on building depth across a season and the show's run (Truby, PDF p. 145).

Three key points (Truby, PDF p. 145):
- **The quality comes from juxtaposition.** The overall story's strength comes mainly from how the plotlines are set against each other. You compare what several people in a small society face at the same moment, and the audience sees in compressed form how different leads try different solutions to much the same problem.
- **Seven steps per line, not twenty-two.** With three to five lines you can't cover the twenty-two steps for any of them. But every line must hit the seven major steps. A line with fewer is not a whole story, and audiences find such a line pointless and irritating.
- **Make one line's hero another line's opponent.** This gives the whole a shape and keeps narrative drive. Without it the story keeps expanding outward (five heroes, five opponents, crowds of minor characters).

**Dramatic density.** Part of the appeal is that there is no lull: the audience sees only each line's punch scenes (Truby, PDF pp. 145-146). Truby credits *ER*'s creator, Michael Crichton, with fusing the medical drama and the action genre and adding a character web broad in class, race, ethnicity, nationality and gender (Truby, PDF p. 146).

### Example: *ER*, "The Dance We Do" (2000)
The episode has five plotlines, each continuing threads from earlier episodes: a doctor's bipolar mother visiting; a surgeon being deposed in a malpractice suit; a beaten girl connected to a doctor's murdered nephew; a doctor waiting to learn whether his brain tumor will kill him; and a doctor in recovery facing mandatory drug testing (Truby, PDF p. 146).

What the example demonstrates:
- **Underlying unity.** All five lines are variations on one problem, which makes the juxtapositions pay off. On the surface several involve drugs. More deeply, all five show different effects of lying and of telling the truth (Truby, PDF p. 146).
- **Two principles drive the weave:** each line is a variation on truth and lies, and all five narrow down to the strongest revelation or self-revelation the character and plot can deliver (Truby, PDF p. 146).
- **Each line hits the seven steps,** so each stands up alone. That foundation is what frees the writer to play with juxtaposing scenes from different lines (Truby, PDF p. 146).
- **Lines can refer to each other.** One plotline's scene is a later moment of an event that a neighboring scene in another plotline is talking about (Truby, PDF p. 148).
- **Clustered parallels.** A run of three scenes from three lines each shows a character lying to others and refusing to admit a problem even privately. Later, one character finally tells someone the truth about himself, and the very next scene shows another character told by her lawyer to hide the truth (Truby, PDF p. 149).
- **Act-end funnel.** An act closes on the most public humiliation in one line, showing where lying ("doing the dance") leads (Truby, PDF p. 150).
- **Late density.** Near the end, battles and self-revelations arrive fast and furious, which Truby calls one of the great payoffs of the multistrand method. One character's battle ends in a moral decision to lie; another explains to a third (who has been lying about his own drug use) the never-ending dance of drugs and lies she shares with her mother (Truby, PDF pp. 150-151).
- **Framing and an old equilibrium.** The episode begins and ends with the same plotline, which frames it and unifies the strands. The last scene, a woman crying in secret so as not to wake her partner, gives that line a twist: nothing will change for these people. It is an old equilibrium, not a new one. The audience realizes, painfully, that life doesn't always deliver change and growth at the end (Truby, PDF p. 151).

## 17. Genre weaves: the shift to multiple examples
After the exercise, Truby breaks his usual pattern of one closing example and walks through several full weaves. Each weave is unique to its story, but he asks the reader to notice how each genre poses its own weaving problems (Truby, PDF p. 152). The four examples below are summarized by what they show; the scene lists themselves are not reproduced.

### 17a. Detective or crime weave: *L.A. Confidential* (1997)
**The shape is a funnel** (Truby, PDF p. 152):
- It starts with three cop heroes inside a corrupt police department and gradually weaves their three lines into one.
- Drive is maintained by making the heroes opponents of each other while all three hunt for the killer at the narrow end.
- The setup lets the writers compare, by crosscutting, the three men's approaches to crime-solving and justice, and gives them a dense run of reveals as the funnel tightens.
- The main opponent, the Captain, appears to be an ally.

Stages Truby's commentary picks out:
- **The opening** voice-over lays down the world (1950s Los Angeles) and its basic thematic opposition: an apparent paradise that is rotten underneath (Truby, PDF p. 152).
- **Introducing three heroes and two opponents.** Each hero gets defining scenes: the tough cop who protects women, the slick cop who works a TV show for side money, and the rising cop who insists on staying legally and morally clean. The fake-ally Captain appears early, and the second major opponent is slipped in quietly in an early scene (Truby, PDF p. 152).
- **A watershed event** (the beating of prisoners) defines all three heroes and the corrupt world, and turns the clean cop into an opponent of the other two (Truby, PDF p. 153).
- **Fragmenting into three crosscut lines:** each hero gets a new situation, and the hidden opponent starts killing gangsters (Truby, PDF p. 154).
- **The inciting event,** a mass murder, starts the funneling (Truby, PDF p. 155).
- **A false drive.** Guided by the fake-ally Captain, all three chase the wrong suspects (Truby, PDF p. 156).
- **Avoiding fragmentation** by putting the focus on two heroes' opposition. A major section closes with the drive apparently accomplished (Truby, PDF p. 157).
- **Moving a background opponent into the foreground** with several scenes showing his reach across the city (Truby, PDF p. 158).
- **Simultaneous lines unified by disillusionment.** Back in crosscut, each hero is becoming disgusted with his own original desire (Truby, PDF pp. 158-159).
- **Gaining focus.** The heroes pursue the real killer, at first separately, each by his own methods and for his own redemption (Truby, PDF p. 159).
- **The funnel speeds up** when two heroes team up, while the rivalry between two of them gets worse (Truby, PDF p. 160).
- **Payoff of the setup.** Having built the world and three distinct lines carefully, the writers can fire a series of revelations. The biggest is an audience stunner: the fake-ally Captain murders one of the heroes (Truby, PDF p. 161).
- **A minibattle** between the two surviving heroes ends in an alliance that drives the rest of the story (Truby, PDF p. 162). More revelations funnel them into the final battle with the Captain (Truby, PDF p. 163).
- **The ending sends the two survivors apart.** The political cop turns the outcome into another medal. His opposite, the simple tough guy, leaves for a small town with the woman he loves (Truby, PDF p. 164).

### 17b. Crosscut weave: *The Empire Strikes Back* (1980)
Truby calls it a textbook crosscut weave, used for a large stretch of the plot (scenes 25-58) (Truby, PDF p. 164).
- **The structural reasons** (Truby, PDF p. 165):
  - As the middle film of a trilogy, it has neither the first film's focus on introducing the hero nor the third's convergence in a final battle.
  - Crosscutting lets the middle story widen the trilogy to the largest possible scope, the universe, while still keeping narrative drive. That is harder still because a middle episode must also stand on its own.
- **Which capabilities of the crosscut it uses.** The crosscut's deepest power, comparing content, is not used here. The film uses its plot powers instead: more suspense, cliffhangers, and more action packed into a film's limited running time (Truby, PDF p. 165).
- **The main reason is the hero's development,** as Truby says it should be (Truby, PDF p. 165):
  - Luke needs long training to become a Jedi.
  - Training is a single structure step, and not even one of the twenty-two, so tracking only Luke in a linear weave would have stalled the plot.
  - Crosscutting the training with the big escape-and-chase scenes of the other leads gives Luke's development the time it needs without the plot grinding to a halt.
- **The tagged list** (71 scenes) shows the steps falling scene by scene: problem, allies, visit to death, inciting event, revelations, opponent's plan and attack, battle, desire, plan, need, drive, apparent defeat, fake-ally opponent, attack by ally, and finally battle with self-revelation and a new equilibrium (Truby, PDF pp. 165-166).

### 17c. Love story weave: *Pride and Prejudice* (1940 film)
What the commentary demonstrates:
- **Desire first.** The first scene after the title goes straight to the desire (finding a husband). That gives the writers a line on which to hang the description of the story world (Truby, PDF p. 167).
- **Five subplots for comparison.** At the first ball the writers set up the main spine, the heroine Lizzy against Darcy. Giving the family five daughters lets them weave five subplots (four sisters plus Charlotte) that compare women and how they find husbands (Truby, PDF p. 168).
  - Truby likens this to *The Philadelphia Story*, where one woman chooses among three suitors.
  - The subplots give density and texture, and audiences enjoy small story moments for minor characters who play off the leads' problem.
- **Setup buys reveals.** Laying out the world, the hero's line and five subplots gives the writers a dense series of reveals later. Truby calls that rare, and welcome, in a love-story form that often has little plot. It also lets the comedy end with several marriages, one of them bad (Truby, PDF p. 168).
- **The world's logic shapes every line.** Property passes to the male heir, so the women must marry, and marry well (Truby, PDF p. 168).
- **Comparisons across characters.** The women are compared through Miss Bingley and Charlotte; the suitors through Wickham and Mr. Collins. The comparisons start at the first party (Truby, PDF p. 168).
- **Opposition first, then a subplot in its place.** The writers set up a strong initial opposition between the eventual lovers. Instead of playing that out continuously, they play out the sister's romance subplot, which lets Lizzy spend time near Darcy while keeping the opposition alive (Truby, PDF p. 169) ⚠️ (OCR partly garbled here).
- **A second suitor sharpens the central conflict.** The foolish rival suitor, also a threat to the family because he will inherit the estate, highlights the conflict for every woman in this world: marrying for money versus marrying for love (Truby, PDF p. 169).
- **Combining scenes.** The second party pulls many lines into one tight knot (suitors, the hero's moral argument with Darcy, the sister's romance, the female opponent, the embarrassing sisters). Truby stresses how much these scenes matter, because the whole community and cast are present (Truby, PDF p. 169).
- **Paired defeats.** The sister's breakup is followed by another apparent defeat for the heroine: her best friend marries the foolish second suitor (Truby, PDF p. 170).
- **A proposal that is also a breakup.** Darcy's surprise proposal turns into a breakup, because both still suffer from the psychological and moral weaknesses of the title, pride and prejudice (Truby, PDF p. 171).
- **A cyclonic ending.** The weave closes with a dense run of revelations and marriages: the audience learns a suitor is really an opponent, Darcy is revealed as good, the heroine realizes she loves him, and the marriages fall into place across the subplots. Truby links this back to the "cyclonic" reveals he described for *Tootsie* in the plot chapter, and calls such plot density rare in love stories (Truby, PDF pp. 171-172).
- The final scenes give the new equilibrium through the mother's eyes, tallying marriages and incomes (Truby, PDF p. 173).

### 17d. Social fantasy weave: *It's a Wonderful Life* (1946)
What the commentary demonstrates:
- **A crisis-framed opening.** The film opens with angels in the sky discussing the hero at a moment of crisis (Truby, PDF p. 174). This:
  - shows the entire arena of the story, the town;
  - starts with dramatic intensity;
  - earns the writers permission to go back over the hero's past, because the audience has been promised high drama later (the suicide attempt);
  - sets up the fantasy payoff, in which the hero sees his town as it would be had he never lived.
- **Childhood scenes as setup.** A run of scenes from the hero's boyhood defines his essential character and the key townspeople. It also builds the web of connections, of character and of action, that pays off at the end (Truby, PDF p. 174).
- **The adult desire line.** Jumping to adulthood, the weave states the desire plainly (leave town, see the world). The minor characters reappear as adults, essentially unchanged (Truby, PDF p. 174).
- **A repeating three-part pattern** (Truby, PDF pp. 174-175). Each scene in a long sequence does the same three things:
  1. the hero states his wish to leave;
  2. something frustrates it and keeps him in town;
  3. a second, conflicting desire binds him to the town more tightly.

  Examples: his father's death puts him in charge of the family business; his brother comes home married with a job offer elsewhere; he falls in love, carries the town through the Depression, builds the housing development and has children.
- **A unique time shift.** After covering almost three decades, the weave slows to one day of scenes leading to the crisis named in the opening. They end exactly where the angel's voice-over began, delivering on the opening's promise (Truby, PDF p. 176).
- **The key sequence:** the angel shows the hero an alternative present and town in which he never lived. This is where the investment in the world and the hero's ties to the townspeople pays off. He sees the minor characters at their worst, and he and the audience see how wide his web of connections is (Truby, PDF p. 177).
- **Ending.** He returns to the real present, happy even though the money is still lost, and the web pays off once more when the town rescues him (Truby, PDF p. 178).
- **Verdict.** The weave makes the most of the large social contrasts social fantasy depends on. The sequence is dense and the juxtapositions are excellent (Truby, PDF p. 178).

## Exercises the chapter gives the writer
**Writing Exercise 8, "Scene Weave"** (Truby, PDF pp. 151-152) asks the writer to:
1. **Make a scene list:** write down every scene in the story, each described in about a sentence.
2. **Tag the steps:** mark every scene that contains one of the twenty-two structure steps, and label each scene with its plotline or subsection if there is more than one.
3. **Check the order:** confirm the sequence builds by structure rather than by chronology. Then look for scenes to cut, pairs of scenes to merge, and gaps in the story's development where a new scene is needed.

## Terms introduced
- **Scene:** usually a single action in a single time and place; the basic unit of what happens in the story "now."
- **Scene weave** (also scene list, scene outline, scene breakdown): the ordered list of every scene, one line each, with structure-step tags; the last step before drafting.
- **Structure tag:** a note beside a scene naming the structure step (desire, plan, apparent defeat and so on) that happens in it.
- **Ordering by structure:** choosing each next scene by how it advances the hero's development rather than by what comes next in time.
- **Juxtaposition:** the meaning made by placing one scene next to another. Judged by contrast of content and by contrast of proportion and pacing.
- **"Find the line and keep the line":** you may step away from the main narrative line (for example to set up a subplot), but never for long.
- **Sight-sound split:** separating the image track from the sound track so that together they create a third meaning.
- **Crosscut:** alternating among two or more action lines. It builds suspense and puts the lines on an equal footing for comparison.
- **Content crosscut:** a crosscut used mainly to compare what the lines have in common, widening the thematic pattern to the whole society.
- **Multistrand plot:** the TV form that crosscuts three to five storylines, each with its own hero, in one episode.
- **Dramatic density:** showing only each line's punch scenes, so there is no lull.
- **Funnel weave:** several lines that start apart and narrow into one as the story closes in on a single point (*L.A. Confidential*).
- **False drive** (Truby's commentary on *L.A. Confidential*): a stretch in which the heroes, misled by a fake-ally opponent, pursue the wrong target.
- **Combining scene** (commentary on *Pride and Prejudice*): a scene, often a gathering, that ties many lines into one knot with the whole community present.
- **Old equilibrium** (commentary on *ER*): an ending in which the characters return to the same state rather than reaching a new one.
