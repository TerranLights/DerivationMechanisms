## 1. Why plot gets underrated (p. 115)

Truby opens by calling plot the storytelling skill writers most often underestimate. Writers accept that character and dialogue matter, even if they write them badly, but they assume plot will sort itself out when the time comes, and it never does (PDF p. 115).

Plot is hard by nature. It braids characters and actions across the whole length of a story, so it has to be worked out in fine detail and still hold together as one thing. A single event that fails can bring the whole story down (PDF p. 115).

**Against three-act structure.** He argues that any plot method unable to cover the story as a whole and its separate threads at once is bound to fail, and he names three-act structure as the main offender. Writers who rely on it keep running into "second-act problems" because the method is mechanical and simplistic: it gives no detailed map for weaving the plot through the long, difficult middle (PDF p. 115). He returns to this at p. 127 (see step 9's file): a method that asks for two or three plot points produces a weak plot, when a typical Hollywood hit carries somewhere between seven and ten big reveals.

## 2. What plot is, and what it isn't (p. 115)

**Three misconceptions.** Writers often think plot is the same thing as story; or that it just follows a hero chasing a goal; or that it is the manner of the telling (PDF p. 115).

**Story is bigger than plot.** Story is every subsystem of the "story body" in concert: premise, character, moral argument, world, symbol, plot, scene, dialogue. Plot is one strand among these. He supports this with a quotation from another critic (note 1 in the book) (PDF p. 115).

**Truby's definition.** Plot is the weaving, below the surface, of several lines of action or sets of events, so that the story keeps building from its start, through its middle, to its end. More narrowly, it follows the complicated back-and-forth between the hero and every one of his opponents as they all struggle for the same goal. It combines two things: what happens, and the way those happenings are disclosed to the audience (PDF p. 115).

**Key point: withholding and revealing.** A plot stands or falls on how the writer holds back information and then lets it out. He quotes another writer (note 2) describing plotting as the management of suspense and mystery, leading the reader through a space full of signs that are always at risk of being misread until the end (PDF p. 115).

## 3. The organic plot (pp. 115-116)

**A sequence of events is not yet a good plot.** Any account of one thing happening after another counts as plot in the loose sense, but such a list has no purpose and no designing principle to decide which events get told and in what order (PDF p. 115).

**What "organic" means.** Truby holds that a good plot is organic, and breaks that into several requirements (PDF pp. 115-116):
- It shows the actions that bring about the hero's character change, or that explain why he can't change.
- Every event is linked to the others by cause and effect.
- Every event is necessary.
- Every action gets length and pacing in proportion to its weight.
- How much plot there is seems to grow out of the main character, not to be forced onto the cast by the author. Forced plot feels like a machine with its gears showing, and it empties the characters of their humanity until they read as puppets or game pieces. A plot that grows from the hero is not the same as a scheme the hero thinks up; it is a plot that suits what this character wants and how well he can plan and act.
- The events add up to a single, complete effect. Truby cites Edgar Allan Poe's test: move any part and the whole is ruined (note 3).

**The built-in contradiction.** Organic plot is hard to understand and harder to make, partly because plotting contradicts itself. The writer invents events from nothing and arranges them, yet the result has to feel as if each stage grew by necessity on its own (PDF p. 116).

## 4. The two "legs" of story: acting and learning (p. 116)

Every story moves on two legs: **taking action** and **learning information**. Over history, plot has shifted its weight from the first to the second (PDF p. 116).
- **Early plot (myth):** a main character performs a series of heroic deeds, which the audience is moved to imitate.
- **Later plot (a broad detective form):** the hero and the audience both start out ignorant of, or confused about, what is really going on, and the job is to work out the truth about the events and the people.

## 5. Plot types (pp. 116-118)

Truby surveys the major plot strategies to show the different ways storytellers order events and try to make a plot organic (PDF p. 116).

### 5.1 The journey plot (p. 116)

**What it is.** The first big plot strategy, from the myth tellers. The hero travels, meets a string of opponents one after another, beats each, and comes home (PDF p. 116).

**Why it should be organic.** In principle it is, for two reasons: one person carries the single line of action, and the journey gives the hero's inner change a physical form. Each defeated opponent can bring a small change; the largest change, the self-revelation, comes when he gets home and finds what was in him all along, his deepest abilities (PDF p. 116).

**Why it usually isn't.** Two problems (PDF p. 116):
- The hero almost never changes, even slightly, as he beats each opponent. He just wins and moves on, so every fight with a new stranger repeats the same plot beat, and the story feels episodic.
- The hero covers so much ground and time that the writer struggles to bring early characters back in a natural, believable way.

**Repairs writers have tried.**
- *Tom Jones* (Henry Fielding): a comic journey. Fielding conceals who the hero and some others really are at the start, so familiar characters can return and be seen more deeply; this grafts the reveal onto the journey. He also sets many early characters traveling separately toward Tom's destination, which funnels them together so Tom keeps colliding with them (PDF p. 116).
- *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (Mark Twain): the raft is a clever miniature floating island for Huck and Jim, but it is too small to carry ongoing opponents, so they meet one stranger after another. Twain also has no natural way to end the trip, so he halts it arbitrarily and rescues the plot with a deus ex machina; Tom Sawyer's return has no story reason beyond getting back to comedy and closing (PDF p. 116).

### 5.2 The three unities plot (pp. 116-117)

**What it is.** The Greek dramatists' strategy (Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides), built on what Aristotle called the unities (of time, of place, of action): the story spans twenty-four hours, stays in one location and follows one line of action (PDF p. 116).

**Why it's organic.** All the action comes from the hero within a very short span, and it fixes the journey plot's main flaw because the opponents are people the hero knows and who stay present throughout (PDF pp. 116-117).

**Its weakness: too little plot.** The short time frame limits how many revelations there can be and how strong they are. Revelations are the "learning" half of plot and the main source of plot complexity. With so little time, the hero is too familiar with his opponents; they may have laid their scheme before the story opens, but once it starts they can't hide much. The result is usually room for one big reveal, as when Oedipus (in what Truby calls the first detective story) learns he has murdered his father and married his mother. A plot-rich story needs reveals spread all through it (PDF p. 117).

### 5.3 The reveals plot (p. 117)

**What it is.** The hero mostly stays in one area, though a much wider one than the unity of place allows (a town or a city), and the story usually covers more time, up to a few years. (A story spanning decades is probably a saga, which leans back toward the journey plot.) (PDF p. 117)

**Its key technique.** The hero knows his opponents, yet much about them stays concealed from hero and audience alike, and the opponents are expert schemers. Together these produce a plot packed with surprises (PDF p. 117).

**Contrast with the journey.** The journey plot limits surprise because the hero disposes of many opponents quickly. The reveals plot uses few opponents and conceals as much about them as it can. Revelations enlarge the plot by taking it beneath the surface (PDF p. 117).

**When it's organic.** When the opponent is the person best placed to attack the hero's weakness, and the surprises land at the moments when hero and audience discover how those attacks were carried out. The hero must then master the weakness and change, or else go under (PDF p. 117).

**Its appeal and history.** Audiences love it because surprise is a basic pleasure. Truby also calls it the **big plot**, both for the number of surprises and because they tend to shock. It is still strong in detective stories and thrillers, but its peak was the nineteenth century: Dickens, Dumas (*The Count of Monte Cristo* among others), and also Henry James's *Portrait of a Lady*, where very powerful villains win through negative plots (PDF p. 117).

**Dickens's combination.** Truby rates Dickens perhaps the greatest master of the reveals plot, partly because he often fused it with the journey plot. That takes great skill because the two are near opposites: the journey shows a broad cross-section of society but drops each person quickly; the reveals plot shows a few people and lets us know them well (PDF p. 117).

### 5.4 Antiplot (pp. 117-118)

**What it is.** Where nineteenth-century fiction went for superplot, much serious twentieth-century work went for antiplot: *Waiting for Godot*, *Ulysses*, *The Catcher in the Rye*, *L'Avventura*, *The Cherry Orchard*, *Last Year at Marienbad*. These treat plot almost with contempt, like a conjuring trick the writer must perform before getting to the real work of character. Truby cites Northrop Frye's remark (note 4) that once readers know how a story turns out they tend to forget its continuity, the very thing that drew them in (PDF p. 117).

**What those plots look like in summary.** *Catcher*: a teenager wanders New York for a couple of days. *The Cherry Orchard*: a family comes back to its estate, waits out the auction, and goes. *L'Avventura*: a detective story where there may have been no crime and nothing gets solved (PDF p. 117).

**Truby's reading.** He suspects these writers were rebelling against *big* plot, the sensational reveals that flatten everything else, rather than plot itself. So "antiplot" is really a set of techniques for making plot organic by having it express the finer shades of character: point of view, changing narrators, branching structure and nonchronological time. Each plays with plot by altering the manner of telling, aiming at a more complex picture of a person (PDF pp. 117-118).

**Fragmented is not the same as inorganic.** Multiple viewpoints can convey collage, montage and a character's dislocation, but also energy and a rush of sensation. If those experiences develop the character, and deepen the audience's grasp of that person, they count as organic and they satisfy (PDF p. 118).

**Digressions.** Digressions, common in antiplot, are a kind of simultaneous (at times backward) action, and they are organic only when they spring from the character's nature. *Tristram Shandy* is often faulted for endless digressions, but Truby reads it the other way round: it is a book of digressions that a main plotline occasionally interrupts, and since its hero is a man who digresses, the telling fits him exactly (PDF p. 118).

**Backward storytelling.** A form of antiplot in which scenes run in reverse order, as in Harold Pinter's *Betrayal*. Normally the causal thread between scenes stays hidden because one scene seems to follow the last naturally; running them backward forces the audience to notice that each event had to grow out of the ones before it. So it actually spotlights the organic unfolding (PDF p. 118).

### 5.5 Genre plot (p. 118)

**What it is.** While serious writers shrank plot, popular movies and novels enlarged it through genre. Genres are story types whose characters, themes, worlds, symbols and plots come largely preset. Their plots tend to be big, with reveals stunning enough to turn the story upside down (PDF p. 118).

**Its limits.** Because the plot is predetermined, the audience broadly knows what will happen, and only the details can surprise. Genre plots feel organically tied to their heroes only because they've been rewritten so often that all the padding is gone, but they miss a major requirement of organic plotting: they aren't unique to their main character. Being generic, they are mechanical. In farce and the caper (heist) story this goes so far that the plot has a fine watch's precision and no character whatsoever (PDF p. 118).

### 5.6 The multistrand plot (p. 118)

**What it is.** The newest strategy, invented by novelists and screenwriters but flowering in TV drama from *Hill Street Blues* on. Each episode has three to five major strands, each driven by a different member of one group, usually an institution such as a police station, hospital or law firm, and the writer cuts back and forth among them (PDF p. 118).

**Done badly / done well.** Done badly, the strands are unrelated and the cutting only keeps the audience alert and speeds things up. Done well, every strand varies one theme, and cutting from one to another produces a jolt of recognition when two scenes sit side by side (PDF p. 118).

**Can it be organic?** It is a much more simultaneous form, focused on the group or mini-society and on comparing its members. It can still be organic: it just moves the unit that develops from one hero to a group. If the strands vary a single theme, the audience grasps something about what humans are, which can move them as much as watching one person grow (PDF p. 118).

## 6. Creating an organic plot: the sequence (pp. 118-119)

Truby then asks how to build an organic plot for a given set of characters and gives a sequence (PDF p. 118). As printed it has three items, numbered 4-6 (⚠️ earlier items appear lost; Writing Exercise 7 begins by checking the plot against the designing principle, the theme and the story symbol, which may be what they covered; unverified):
- **Decide whether to use a storyteller.** This strongly shapes how the audience learns what happens, and so how the plot is designed.
- **Work out the structure in detail with the twenty-two steps.** This supplies most of the plot beats (he glosses beats as major actions or events) and is the best available guarantee that the plot is organic.
- **Decide whether to use genre (one or several).** If you do, add each genre's own beats in the right places and twist them so the plot doesn't become predictable.

The storyteller decision comes first in practice, but Truby explains the tools in reverse order (the twenty-two steps first, then the storyteller, then genre) because that order is easier to follow (PDF pp. 118-119).

### 7.2 Not a formula

Truby expects the objection that a fixed list will cramp creativity and turn out mechanical stories. He sees this as part of a wider fear of over-planning, which pushes writers to improvise as they go and end in a mess. The steps steer between the two extremes: they are scaffolding that lets a writer try something genuinely creative while knowing it will hold up as the story unfolds (PDF p. 119).

### 7.3 The number is not fixed: the accordion

Don't fixate on "twenty-two." A story can have more steps or fewer, according to its type and length. He likens a story to an accordion: it can stretch a long way, but it can only shrink so far. The floor is the **seven key steps** (ch. 3), the fewest an organic story can have; even a good thirty-second commercial hits all seven (PDF p. 119).
- **Short story, sitcom:** only room for the seven.
- **Feature film, short novel, one-hour TV drama:** usually at least twenty-two. In a multistrand drama, each strand hits the seven instead.
- **Long novel:** many more than twenty-two, because of the extra twists. *David Copperfield* has over sixty revelations.

### 7.4 What the steps combine

Looked at closely, the twenty-two steps fuse several of the story's systems into one plotline: character web, moral argument, story world, and the actual chain of events. They choreograph hero against opponents as the hero chases a goal and tries to solve a deeper problem in his life, and so they make sure the main character drives the plot (PDF p. 119).

**The four threads.** Truby's table (on p. 120 in this PDF; the text calls it "page 270") sorts the steps into four subsystems: character, plot, story world and moral argument (see the last column of the table in 7.1). A step can belong to more than one. His example is the drive: mainly a plot step (the hero's actions toward the goal), but also part of the moral argument, since the hero may act immorally to win (PDF pp. 119-120).

**The seven inside the twenty-two.** By name, the seven key steps of ch. 3 sit at steps 3 (weakness and need), 5 (desire), 7 (opponent), 10 (plan), 19 (battle), 20 (self-revelation) and 22 (new equilibrium). Truby says the twenty-two-step breakdown adds the crucial plot detail to the seven steps the writer has already worked out (PDF p. 140). For the seven steps themselves see `03 - The Seven Key Steps.md`.

### 7.7 Two closing cautions (p. 134)

The steps give great power for building a detailed, organic plot, but they take a lot of practice, so Truby advises applying them to everything you write and read, with two opposite cautions in mind (PDF p. 134):
- **Be flexible.** The order isn't fixed and the steps aren't a mold to force a story into. They follow the general order in which people try to solve problems in life, but no two problems, and no two stories, are alike. Treat them as a framework for these characters working through their particular problem.
- **Be wary of breaking the order.** For the same reason (they mirror how people actually solve problems), they form an organic order, the growth of a single unit. Rearranging them too drastically to seem original or surprising risks a story that feels fake or contrived.

## 8. The revelations sequence (pp. 134-136)

**Why isolate it.** Good writers know that plot turns on its revelations, so Truby recommends lifting the reveals out and studying them as one unit. For him, tracking this sequence ranks among the most useful things a storyteller can do (PDF p. 134).

**Does it build?** The test is whether the sequence builds properly (PDF p. 134):
- **Logical:** reveals arrive in the order the hero would most plausibly discover them.
- **Rising intensity:** ideally each is stronger than the last. In long stories that isn't always possible, and forcing it can defy logic, but there should be an overall rise so the drama grows.
- **Quickening pace:** they come faster toward the end, so the audience is hit by surprise more densely.

**The reversal.** The strongest kind of reveal turns the audience's understanding of the whole story upside down: suddenly every plot element looks different, and reality changes in a moment. It is most common in detective stories and thrillers (PDF p. 134).
- *The Sixth Sense*: the audience learns that the character Bruce Willis plays died before most of the film took place.
- *The Usual Suspects*: the audience learns that meek Verbal invented the whole tale and is himself the feared Keyser Soze.
- In both, the reversal comes at the very end, sending the audience out on a knockout blow, which Truby sees as the biggest reason they were hits.

**Caution.** A reversal can shrink a story into a plot-delivery device, and few stories can stand being dominated by plot. O. Henry won fame with reversals ("The Gift of the Magi" is one) but was also faulted as forced, gimmicky and mechanical (PDF p. 134).

**Examples of revelation sequences.** Each is set out as a chain: revelation, then decision, then the change in desire and in motive, with obsessive drive and audience revelation where they occur.
- *Alien* (O'Bannon and Shusett): six revelations, running from the crew discovering that the creature moves through the air vents, through the computer's disclosure that the crew is expendable and the discovery that Ash is a robot protecting the creature, to the final discovery that the creature is hiding in the escape shuttle. There is no separate audience revelation: the alien stays an unknown force, the audience learns things when Ripley does, and that lack of superiority raises their fear. The last reveal is the classic horror move: the place you flee to is the most dangerous of all (PDF pp. 134-135).
- *Basic Instinct* (Eszterhas): six revelations, each leading Nick toward a different suspect. Truby's point: in the detective thriller the revelations grow bigger and come closer to home (PDF p. 135).
- Borges's short story about a traitor and a hero ("Theme of the Traitor and the Hero"): a very short story built almost entirely from revelations. Borges rarely lets reveals crowd out character, symbol, world or theme; because his work treats learning as an escape from a maze at once private and cosmic, his reveals carry strong thematic force. Nine revelations lead the narrator, Ryan, to discover that his heroic ancestor was the traitor, that the assassination was staged with borrowed Shakespearean elements, and that he himself has become part of that old plan. The closing audience revelation is that Ryan hides what he found and publishes a book that glorifies the ancestor (PDF pp. 135-136).

## 9. The storyteller (pp. 136-140)

### 9.1 Why it belongs in the plot chapter

Choosing whether to have a storyteller ranks among the biggest decisions a writer makes. Truby discusses it under plot because a storyteller can completely change how the plot is sequenced, though in an organic story it affects characterization just as much. It is also badly misused, because most writers don't understand what it implies or what it is good for (PDF p. 136).

### 9.2 The default: no recognizable storyteller

Most popular movies, novels and plays have no identifiable teller. They are linear stories from an omniscient teller whom the audience neither knows nor cares about, and they tend to move fast, with one strong desire line and a big plot (PDF p. 136).

### 9.3 What a storyteller is and implies

**Definition.** A narrator who recounts what a character did, speaking in first person (about himself) or third person (about another). A recognizable teller adds complexity and subtlety: you get the hero's actions *plus* commentary on them (PDF p. 136).

**Implications** (PDF pp. 136-137):
- **Why this teller, why now?** Once a teller is named, the audience wonders why this person is telling this story and why it has to be retold in front of them now. The teller draws attention to himself and at first can distance the audience, which gives the writer detachment.
- **Voice.** "Voice" is a much-used word; Truby says it really means putting the audience inside the teller's mind as he speaks, shown precisely through his choice of subject and his manner of speaking. That mind belongs to a real person, complete with biases, blind spots and lies he may not notice. Whatever truth comes out is subjective, not the word of God, and pushed to the limit a teller can blur or erase the boundary separating what is real from what is illusion.
- **Memory.** A teller is recounting the past, so memory enters, bringing loss, sadness and a sense of what might have been. The story also feels complete, and the teller, speaking from after the end, may seem a little wiser.
- **The "trust me" move.** Some writers combine direct address with memory to make the account seem *more* reliable ("I was there; here's what really happened"). Truby reads this as an unspoken invitation to distrust the teller and to make truth itself a question in the story.

**Advantages** (PDF p. 137): an intimate link between character and audience; subtler characterization and sharper distinctions between people; and often a move from the hero as doer (typically a fighter) to the hero as maker (an artist). Telling becomes the central act, so the road to "immortality" runs through telling the deed rather than doing it.

**Freedom in plotting** (PDF p. 137): because the events are framed by someone's memory, the writer can drop chronology and order events however makes the best structural sense. A teller also helps stitch together events spread across wide spans of time and space, or a journey; plots like these tend to feel fragmented, but inside a remembering teller's frame they gain unity and the big gaps between events seem to close.

### 9.4 What to avoid: the empty frame

The teller who opens with "let me tell you a story," narrates events in order and closes with "and that's what happened" is, for Truby, worse than useless. It draws attention to the teller for no reason, uses none of the technique's strengths, and seems to exist only to announce that the story is being told "artistically" (PDF p. 137).

### 9.5 Thirteen storyteller techniques (pp. 137-140)

These work because they arise from the very shape of someone compelled to tell and a tale that demands telling. Don't use the whole set together; choose the ones that suit the story (PDF p. 137).

1. **The teller is most likely your real main character.** In first- or third-person narration alike, nine times in ten the teller is the real hero. The reason is structural: telling the story splits the self-revelation step in two. At the start the teller looks back to understand how certain actions (his own, earlier, or someone else's) have affected him; in recounting them he sees an outside model of action and reaches an insight that changes his life now (PDF p. 137).
2. **Introduce the teller in a dramatic situation**, such as just after a fight or just before a big decision. This puts the teller inside the story, creates suspense about him and gives his tale a running start. Examples: *Sunset Boulevard* (the narrator has just been shot dead by his lover), *Body and Soul* (a boxer about to throw a title fight), *The Usual Suspects* (possibly the only survivor of a massacre, under interrogation) (PDF p. 137).
3. **Give the telling a good trigger.** The teller should be driven by a problem in the present that explains why he must tell this story right now. Examples: *Body and Soul* (before the bout he must understand what brought him to this point), *The Usual Suspects* (he is threatened with a contract on his life if he stays silent), *How Green Was My Valley* (driven from his home valley, he must understand why before leaving) (PDF p. 137).
4. **The teller shouldn't know everything at the start.** An all-knowing teller has no dramatic life in the present and becomes a dead frame. Give him a serious weakness that telling the story will resolve, and make recalling and telling a struggle, so that the telling itself becomes heroic. Examples: *Cinema Paradiso* (a rich, famous, despairing man who has never truly loved, called home by a friend's death), *The Shawshank Redemption* (Red, hopeless and dependent on prison walls, whose account starts with Andy's arrival), *Heart of Darkness* (a detective story whose "crime" is never known; Marlow's lie to Kurtz's fiancée may be why he is driven to retell the tale endlessly) (PDF pp. 137-138).
5. **Find an unusual structure rather than plain chronology.** Otherwise the teller is just a frame. A singular way of telling justifies the teller. Examples: *It's a Wonderful Life* (angels recount a man's life, then show him a world without him), *The Usual Suspects* (cutting between interrogation and recounted events, with the final discovery that the "past" was invented in the room) (PDF p. 138).
6. **Let the teller try different versions as he struggles toward the truth.** The story isn't fixed in advance; it is a dramatic argument between writer and audience, and the audience's silent questioning should partly shape it. The teller leaves gaps for the audience to fill, and through his struggle both he and they reach deeper meaning. Examples: *Heart of Darkness* (three nested narrators make the "true" story unreachable; Marlow keeps retelling and keeps failing), *Tristram Shandy* (the comic version: the narrator goes backward and forward, scolds the reader, and complains about explaining things too early) (PDF p. 138).
7. **Close the storytelling frame roughly three-quarters through, not at the end.** If the frame only closes at the end, remembering and telling cannot affect the present, dramatically or structurally; there has to be room left for the telling to change the teller. Examples: *It's a Wonderful Life* (the recounting stops with about a third left, and in the last third the angel shows George another possible world and helps him change), *Cinema Paradiso* (the memory stops as the hero departs for Rome; in the present he goes home for the funeral and receives Alfredo's gift) (PDF pp. 138-139).
8. **The telling should bring the teller to a self-revelation.** Looking back gives him an important insight about himself now; the whole storytelling process is structurally one large self-revelation step, and telling is how the teller-hero meets his need. Examples: *The Great Gatsby* (Nick's closing reflection on belonging to the Midwest), *The Shawshank Redemption* (Red learns hope), *Goodfellas* (a black comedy that uses its first-person teller to stress, ironically, that he gets no self-revelation though he plainly should) (PDF p. 139).
9. **Consider showing how the act of telling can do harm or be wrong**, to the teller or others. Storytelling then becomes a moral question carrying dramatic weight now. Example: *Copenhagen*, a contest among three tellers whose rival versions of a wartime meeting each carry a moral view and are used to attack one another (PDF p. 139).
10. **The telling should trigger a last dramatic event,** most often the hero's moral decision, made on the strength of the self-revelation. Examples: *The Great Gatsby* (Nick leaves New York for home), *It's a Wonderful Life* (George chooses to live and face his troubles), *Body and Soul* (the boxer chooses to fight honestly), *The Shawshank Redemption* (Red refuses to give up as Brooks did and goes to join Andy) (PDF p. 139).
11. **Don't push the false idea that someone's death finally lets the whole true story come out** (printed "77" in this PDF). A common trigger has the teller claim that someone's death finally allows the truth, with the deathbed words as the last piece. Truby calls this false: dying does not let you see your life whole; behaving as though death is coming is what makes meaning, because it pushes you to choose now, and the search for meaning continues as long as you live. Meaning comes from telling and retelling, and with every pass the "true" story shifts; like Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, the teller can hold *a* meaning at a moment but never *the* meaning. Examples: *Citizen Kane* ("Rosebud" matters because it cannot sum up Kane's life), *Heart of Darkness* (Kurtz's last words deepen the mystery rather than solve it) (PDF p. 139).
12. **Make the deeper theme the truth and beauty of making things (creativity), rather than heroic deeds.** With all action inside the frame and the teller's struggle in the foreground, storytelling becomes the main action and the great achievement. Examples: *The Usual Suspects* (Verbal's greatest feat is the tale that convinces everyone of his weakness), *Gilgamesh* (the warrior who seeks immortality and is left only with the immortality of being told), *The Shawshank Redemption* (Andy's gift is teaching the others to live hopefully, stylishly and freely, prison or not) (PDF pp. 139-140).
13. **Beware of too many storytellers.** The teller's chief cost is that he puts a frame between story and audience, which usually drains some emotion. The more tellers, the greater the risk the audience watches from a cold, clinical distance (PDF p. 140).

**Models.** Truby names about twenty works that use a teller especially well, among them *American Beauty*, *Madame Bovary*, *Sunset Boulevard*, *Presumed Innocent*, *Goodfellas*, *The Conformist*, *The Magnificent Ambersons*, *Forrest Gump*, *Citizen Kane* and *The Great Gatsby* (PDF p. 140).

## 10. Genres (p. 140)

**Genre as the next structural element.** Truby defines a genre as a story form: a specific kind of story. Most movies, novels and plays rest on at least one and usually mix two or three, so the writer must know which form, if any, is in play. Each genre has plot beats the audience expects; leave them out and the audience is let down (PDF p. 140).

**Genres as subsystems.** Every genre carries out the universal structure steps (the seven and the twenty-two) in its own way. A great story needs no genre at all, but a writer who uses one has to learn how that form handles the structure steps, character, theme, world and symbol, and then use those elements originally, so the story differs from every other in the form while sharing much with them. Genre audiences want the familiar skeleton with a new skin (PDF p. 140).

**Scope.** The genres themselves are outside this book (Truby says he has written about them at length elsewhere). They are complex; aim to master one or two, which practice makes possible (PDF p. 140).

## 11. Worked example: *The Godfather* in twenty-two steps (pp. 140-142)

Truby closes with a full breakdown of *The Godfather* (Puzo; screenplay by Puzo and Coppola) to show the twenty-two steps adding plot detail to the seven key steps (PDF p. 140). Each step's file summarizes its entry. Features worth noting at chapter level:
- **No self-revelation for the hero.** Michael never sees what he has become; the writers give the moral self-revelation to his wife, Kay, as the door shuts on her, which Truby calls an advanced structural technique (PDF pp. 140, 142).
- **A ghost that is not an event** but an inherited family tradition of crime the hero despises (PDF p. 140).
- **Many fake-ally opponents,** which greatly enlarges the plot (PDF p. 141).
- **A two-blow apparent defeat** (his brother's murder, then his wife killed by a car bomb intended for him) (PDF p. 142).
- **No gate or gauntlet,** because Michael fights so well that he fools even the audience; his visit to death is the car bomb (PDF p. 142).
- **The moral decision placed just before the battle** rather than after it (PDF p. 142).
- **A new equilibrium that is a fall:** he gains power and loses his soul (PDF p. 142).
- **The rest of the breakdown on p. 142, in brief:** Kay, as ally, objects to his joining the family business, and he answers that it will be legitimate within five years (step 13); seeing the car bomb, he turns obsessed with avenging his wife and brother (step 15); the audience alone watches the Don's most dangerous enforcer being killed (step 16); learning that Tessio has switched sides and Barzini means to kill him, he resolves to strike first (step 17); and the battle intercuts the baptism, where he renounces Satan, with the simultaneous killing of his rivals (step 19) (PDF p. 142).

## Exercises the chapter gives the writer

**Writing Exercise 7, "Creating Your Plot"** (PDF p. 140) asks the writer to:
- check that the plot follows the story's designing principle and theme;
- if there is a story symbol, make sure the plot expresses it;
- decide whether there will be a storyteller, and which type, keeping the storyteller techniques in mind;
- write out the story's twenty-two steps in detail, starting from step 1 (the frame) so the others fall into place;
- list the reveals on their own and test them: is the order logical, does each top the last, does each change the hero's original desire in some way, and do they come faster toward the end?

**The framing questions in step 1** (PDF p. 121) are also an exercise in effect: what the hero learns at the end, what he believes at the start, and what he is wrong about at the start (see `22 Steps/01 - ...`). The opponent questions in step 7 (PDF p. 125) work the same way.

## Terms introduced

- **Plot (Truby's sense):** weaving lines of action beneath the surface so the story builds from start to end; the dance of hero and opponents over one goal; what happens plus how it is revealed.
- **Organic plot:** a plot whose events are causal, essential, proportionate, grown from the main character, unified, and tied to the hero's change (or his failure to change).
- **Two legs of story:** taking action and learning information.
- **Journey plot:** the hero travels, meets and beats opponents in sequence, and returns home.
- **Funnel effect:** in *Tom Jones*, sending early characters toward the hero's destination so they keep meeting him.
- **Three unities plot:** a plot confined to one day, one place and one line of action.
- **Reveals plot / big plot:** few, well-known opponents with much hidden about them, producing many (often shocking) surprises.
- **Antiplot:** twentieth-century techniques (viewpoint, shifting narrators, branching, nonchronological time, digression, backward telling) that play with how a story is told in order to show character more subtly.
- **Backward storytelling:** scenes in reverse chronological order, which exposes the causal thread.
- **Genre plot:** a predetermined plot belonging to a story type; big but generic.
- **Multistrand plot:** three to five crosscut strands driven by members of one group, ideally variations on a theme.
- **Twenty-two building blocks / twenty-two steps:** Truby's detailed structure sequence (index in 7.1).
- **Accordion:** his image for how the number of steps expands with length but cannot shrink below seven.
- **Plot beat:** a major action or event.
- **Revelation / reveal:** new, surprising information that turns the plot.
- **Revelations sequence:** the reveals pulled out and studied as one unit; it should be logical, build in intensity and quicken.
- **Reversal (reversal reveal):** a reveal that overturns the audience's understanding of the whole story.
- **Storyteller:** a recognizable narrator recounting actions in first or third person, with commentary.
- **Simple frame:** a storyteller who only opens and closes a chronological tale; Truby's main thing to avoid.
- **Genre (as subsystem):** a story form that carries out the universal steps in its own way, with expected beats.
- **Step-level techniques** (defined in the step files): community start, running start, slow start; levels of desire; subplot; iceberg opponent; added revelations; training; apparent victory; double reversal; thematic revelation.
