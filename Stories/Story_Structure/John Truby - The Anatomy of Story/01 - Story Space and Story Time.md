# Story Space and Story Time (Truby, chapter 1, with the book's opening)

**Source:** John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story: 22 Steps to Becoming a Master Storyteller* (2007), chapter 1 "Story Space, Story Time," PDF pp. 4-11. The book has no separate introduction: the chapter opens with Truby's case against the usual story terms (pp. 4-5), which serves as the book's introduction, so it is summarized first below.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## Part A. The introduction's key ideas (PDF pp. 4-5)

### Telling a story is easy; telling a great one is hard
Everyone tells stories daily, in casual forms like an anecdote from work or a bar joke. The difficulty begins when a writer wants to tell a great story, perhaps professionally. Two demands stack up: the writer needs a deep, exact grasp of how and why people live as they do, which Truby calls the largest and most complicated subject there is, and then has to turn that grasp into a story. He names the second demand as the hardest part for most writers (Truby, PDF p. 4).

### Obstacle 1: the classic vocabulary is too vague to use
Inherited labels such as climax, denouement, rising action and progressive complication, which reach back to Aristotle, are so general that Truby calls them nearly meaningless for working writers. His test case: a hero dangling from a ledge seconds from death. Is that scene a rising action, a complication, a denouement or an opening? It could be any of them or none, and the label tells you neither how to write the scene nor whether it belongs in the story (Truby, PDF p. 4).

### Obstacle 2: Aristotle is too narrow and too theoretical
Truby ranks Aristotle above every other philosopher, yet argues that the *Poetics* treats a small set of plots and genres and is hard to turn into practice. Writers who try to learn working technique from it come away with little (Truby, PDF p. 4).

### Obstacle 3: three-act structure is simplistic and partly wrong
Screenwriters usually move from Aristotle to three-act theory: a beginning of about thirty pages, a middle of about sixty and an end of about thirty, with two or three "plot points" that are never clearly defined. Truby's objections (Truby, PDF p. 4):
- It is even less useful in practice than the *Poetics*.
- It is **mechanical.** The act break comes from stage practice, where a curtain closes to mark an act's end. Film, novels, short stories and many modern plays don't need that device.
- Act breaks are **external.** They are imposed from outside and ignore the story's own inner logic, that is, the direction it ought to take.
- A mechanical view leads to **episodic** stories: events sit side by side like parts in a box instead of connecting and building from start to finish, so the audience is moved only now and then, if at all.

### Obstacle 4: a mechanical writing process
Many writers, screenwriters above all, chase what they think will sell. They take a small variation on a film they saw recently, pick a genre (detective, love story, action), and plug in the stock characters and beats that come with it. The result is generic and formulaic, neither popular nor good (Truby, PDF pp. 4-5).

### What the book promises instead
Truby offers a "practical poetics" that works across screenplays, novels, plays, teleplays and short stories (Truby, PDF p. 5). It has three aims:
1. Demonstrate that great stories are **organic**: living, developing bodies rather than machines.
2. Handle storytelling as an **exacting craft** built on precise methods, whatever the medium or genre.
3. Use a writing process that is itself organic, growing characters and plot out of the writer's original idea.

### The central tension: craft versus organic feel
Aims 1 and 2 pull against each other. A story is built from hundreds or thousands of parts using many techniques, yet the audience must experience it as one thing that grows toward a climax. The writer has to master technique so thoroughly that the characters seem to act on their own, even though the writer is making them act. Truby compares this to a great athlete whose technique has vanished from view so that the audience sees only grace (Truby, PDF p. 5).

## Part B. The chapter's concepts (PDF pp. 5-11)

### The single-sentence definition
Truby's barest definition of story: someone recounts to an audience what a person did to obtain what he desired, and the reasons behind it. Three distinct parts are present: whoever tells, whoever hears, and the tale itself (Truby, PDF p. 5).

### The teller as a player
First of all, the teller is a player. A story is a word game between author and audience, and the only ones keeping score are the studios, networks and publishers. The teller invents people and deeds and reports a chain of actions that have, in some sense, finished. Even when the form is in the present tense, as in plays and screenplays, the teller is in effect summarizing everything, so the listener receives it as one whole (Truby, PDF p. 5).

### Selecting and building intense moments
Telling a story is not just inventing or recalling events, because events on their own only describe. The teller chooses, links and builds a chain of intense moments, so charged that the listener seems to go through them himself. Good storytelling doesn't merely report the events of a life; it lets the audience experience that life, boiled down to its essential thoughts and events but delivered so freshly that it feels like part of the audience's own life (Truby, PDF p. 5).

### Stories give emotional knowledge
Because the audience relives the events as if now, they come to understand what pressures, choices and feelings pushed the character to act as he did. Truby calls this a form of **emotional knowledge**, close to what was once called wisdom, delivered in a playful, entertaining way (Truby, PDF p. 5).

### The story as a puzzle: telling and withholding
The teller builds a puzzle about people and asks the listener to solve it. He does it in two ways: by telling certain information about an invented character and by **withholding** other information. Withholding is essential because it makes the audience work out who the character is and what he is doing, which pulls them into the story. When nothing remains for them to work out, they are no longer an audience, and the story ends (Truby, PDF p. 5).

### Feeling and thinking
Audiences enjoy both halves of a story: the feeling half (reliving a life) and the thinking half (solving the puzzle). Every good story has both, though some forms lean hard toward one end, from tearful melodrama at one pole to the purely intellectual whodunit at the other (Truby, PDF p. 5).

### The dramatic code
**Key point:** whatever else it does, every story communicates the **dramatic code** (Truby, PDF p. 6). This code, buried deep in the human mind, is an artistic account of how people can develop. It runs as a process beneath every story. The teller disguises it under particular characters and deeds, yet growth is the thing the audience ultimately carries away from a good story.

### Desire drives change
Within this code, desire is the fuel of change. Truby rewrites Descartes for the story world as "I want, therefore I am" (Truby, PDF p. 6). Desire moves every conscious living thing and gives it direction. A story tracks a person's want, the lengths he goes to for it, and the price he pays.

### The two legs of story: acting and learning
Once a character wants something, the story moves on two "legs" (Truby, PDF p. 6):
- **Acting:** the character takes steps to get what he wants.
- **Learning:** the character discovers more effective routes to the goal.

Each discovery leads to a decision and a change of course. Every story walks this way, but some forms favor one leg. Action is foremost in myth and in its modern descendant, the action film. Learning is foremost in detective fiction and in drama told from many viewpoints (Truby, PDF p. 6).

### Struggle forces change
A character who pursues a desire and is blocked has to struggle, or the story ends. The struggle changes him. So the code's ultimate purpose, and the teller's, is to depict a character's change, or to explain why it failed to happen (Truby, PDF p. 6).

### How each medium frames change
Truby sketches how different story forms frame human change (Truby, PDF p. 6):
- **Myth** spans the broadest arc: a whole life, and a rise from beast to god.
- **Plays** usually zero in on the instant the lead character decides.
- **Film,** American film above all, shows a modest change that comes from pursuing a narrow goal with great force.
- **Classic short stories** follow a few events that bring the character to one important insight.
- **Serious novels** show a person interacting with and changing inside a whole society, or trace the exact mental and emotional steps that lead to his change.
- **TV drama** follows several characters in a small community, each straining to change at the same time.

### Drama as a code of maturity
Drama centers on the **moment of change**, when someone escapes old habits, weaknesses and ghosts and becomes a richer, fuller self. The dramatic code carries the idea that people can become psychologically and morally better versions of themselves, and Truby says that is why audiences love it (Truby, PDF p. 6).

### Story world versus the real world
**Key point:** what a story presents is not reality but the **story world**: not a transcript of life but life as people imagine it might be, compressed and intensified to help the audience see how life actually works (Truby, PDF p. 6).

### The story body
A great story depicts people undergoing an organic process, and it is itself an organism. Even a simple children's story has many subsystems that connect and feed one another, the way a human body has nervous, circulatory and skeletal systems. Truby lists the story's subsystems as characters, plot, the revelations sequence, story world, moral argument, symbol web, scene weave and symphonic dialogue, each treated in a later chapter (Truby, PDF p. 6). He maps some of them onto body parts:

| Story subsystem | Body part |
|---|---|
| Theme (his "moral argument") | brain |
| Character | heart and circulation |
| Revelations | nervous system |
| Story structure | skeleton |
| Scenes | skin |

### Every element is defined by its web
**Key point:** each subsystem is a web of elements that define and set one another apart. No single element, the hero included, can succeed unless it is created and defined against every other element (Truby, PDF p. 6).

### Story movement: five patterns from nature
To show how an organic story moves through time, Truby borrows from nature, which links elements in sequence through a few basic patterns: **linear, meandering, spiral, branching, explosive** (Truby, PDF p. 7; he footnotes a source on natural patterns). Storytellers use the same patterns alone or mixed. Linear and explosive are the two extremes: linear is one thing after another on a straight path, explosive is everything at once. Meandering, spiral and branching are blends of the two.

**Truby's drawings** (PDF pp. 7-9, images not in the text file): a cloud of unconnected dots stands for the story elements waiting to be linked in time; linear is a single straight arrow; meandering is a snaking line; spiral appears as a flat coil and as a cone winding down to a point; branching is drawn as a stem with side shoots and as a tree splitting into ever-smaller twigs; explosive is a starburst of lines radiating from one center.

#### Linear story
Follows one main character from start to finish, and suggests that events are explained by history or biology. Most Hollywood films are linear: one hero pursues one desire with great intensity, and the audience watches how he goes after it and is changed by it (Truby, PDF p. 7).

#### Meandering story
Winds along with no obvious direction, like a river, a snake or the folds of the brain. The hero wants something, though not fiercely. He covers a lot of ground haphazardly and meets characters from many levels of society (Truby, PDF pp. 7-8). Examples: the *Odyssey*; Dickens, for example *David Copperfield*; and comic journeys such as *Don Quixote*, *Huckleberry Finn*, *Tom Jones*, *Flirting with Disaster* and *Little Big Man*.

#### Spiral story
A path that circles inward toward a center, as in cyclones, horns and seashells. A character circles back again and again to a single event or memory, digging further into it each time. Thrillers favor it: *Vertigo*, *Blow-Up*, *The Conversation*, *Memento* (Truby, PDF p. 8).

#### Branching story
Paths spread from a few central points, splitting into smaller and smaller parts, as in trees, leaves and river systems. In a story, each branch usually depicts an entire society, or one fully developed phase of a single society, that the hero passes through. Truby places it in more advanced fiction: social fantasies (*Gulliver's Travels*, *It's a Wonderful Life*) and multiple-hero stories (*Nashville*, *American Graffiti*, *Traffic*) (Truby, PDF pp. 8-9).

#### Explosive story (the appearance of simultaneity)
Many paths extend at once, as in volcanoes and dandelions. Strictly speaking, no story is explosive, because a teller must present one thing after another, even within a scene. A story can only create the *appearance* of simultaneous action; film does it with the crosscut (Truby, PDF p. 9).
- Stories built on apparent simultaneity suggest a **comparative** explanation of events: seeing many elements side by side lets the audience pick out the central idea in each.
- They put more weight on exploring the story world: how the elements connect and how each person fits, or doesn't fit, in the whole.
- They usually branch. Each mixes linear and simultaneous telling differently, but each stresses characters coexisting in a world over one character developing from start to end (Truby, PDF pp. 9-10).
- Examples (film): *Nashville*, *Traffic*, *Crash*, *Syriana*, *Pulp Fiction*, *American Graffiti*, *L.A. Confidential*, *Hannah and Her Sisters*, *Last Year at Marienbad*. (Literature): *Ulysses*, *Ragtime*, *Tristram Shandy*, *The Canterbury Tales*.

### The common writing process, and why it fails
Truby says most writers choose the easiest process rather than the best, and he labels it with four adjectives: **external, mechanical, piecemeal, generic** (Truby, PDF p. 10). His portrait of the typical writer:
- **Premise:** generic, a loose copy of an existing story or two stories glued together.
- **Hero:** nearly all attention goes here; the hero is "fleshed out" by piling on traits, and his change is saved for the last scene.
- **Opponent and minor characters:** kept apart from the hero and ranked below him, so they end up thin and blurry.
- **Theme:** avoided so no one can accuse the writer of preaching, or stated only in dialogue.
- **World:** whatever seems normal for the character, usually a big city because that's where the audience lives.
- **Symbols:** skipped as obvious or pretentious.
- **Plot and scenes:** driven only by asking what comes next, often sending the hero on a physical journey and dividing the story with the three-act template, which splits it into three pieces without linking events underneath. The plot comes out episodic, the writer complains of "second-act problems," and the story never builds to a climax that hits hard.
- **Dialogue:** only pushes the plot, with all conflict about the surface action; an ambitious writer has the hero state the theme outright near the end.

### Truby's alternative: internal, organic, interconnected, original
Against that, Truby's process is **internal, organic, interconnected and original** (Truby, PDF p. 10). He warns it is hard, but he holds that this method or something close to it is the only one that succeeds, and that anyone can learn it. Two principles:
- **Work in construction order.** The book takes the techniques in the same order in which a writer builds a story.
- **Build from the inside out,** which has two parts: the story must be personal to its writer, and the writer must locate and grow whatever is fresh in the idea. With each chapter the story grows more detailed, and every part connects to every other part.

### The book's writing sequence
The chapters follow the order of the process (Truby, PDF pp. 10-11):
1. **Premise:** the whole story in one sentence, which hints at the story's essence and is then developed to get the most from the idea.
2. **The seven key structure steps:** the principal stages in the story's growth, which are also the stages of the hidden dramatic code. Truby calls them the story's DNA, a stable foundation.
3. **Character:** characters drawn out of the original idea rather than out of thin air, each compared and connected to every other, then given a function in the hero's development.
4. **Theme (moral argument):** the writer's moral vision of how people should act, taken from what is already inherent in the idea and expressed through structure rather than through characters used as mouthpieces, so it surprises and moves.
5. **Story world:** built as an outgrowth of the hero, defining him and giving his growth a physical form.
6. **Symbol web:** symbols as packets of highly compressed meaning, arranged in a web that brings out aspects of character, world and plot.
7. **Plot:** the right story form found from the characters, and the plot grown from them. It uses the twenty-two steps (the seven key ones and fifteen others) so that every event connects under the surface and builds to an ending that is surprising yet logically necessary.
8. **Scene weave:** before drafting, a list of every scene with all plotlines and themes woven together.
9. **Scene construction and symphonic dialogue:** each scene written to advance the hero's development, and dialogue that blends many "instruments" and levels at once instead of merely pushing the plot.
