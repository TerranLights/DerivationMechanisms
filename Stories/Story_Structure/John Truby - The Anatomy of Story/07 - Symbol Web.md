## 1. Why symbols matter (p. 99)

Truby says many writers think of symbols as fussy details that only mattered in school literature classes, and he calls that a serious error. He asks writers to picture symbols instead as gems sewn into the fabric of the story, gems with real emotional force (Truby, PDF p. 99).

**What a symbol is.** For Truby, symbol works at small scale: a word or thing standing in for something beyond itself (a person, a place, an action, an object) that comes back again and again over the course of the story. Character, theme and plot are the big puzzles that trick and please viewers; symbol is the little puzzle doing its work far under the surface. It matters because it hands the writer a **hidden language** that moves the audience's feelings (Truby, PDF p. 99).

---

## 2. How symbols work (pp. 99-100)

**Concentrated meaning.** A symbol is a specially charged image that the audience values. Truby's analogy: as matter is energy in concentrated form, a symbol is meaning in concentrated form, the tightest **condenser-expander** among all storytelling tools (Truby, PDF p. 99).

**"Refer and repeat."** Truby's two-word guide. You begin with a feeling, invent a symbol that will produce it in the audience, and then bring the symbol back with small changes. His shorthand chain: your feeling leads to the symbol, which leads to a feeling in the audience; the altered symbol then produces a stronger audience feeling (Truby, PDF p. 99).

**Resonance.** Symbols affect people quietly but strongly. Every appearance sends out a resonance, like rings spreading on water; each repetition widens and deepens the echo in viewers' minds, frequently without their being aware of it (Truby, PDF p. 100).

---

## 3. The symbol web (p. 100)

**The worst error.** Truby echoes his character chapter, where he warned that treating a character as a lone individual is the fastest way to lose individuality. The matching error with symbols is treating each one as an isolated object (Truby, PDF p. 100).

**Key point.** Build a network of symbols, each helping define the others (Truby, PDF p. 100).

**How the symbol web differs from the other subsystems.** Each subsystem reveals something deeper about how life operates, but by a different route:
- the **character web** sets people side by side and shows their likenesses and differences;
- the **plot** works through a chain of events whose logic surprises yet convinces;
- the **symbol web** works by pointing from one object, person or action to another. When viewers draw that link, even partially or in passing, they see into the essential nature of both things (Truby, PDF p. 100).

*Examples:* when *The Philadelphia Story* likens Tracy Lord to a goddess, it brings out her grace and beauty, yet also her chill and her sense of being above others. Setting Lothlórien's calm woodland against Mordor's fiery mountains in Tolkien contrasts a nurturing fellowship of equals with a lethal tyranny. In *For Whom the Bell Tolls*, setting planes against horses captures a culture of impersonal machine power pushing aside a culture of personal honor, loyalty and chivalry (Truby, PDF p. 100).

**What can carry symbols.** Truby lists the whole story, its structure, characters, theme, world, actions, objects and dialogue, and the rest of the chapter takes these up in turn (Truby, PDF p. 100).

## 4. Story symbols (pp. 100-101)

**What they are.** At the level of the premise, one image can carry the story's main twists, its core theme or the shape of its structure, and unite them (Truby, PDF p. 100).

**Examples Truby gives, in brief (pp. 100-101):**
- The *Odyssey*: the title names the symbol, a long journey to be survived.
- Twain's *Huckleberry Finn*: the key symbol is the **raft** rather than the river trip, a flimsy floating island on which a white boy and an enslaved black man share life as equal friends.
- *Heart of Darkness*: the dark heart of the title is the jungle's innermost point, the physical, mental and moral terminus of Marlow's voyage.
- The superhero titles (*Batman*, *Spider-Man*, *Superman*): they name hybrid men with powers, and also hint at people split inside and set apart from society.
- Chekhov's orchard: lasting beauty that is also useless in practical terms, and so expendable in a changing world.
- Hawthorne's scarlet letter: first a mark forcing a woman to publicize her "sinful" love, later the emblem of a different morality built on genuine love.
- Joyce's *Portrait of the Artist*: the hero's surname points to the mythic maker of the labyrinth, and to the wings he made so he and Icarus could flee it; critics have read the novel's structure as a string of practice flights by which the young artist escapes his history and his homeland.
- *How Green Was My Valley*: the green valley versus the black pit, framing a life's emotional arc from nature, youth, innocence and family toward a sooty industrial world, ending in exile with the family broken.
- *One Flew over the Cuckoo's Nest*: the madhouse and the spirit that takes flight, which again sum up the plot of a fun-loving inmate stirring up a mental ward.
- *Network*: a TV company on the surface, and underneath a net that traps everyone tangled in it.
- *Alien*: the outsider as symbol; structurally, the frightening other that gets in.
- Proust: the madeleine, whose taste sets off the remembering that becomes the whole novel.
- *A Farewell to Arms*: the "farewell" is the hero's desertion, the story's central act.
- Salinger's catcher: the imaginary figure Holden wants to be, which shows his compassion and also his impossible wish to stop change (Truby, PDF pp. 100-101).

## 5. The symbol line (pp. 101-102)

**What it is.** Before threading symbols through the story, write one sentence tying together the web's main symbols. It should come out of the work already done on the designing principle, the story world and the theme line (Truby, PDF p. 101).

**Worked examples (pp. 101-102).** Truby revisits his eleven ch. 2 stories one last time and gives each a symbol line. In our paraphrase:

| Story | Gist of the symbol line |
|---|---|
| Moses (Exodus) | God's word made physical: the burning bush, the plagues, the stone tablets. |
| *Ulysses* | Modern counterparts of the *Odyssey*'s father, son and wife. |
| *Four Weddings and a Funeral* | Weddings set against a funeral. |
| *Harry Potter* | A magic kingdom shaped as a school. |
| *The Sting* | How the mark is conned. |
| *Long Day's Journey into Night* | Darkness deepening until one light shows in the dark. |
| *Meet Me in St. Louis* | The house transforming through the seasons. |
| *Copenhagen* | Heisenberg's uncertainty principle itself. |
| *A Christmas Carol* | Spirits of three different times bring about one man's Christmas rebirth. |
| *It's a Wonderful Life* | Small-town America across its history. |
| *Citizen Kane* | One life given physical form in objects such as the snow globe, Xanadu, the newsreel and the sled. |

(Truby, PDF pp. 101-102)

## 6. Symbolic characters (pp. 102-104)

**Two linked subsystems.** After the symbol line, Truby turns to character. Character and symbol are separate subsystems but not cut off from each other; symbols are excellent tools for defining characters and serving the story's larger aim (Truby, PDF p. 102).

**Choosing the symbol.** Choose a symbol for a character's **defining principle, or for its opposite**. Truby's example of the opposite: Steerforth in *David Copperfield*, whose name promises the steady, upright fellow he is not. Fixing one specific symbol to an essential trait gives viewers an instant read on that part of the character, plus a feeling they will tie to him from then on. Each slightly altered repetition sharpens the character while cementing the core trait and feeling in viewers' minds. Use it **sparingly**: the more symbols a character carries, the less each one strikes (Truby, PDF p. 102).

**Work from the web.** No character exists in isolation; each is defined by the others. When picking a symbol for one, think about symbols for several, starting with the hero and the main opponent, whose symbols should be opposed just as they are (Truby, PDF p. 102).

**Opposition inside one character.** Think about assigning a single character **two symbols that pull against each other**. You get a more complex person and still keep the power of symbol (Truby, PDF p. 102).

**Truby's process, in our summary (p. 102):** look over the entire character web first; start with the hero-opponent opposition; choose one trait of the character, or one emotion he should stir; consider an internal symbol opposition; repeat the symbol with that character many times; and alter its details each time (Truby, PDF p. 102).

**Shortcut: broad categories.** A powerful shorthand ties a character to a whole category, above all **gods, animals or machines**. Each category is a fundamental mode and rank of being, so attaching a character to one hands him a trait and a level the audience recognizes immediately. It can be used anywhere but turns up most in strongly metaphorical forms: myth, horror, fantasy, science fiction (Truby, PDF pp. 102-103).

### 6a. God symbolism (p. 103)
- Joyce's *Portrait of the Artist*: tying Stephen to Daedalus, the inventor whose wings carried him out of the labyrinth, makes Stephen airy and marks him as an artist fighting to get free. Joyce then adds an **internal opposition**: Stephen is also tied to Icarus, the son who flew too near the sun out of overreaching and fell, and to the labyrinth, in which Stephen is lost (Truby, PDF p. 103).
- *The Godfather*: Puzo chooses another face of God, a father-god who rules his domain and dispenses justice, but a vengeful one, a human with godlike, dictatorial power no person should hold. Puzo builds in an internal opposition by linking this god to the **devil**; merging holy and unholy is basic to the character and to the whole film (Truby, PDF p. 103).
- *The Philadelphia Story*: Philip Barry ties Tracy Lord to aristocracy and to the idea of a goddess; her surname sounds "lordly," and her father and ex-husband both call her a bronze goddess. The link lowers her and lifts her at once. The plot hinges on whether she gives way to the goddess's worst side (icy, proud, unforgiving) or rises to its best, a large spirit that ironically lets her become fully human and forgiving (Truby, PDF p. 103).
- *Also:* Christ figures such as Neo (*The Matrix*), Luke (*Cool Hand Luke*) and Carton in Dickens's *Tale of Two Cities* (Truby, PDF p. 103).

### 6b. Animal symbolism (p. 103)
- *A Streetcar Named Desire*: Williams compares his people to beasts, which diminishes them but also anchors them in biological drives. Stanley is called pig, bull, ape, hound and wolf, stressing his greed, brutality and maleness; Blanche is linked to a bird and a moth, delicate and scared. The images return in varied forms until, finally, the wolf devours the bird (Truby, PDF p. 103).
- Superhero and jungle-hero stories (*Tarzan*, *Spider-Man*, *Batman*, *Crocodile Dundee*): comic books are today's myths, so they make the animal link literal from the outset, the most extravagant, metaphor-heavy symbolizing possible, through name, body and costume. Unlike Stanley, who merely has beastly traits, these are **animal men**, split down the middle between human and beast. The harsh, savage condition of human life drives them to take on an animal's powers to fight for justice; the price is an inner division they can't govern and an isolation from others they can't overcome (Truby, PDF p. 103).
- **Why audiences love it:** linking a character to an animal appeals because it amounts to enlarging him, though not so much that the story stalls (compare ch. 6 on getting big). Swinging through jungle or city, or commanding animals, are dreams buried far down in people. *Other examples:* *The Silence of the Lambs*, *The Wolf Man*, *Dracula*, *Dances with Wolves* (Truby, PDF p. 103).

### 6c. Machine symbolism (pp. 103-104)
**What it is.** A robot or machine character typically has mechanical, superhuman strength but no emotion or compassion. It appears mostly in horror and science fiction, forms where extravagant symbols are part of the bargain (Truby, PDF p. 103).

**How skilled writers repeat it.** With most symbolic characters, repetition adds detail; with the machine man, skilled writers **flip** the symbol. By the end, the machine has turned out to be the most humane character present, while the human has acted like a beast or a mechanism (Truby, PDF p. 104).

*Example:* *Frankenstein* (Shelley's novel and the 1931 film), which Truby credits with inventing the character-as-machine approach. The doctor begins as the human but soon rises to a god's level, a man able to make life. He builds the machine man, the monster stitched together from parts and lacking human grace of movement. The hunchback sits between, a sub-human outcast who works for the doctor. Truby points out how simply and clearly these types define and contrast the cast. Precisely because he is handled as something lesser, a device to chain up, burn and throw away, the monster turns on his heartless, godlike creator. *Other examples:* the replicants (*Blade Runner*), the Terminator, HAL (*2001*), the Tin Woodman (*The Wizard of Oz*) (Truby, PDF p. 104).

### 6d. Other symbolism (p. 104)
*The Sun Also Rises*: Truby's model of a symbolic character made without god, animal or machine types. Hemingway builds an internal opposition into Jake Barnes, a sturdy, self-assured, principled man who is also impotent from a war wound. That pairing produces a man whose essence is being **lost**, deeply ironic, moving from one sensual pleasure to the next yet unable to function at that most basic level. A man unable to be fully a man, he is completely realistic and also stands for a whole drifting generation of men (Truby, PDF p. 104).

### 6e. Technique: the symbolic name (p. 104)
**What it is.** Turning a character's core principle into what he is called (Truby, PDF p. 104).

*Examples:* Dickens, a genius at this, chose names whose look and sound announce a nature at once. Scrooge obviously cares for money above everything; Uriah Heep's stiff first name can't disguise the oiliness of "Heep"; Tiny Tim is plainly the perfect good child well before his famous blessing (Truby, PDF p. 104).

**Caveats.** Truby notes Nabokov's remark that the device became much rarer after the nineteenth century, likely because it can be showy and too obviously thematic. Well handled, it is excellent, but it generally suits **comedy**, which tends toward types (Truby, PDF p. 104).

*Example:* the guest list at one of Gatsby's parties: Fitzgerald strings together names straining to sound like American aristocracy, then undercuts them with blunt facts about who the guests really are or how they ended up (a drowning, a prison sentence, and so on). Truby quotes the passage at length (Truby, PDF p. 104).

**Real people among invented ones.** A related device mixes historical figures into fiction, as in Doctorow's *Ragtime*, *The Wind and the Lion*, DeLillo's *Underworld*, *Carter Beats the Devil* and Roth's *The Plot Against America*. Such figures aren't truly "real" on the page; fame has turned them into icons, sometimes near-gods, in readers' minds, a nation's mythic pantheon. Their names arrive with **ready-made power**, like a flag, which the writer may reinforce or work against (Truby, PDF p. 104).

### 6f. Technique: a symbol that tracks character change (pp. 104-105)
**What it is.** An advanced device: pick a symbol the character **turns into** when he changes, and use it to mark the change (Truby, PDF pp. 104-105).

**How.** Concentrate on the **framing scenes**, the opening and closing structural scenes. Tie the symbol to him while you set up his weakness or need; reintroduce it, with a variation, when the change happens (Truby, PDF p. 105).

*Example: The Godfather* (p. 105). The first scene is a classic visit to Vito Corleone by a man seeking justice. It plays as a bargaining session, and Vito's closing remark, that he may someday call in a favor, hints that a deal with the devil has just been struck and that Vito is the devil. Near the end the devil symbol returns: Michael, now the Godfather, stands as godfather at his nephew's baptism and formally rejects Satan while, in the same moments, his men kill the heads of the rival families; his deeds make him Satan even as he vows to protect a child whose father he will have murdered once the ceremony ends. The scene after this battle would usually hold the self-revelation, but Michael has become the devil, so the writers deny him one and hand it to his wife Kay, who sees his men pay homage to the underworld's new ruler before the door closes on her. **Why it works:** nobody mentions the devil in the opening. The writers attach the symbol purely by how the scene is built, placing the word "Godfather" last, right before the line hinting at a bargain. Truby stresses that this subtlety is the reason for the impact, not an obstacle to it (Truby, PDF p. 105).

## 7. Symbolic themes (pp. 105-106)

**What it is.** Next after story symbols and character symbols: packing **entire moral arguments** into a symbol. This packs meaning more densely than any other symbol technique, and so it is also the **riskiest**; handled crudely or obviously, it makes the story preach (Truby, PDF p. 105).

**How.** Look for one image or thing that stands for a chain of deeds that harm people. Stronger still: one that stands for **two opposing moral chains** (Truby, PDF p. 105).

*Examples:*
- Hawthorne's letter A: at first glance it seems to carry a plain argument against adultery. Over the novel it comes to stand for two rival moralities: the harsh, inflexible, hypocritical code that humiliates Hester in public, and the truer, more supple morality she and her lover actually lived in private (Truby, PDF p. 105).
- *Beau Geste*: reveals something essential about the technique, that it **succeeds best when carried by the plot**. As boys playing at King Arthur, the eldest brother hides inside a suit of armor and overhears a secret about the family's sapphire; grown up, he steals the gem and enlists in the Foreign Legion to protect his aunt's honor. The armor comes to mean chivalrous self-sacrifice, the "beautiful gesture" at the heart of the theme. Because the symbol lives in the plot, its tie to the theme can deepen as events unfold (Truby, PDF p. 105).
- *The Great Gatsby*: Fitzgerald builds a thematic progression out of three linked symbols. The green light is modern America, its first dream bent into a pursuit of riches and of a golden girl prized for her wrapping. The giant eyeglasses billboard over the ash heap is the exhausted America behind the glitter, the machine's waste; in Truby's phrase, the machine has swallowed the garden. The closing image of the continent's fresh green landscape is America as first found, full of promise for a new way of living, a second shot at Eden. Truby observes that the three don't appear in time order, but they do appear in the order structure demands: Fitzgerald holds the image of the untouched continent for the last page, after Nick's self-revelation, so its richness becomes shockingly vivid against what was done to it, and it bursts on the reader as a **thematic revelation**. He calls this masterful (Truby, PDF pp. 105-106).

## 8. Symbols for the story world (pp. 106-107)

**What they do.** Some ch. 6 world-building devices, the miniature for one, double as symbol techniques. A central job of symbol is to **sum up a whole world, or a whole set of forces, in one graspable image** (Truby, PDF p. 106).

**Layering onto natural settings.** Islands, mountains, woods and seas carry built-in symbolic force, but writers can add further symbols to intensify or shift what audiences expect of them. One way is to **make the place magical**: Circe's and Prospero's islands, the enchanted woods of *As You Like It* and *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, Lothlórien, Hogwarts' Dark Forest. Strictly, magic isn't itself a symbol; it is an alternative set of laws governing the world. Still, enchanting a place acts as a symbol would: it packs meaning in and gives the place an energy field that seizes viewers' imaginations (Truby, PDF p. 106).

**Symbols for unseen forces.** *Moonstruck*: Shanley makes the **moon** the visible form of fate. That helps a love story, where what is truly at stake is the love more than the lovers. The audience has to believe this is a great love whose failure would be tragic, and one way is to show the love as destined by powers far beyond two ordinary people. Shanley first sets Loretta up as unlucky in love, which hints at bigger forces; her grandfather tells his old friends that the moon is what draws a woman to a man; her uncle recalls waking under an enormous moon to find her father in the street staring up at her mother's window. Then Shanley **crosscuts** the whole family under the full moon (the mother gazing at it, the new lovers at their window, the long-married uncle and aunt moved to make love, the grandfather and his dogs howling), so the moon turns into love's great engine, washing the city in its light (Truby, PDF p. 106).

**Symbols for social change.** When a story's society moves from one stage to another (village to city, for example), the forces are tangled, and one symbol can make them concrete, unified and clear (Truby, PDF p. 106):
- *She Wore a Yellow Ribbon*: a cavalry captain's final days at a remote post parallel the closing of the frontier and its warrior ethic. A blustering sergeant, retiring days earlier, drinks to news that buffalo are returning in herds; viewers know the return won't last, any more than these men will (Truby, PDF pp. 106-107).
- Leone's *Once Upon a Time in the West*: a mail-order bride arrives to find her new family murdered and herself owner of apparently useless desert land; going through her husband's belongings she discovers a **toy town**, at once a model in miniature and an emblem of what's to come, the town he pictured rising when the railroad reached him (Truby, PDF p. 107).
- *Cinema Paradiso*: the theater stands for the entire story and for its world: a shelter where people gather for the magic of film and, in doing so, become a community. As the town grows into a city, the theater crumbles and is finally replaced by a parking lot; the utopia dies, and the community breaks apart. Truby holds it up as proof of how much meaning a symbol can hold, and how it can make viewers weep (Truby, PDF p. 107).

**Symbols for huge systems.** *The Matrix* and *Network*: a story set inside something as vast as a whole society or institution practically needs a symbol to reach viewers. Each title suggests one entity that is at the same time a net of threads that hold people captive. They signal from the start that this is an intricate world driven by many forces, several of them hidden, telling viewers not to try to solve it all at once and promising satisfying discoveries to come (Truby, PDF p. 107).

## 9. Symbolic actions (p. 107)

**What it is.** One action is ordinarily one link in the long chain of moves by which hero and opponent fight for the goal. Making it symbolic means tying it to a further action or object, which gives it charged meaning (Truby, PDF p. 107).

**Caution.** A symbolic action breaks out of the plot's flow and calls attention to itself, as if declaring that it matters especially and sums up the story's theme or a character in miniature. Use it with care (Truby, PDF p. 107).

*Examples:*
- *Wuthering Heights*: Heathcliff's pretend duel with a "black knight" over Cathy at their imaginary moorland castle expresses their shared romantic fantasy and her hunger for wealth and rank, and acts out in small his later struggle with the wellborn Linton over her (Truby, PDF p. 107).
- *Witness*: raising the barn alongside the Amish men while exchanging looks with Rachel shows John is ready to give up a cop's violent life for love inside a peaceful community (Truby, PDF p. 107).
- Dickens's Carton: like Christ crucified, he goes willingly to the guillotine so that others can live (Truby, PDF p. 107).
- *Gunga Din*: the water carrier who dreams of being a soldier sounds his bugle in the last battle; he is sure to die, but it saves the regiment from an ambush (Truby, PDF p. 107).

## 10. Symbolic objects (pp. 107-110)

**Never alone.** A symbolic object almost never works in isolation, since on its own it can barely point beyond itself. Joined by some organizing idea, objects can build a rich, layered pattern, generally serving the theme (Truby, PDF p. 107).

**The glue.** Go back to the **designing principle**, which turns separate objects into a group; each then points not only outward but also to the story's other symbolic objects (Truby, PDF pp. 107-108).

**Genre webs.** Such webs can be built in any story but stand out most in **myth, horror and the Western**. Retold so often, these forms have been polished to near perfection, including objects used so many times that they have become familiar metaphors: **ready-made symbols** viewers understand instantly at some level (Truby, PDF p. 108).

### 10a. The myth symbol web (p. 108)
**Gods as a model of the mind.** Myth is the oldest form and still the most popular. The Greek myths, a cornerstone of Western thinking, work through allegory and metaphor. They always present at least two orders of being, gods and humans, but Truby warns against reading this as the Greeks' literal belief that gods govern people. The gods are better seen as the part of a person that can reach excellence or enlightenment: a brilliant **psychological model** in which a web of figures embodies traits and behaviors to aim for or shun (Truby, PDF p. 108).

**A fixed set of objects.** Myths also use a set repertoire of symbolic objects whose meanings original audiences knew precisely; storytellers created effects by placing these side by side as the tale went on. Most important, each symbol also stands for **something inside the hero**. Truby lists the main ones with their likely ancient meanings, adding that no symbol's meaning is ever wholly fixed. In our summary: the journey means the road of life; the labyrinth, bewilderment in seeking the way to enlightenment; the garden, harmony with nature's law, within oneself and with others; the tree, life itself; animals (horse, bird, snake), guides on the road toward enlightenment or toward hell; the ladder, steps toward enlightenment; the underworld, uncharted regions of the self and the realm of the dead; talismans (sword, bow, shield, cloak), right action (Truby, PDF p. 108).

*Example: the Odyssey* (p. 108). Truby ranks it as the most artistic Greek myth and the most influential one for storytelling, partly because of its symbolic objects. As always, begin with the characters:
- Homer moves from the *Iliad*'s fighter who battles until he dies to a cunning fighter who wants to get home alive. Odysseus can fight, but he is more a seeker, a schemer and a lover.
- That change shifts the symbolic theme **from mother-rule to father-rule**: rather than the king dying while the mother endures, Odysseus comes back to reclaim the throne. Like most great heroes he changes, arriving home as himself but enlarged as a person. His greatest moral choice proves it: going home means choosing to be mortal rather than immortal.
- A core symbolic opposition is male against female: Odysseus learns by traveling, while Penelope stays put, learns from dreams and decides by them.
- The object web grows from these characters and this theme, so it rests on masculine objects (bow, oars, mast, staff, ax), each a form of direction and right action. Against them stands the **tree** at the base of the couple's bed: the tree of life, signaling that marriage is a living thing that flourishes or withers. If the husband roams too far or stays away too long in search of glory, the warrior's highest prize, the marriage dies, and so does life (Truby, PDF p. 108).

### 10b. The horror symbol web (pp. 108-109)
**What horror is about.** The dread of something not human invading human society: the lines between alive and dead, sane and insane, good and evil are breached, and ruin always follows. Because the genre asks the most basic question, where the human ends and the inhuman begins, it has acquired a religious outlook, which in Western horror is **Christian**, and Christian cosmology shapes nearly all of its characters and symbols (Truby, PDF pp. 108-109).

**Cast and argument.** The hero usually reacts, while the main opponent drives events: the devil, or one of his servants, evil in person, the wicked father who will drag people to damnation unless stopped. The moral argument is always a simple binary of good against evil (Truby, PDF p. 109).

**The web.** It too starts from a binary, shown visually as **light against darkness**. The chief light symbol is the **cross**, powerful enough to drive back Satan himself. Dark symbols are often animals. In pre-Christian myth, creatures such as the ram, bull, stag, horse and serpent stood for ideals leading upward to right conduct; Christian symbolism recast them as evil, which is why the devil wears horns. Wolves, apes, bats and snakes signal the loosening of restraints, the victory of the body and its passions, the road to hell, and they are strongest in the dark (Truby, PDF p. 109).

*Example: Dracula* (p. 109). The undead vampire is the supreme creature of night, feeding on people he kills or infects into slaves, resting in a coffin, burning in daylight. Vampires are intensely sensual, gazing hungrily at a bare throat; in these stories **sex means death**, and smudging the border between living and dead brings a fate worse than dying, an endless nighttime purgatory. Dracula can change into bat or wolf and haunts rat-infested ruins. As a count, he belongs to a decaying, corrupt nobility that feeds off ordinary people. At night he is overwhelming, but whoever knows his weaknesses can stop him: he cringes from the crucifix and burns under holy water. *Other users of this set:* *The Omen*, *The Exorcist*. *Carrie* takes the identical set and turns it upside down, making Christian symbols signs of bigotry and narrow minds; Carrie's revenge on her fanatically religious mother uses a crucifix (Truby, PDF p. 109).

### 10c. The Western symbol web (pp. 109-110)
**What the Western is.** Truby calls it the final great myth of creation, since the American West was the earth's final habitable frontier, and America's national myth, retold thousands of times, which gives it a highly metaphorical symbol web. Its story: masses of people head west, subdue the wild and build homes, guided by a solitary fighter-hero who beats the "savages" so that settlers can found a town. Like Moses, he brings his people to the edge of the promised land yet can't go in himself; he stays single and alone, riding the wild country until both he and it are gone (Truby, PDF p. 109).

**A future dressed as the past.** The form peaked roughly from 1880 to 1960, always about a period already over. Yet as a creation myth it was always a picture of the future, a stage of national growth that Americans had chosen together, though placed in the past and impossible to build for real. Its program: subdue the land; destroy or "civilize" the peoples considered inferior; carry Christianity and civilization westward; convert nature into riches; and found the nation. Its designing principle: all of world history replayed on the untouched blank page of the American wilderness, America as humanity's final opportunity to get back to Eden. A national story turns religious depending on how it defines its rituals and values and how fervently people believe it, and such a story generates a strongly metaphorical web (Truby, PDF p. 109).

**The five main symbols, in our summary (pp. 109-110):**
- **The horseman:** hunter and fighter at once, the high point of warrior culture, borrowing from England's Arthur myth: an everyman knight of noble nature who follows a chivalric code of right conduct, the Code of the West.
- **The six-gun:** no armor, but a weapon of mechanical force, a vastly amplified "sword" of justice. His code forbids drawing first, and justice must be done in an open street showdown in front of everyone.
- **The hat:** the Western, like horror, deals in binaries; white for the hero, black for the villain.
- **The badge,** shaped like a **star:** the hero upholds right, often to his own cost, since his violence isolates him; he may formally join the community by becoming a lawman, imposing law both on the frontier and on the wildness inside people.
- **The fence:** always a thin wooden one, showing how shallow civilization's hold is over untamed land and untamed human nature (Truby, PDF pp. 109-110).

*Examples of the web:* *Stagecoach*, *My Darling Clementine*, *The Virginian*, and the most diagrammatic of all, *Shane* (Truby, PDF p. 110).

**Extended example: *Shane* (p. 110).** Its schematic design makes the symbols easy to spot, but so conspicuous that viewers never stop feeling they are watching a "classic Western"; Truby calls that the main danger of strongly metaphorical symbols. His points:
- It pushes the mythic Western as far as it goes: a mysterious rider, already traveling when we first see him, comes down off the mountain, stops in one place, then heads back up.
- It belongs to what Truby calls the **traveling angel story**, found too in detective fiction (Poirot), comedy (*Amélie*, *Chocolat*, *Crocodile Dundee*) and musicals (*The Music Man*, *Mary Poppins*): the hero arrives in a community in trouble, helps put it right, and leaves for the next. Shane plays the wandering warrior-angel who battles the cattlemen so farmers can settle and build a town.
- Its cast is strongly symbolic: a Christ-like hero against a demonic hired gun; a farmer devoted to family (his name is Joseph) against an old, merciless cattle baron with no wife; Marian, the model wife and mother; and the son who hero-worships whoever handles a gun best. They get almost no personal detail (Shane's gun-haunted past is never explained), so they work purely as attractive metaphors.
- Every stock Western symbol appears in its cleanest form, but the **gun** is made the core of the theme: each man is measured by whether he dares to use it. Cattlemen loathe the farmers for fencing land; farmers fight for a proper town with law and a church; Shane dresses in pale buckskin while the hired killer wears black. The store where farmers buy what they need to build homes has a connecting door into the saloon where cattlemen drink, brawl and kill; Shane tries to start fresh on the store side but keeps being drawn through to his old life as a lone gunman.
- Truby's judgment: not poor storytelling; its force comes from how tidy its symbol web is, with nothing wasted. But that tidiness also makes it feel diagrammatic, its moral argument edging into moral philosophy, as religious stories nearly always do (Truby, PDF p. 110).

## 11. Technique: turning a genre's symbol web inside out (pp. 110-111)

**Weakness as opportunity.** A ready-made metaphorical web draws so much attention to itself and is so easy to predict that viewers experience a diagram instead of living through the story. That flaw is also a chance: turn viewers' familiarity with the genre into a tool and **invert** the web. Keep every expected symbol but twist each so it means something quite unlike what the audience anticipates, which forces them to rethink everything they assumed. This works with any story whose symbols are familiar; within a genre such as myth, horror or the Western, Truby calls it **undercutting the genre** (Truby, PDF p. 110).

**Extended example: *McCabe and Mrs. Miller* (pp. 110-111).** Much of its brilliance is its plan for inverting Western symbols, which follows from inverting the usual Western theme: instead of people carrying civilization into the wilds, a businessman carves a town out of the wilds and is crushed by big business.
- **Characters:** McCabe, a dapper gambler, gets rich running a brothel, creating community out of the wilderness through the commerce of sex; the woman he loves is a madam who smokes opium.
- **Subworlds:** no neat grid of clapboard storefronts on a dry Southwestern flat, but a rough settlement of planks and tents hacked out of a soaking Northwestern forest; no lively town under a kindly marshal, but a scattered, half-finished place of apathetic, mistrustful loners.
- **Symbolic action:** the showdown, usually staged on main street before the whole town, with the hero letting the villain draw first and still beating him to reaffirm law and order. Here McCabe, who is no lawman, is stalked through town by three gunmen in a whiteout while the townsfolk, oblivious, battle a fire at a church nobody uses.
- **Objects:** law is absent; the church is empty; one killer ambushes McCabe with a shotgun from behind a building; McCabe, seemingly dead, kills him with a concealed derringer (in classic Westerns, a woman's gun); he wears an Eastern suit and bowler rather than chaps and a white hat.
- Truby calls the film a lesson in renewing worn metaphorical symbols and a milestone of American cinema (Truby, PDF pp. 110-111).

## 12. Examples of symbol webs (pp. 111-113)

Truby says these techniques are best learned by seeing them at work across many kinds of stories (Truby, PDF p. 111).

- ***Excalibur* and the Arthur story (pp. 111-112).** If the Western is America's national myth, the Arthurian legend has a claim to be England's, shaping thousands of later tales, so writers should understand its key symbols. *Characters:* Arthur is more than man or monarch: a modern centaur, the armored rider, the first superman, maleness at its extreme, the embodiment of warrior culture (bravery, strength, right action, justice won in public combat). Ironically, that extreme masculinity follows a chivalric code that sets woman on a pedestal of perfect purity, turning all women into a symbol split by the Christian opposites of virgin and whore. He also stands for the modern leader torn by conflict: he builds an ideal community at Camelot on purity of character, then loses it as his queen falls for his best and purest knight; Truby ranks duty versus love among storytelling's great moral oppositions. Merlin is the archetypal mentor-magician, a survivor from the pre-Christian world of magic who understands nature's deeper forces, the consummate artisan of nature and human nature, whose spells start from a close reading of what each person needs and craves. The villains have been borrowed by many writers: Mordred, the wicked son whose very name suggests death, and his mother Morgana (Morgan le Fay), a sorceress. The knights are supermen too, above ordinary men in skill and in purity of character; bound by the code, they seek the Grail as their way into heaven, helping everyone in need along the way. *Places and objects:* Camelot, the ideal community where members give up personal glory for the peace and happiness of all, represented in turn by the **Round Table**, where every knight sits as an equal beside the king; and the sword Excalibur, the masculine emblem of right conduct, which only the rightful, pure-hearted king can pull from the stone and use to build the ideal community. These symbols saturate later stories (*A Connecticut Yankee*, *The Fisher King*, *Star Wars*, *The Lord of the Rings*, *Hope and Glory*, countless Westerns). Truby's advice: if you borrow them, **bend their meaning** until they belong to your story (Truby, PDF pp. 111-112).
- ***The Usual Suspects* (p. 112).** A rare case of a character building his own symbolic character with these techniques while the story is happening. Verbal, who seems to be a minor crook and sidekick, is actually the hero, the criminal mastermind (main opponent) and the storyteller. Talking to the customs investigator, he invents the fearsome Keyser Söze and pins the **devil** symbol on him until the name alone terrifies. In the end we learn Verbal is Söze, a great criminal partly because he is a great storyteller; Truby rates it storytelling and symbol work of the highest order (Truby, PDF p. 112).
- ***Star Wars* (p. 112).** A key to its success is **symbolic theme**, focused in the **light saber**. In a world of starships crossing space at light speed, both sides fight with swords: not realistic, but believable enough within that world to bear thematic weight. The saber stands for a samurai-style discipline of training and behavior usable for good or for evil; Truby says its role in the films' global popularity is hard to exaggerate (Truby, PDF p. 112).
- ***Forrest Gump* (p. 112).** Two objects carry themes: the **feather** (Forrest's free, easygoing spirit) and the **box of chocolates** (his mother's famous saying about not knowing what you'll get, a direct statement about how to live wrapped in a metaphor). One could call this heavy-handed, but Truby argues it works better than it looks, for instructive reasons: the film is myth fused with drama and spans roughly four decades, so the story drifts like the feather with no direction but history's; and the hero is a simple man who thinks in catchy sayings, so a line that would sound preachy from an ordinary character delights when Forrest, quoting the mother he adores, says it, and it delights most viewers too (Truby, PDF p. 112).
- ***Ulysses* (pp. 112-113).** Joyce pushes the writer's role as conjurer, symbol-builder and puzzle-setter beyond anyone else. The price: the reader's response moves from feeling to intellect. Thousands of subtle, often obscure symbols, deployed in thousands of tricky ways, turn readers into literary detectives who step back to study how the puzzle is assembled; like *Citizen Kane* (for other reasons), it is easier to admire than to love (Truby, PDF p. 112).
  - *Story symbol and symbolic characters:* Joyce lays *Hamlet*, the Gospels' Christ and the *Odyssey* over his story, adding real Irish figures and national icons. The gains: it links character to theme, since Joyce is building a natural, humanist religion out of what his people do, so ordinary Molly, Bloom and Stephen acquire heroic, even divine stature through their deeds and their constant echoes of those figures; it sets them within a great tradition while showing them break from it and become individuals, which is exactly Stephen's struggle against Church and English rule as he tries to become his own man and a true artist; and it provides a network of **character signposts** across a long, intricate book, letting Joyce measure how his leads change by referring to the same symbolic figures in different ways (Truby, PDF pp. 112-113).
  - *Symbolic actions and objects:* Joyce keeps matching his characters' deeds to those of Penelope, Telemachus and Odysseus, an effect both heroic and ironic: Bloom bests his Cyclops and escapes the bar's cave; Stephen's dead mother haunts him, as Odysseus meets his mother among the dead and Hamlet his father's ghost; Molly stays home like Penelope but, unlike her, is notorious there for unfaithfulness. The objects form a web of holy things in Joyce's everyday religion: both men go out without their keys; Stephen's glasses broke the day before, dimming his eyesight while he gains an artist's vision; an advertisement for potted meat alludes to the sex missing from Bloom's marriage and the damage to his home; Stephen swings his walking stick like a sword at the brothel chandelier and breaks the past's grip; and Bloom, who regards Catholic communion as candy for believers, shares a genuine communion of coffee and cocoa with Stephen at home (Truby, PDF p. 113).

## 13. Exercises: Writing Exercise 6, "Creating Symbols" (p. 113)

In our summary, the exercise asks the writer to:
- look for a single symbol capturing the premise, the main twists, the core theme or the shape of the whole story, reviewing premise, theme and one-line world first, and then sum up the story's chief symbols in a sentence;
- work out **character symbols**, using the chapter's six-part process (whole web first; hero against opponent; one trait or emotion; possible internal opposition; frequent repetition; varied detail);
- think about tying some characters to a **type**, chiefly god, animal or machine;
- find a symbol for the hero's **change**, focusing on the opening scenes of weakness and need and the closing self-revelation;
- find a symbol for the main **theme**, remembering a theme symbol has to represent a chain of morally consequential acts, and at a higher level two such chains in conflict;
- choose symbols for the **world**'s elements: time, technology, man-made spaces and natural settings;
- pick out any **actions** worth treating symbolically and give each one a symbol;
- build a web of symbolic **objects** by starting from the designing principle and checking each object against it;
- track how each symbol **develops** across the story (Truby, PDF p. 113).

**Worked example: *The Lord of the Rings* (pp. 113-115).** Truby describes it as a modern English cosmology and mythology, blending myth, legend and high romance with echoes of Norse and Greek myth, Christianity, fairy tale and Arthurian quest stories. It is allegorical in Tolkien's sense of being applicable to our own era, which means its people, places, deeds and things are necessarily highly metaphorical: still Tolkien's own creations, but resonating with older symbols lodged deep in readers. Highlights, paraphrased:
- **Story symbol:** the ring of the title, limitless power everyone wants; whoever holds it becomes a godlike lord, and inevitably a destroyer. It is the supreme temptation pulling people away from a good, contented life, and it never stops pulling.
- **Symbolic characters:** the book's great strength is its dense web of figures, sorted three ways: moral side, rank of power (divine beings, wizards, men, hobbits), and kind (humans, elves, dwarves, orcs and so on), rather than by man against man, beast or machine. Myth relies on types, which gives it epic sweep but little subtlety about people; by making the web of types so intricate, Tolkien and his readers get both, a lesson Truby offers to anyone writing myth-based symbolic characters. Goodness appears in those who give themselves up for others (Gandalf, Sam), in a warrior-king who can heal as well as kill (Aragorn), and in those who live in harmony with nature and rule themselves rather than others (Galadriel, Tom Bombadil). Tolkien's hero isn't the mighty fighter but small Frodo, whose greatness of heart makes him the bravest of all; like Joyce's Bloom, a new kind of myth hero, defined by humanity rather than might. On the dark side, Morgoth, the primal evil from the backstory, calls up Satan and is bound to death in name and deed; Truby jokes that English authors love villains named with "mor" (Mordred, Maugrim, Voldemort), perhaps because it echoes the French for death. Sauron, the main opponent, is evil for craving total power and for meaning to use it to wreck Middle-earth; Saruman is a "switch" figure, a wizard sent against Sauron who was poisoned by a taste of absolute power; Gollum, the Nazgûl, the orcs, Shelob and the Balrog embody destruction, brutality, hatred and envy.
- **Symbolic theme:** as in any good story, and above all in allegory, everything rests on the theme line and its oppositions; for Tolkien that is a Christian good-versus-evil structure in which evil is loving and using power, good comes from caring for what lives, and its peak is self-sacrifice, above all giving one's life for another.
- **Symbolic worlds:** as rich as the cast, natural and supernatural at once, with even the built places growing out of the landscape, and set in opposition: lovely, harmonious Lothlórien and the Ents' forest against a malign wood; the good forests against the mountains where evil lives (Sauron's stronghold behind its huge gate, which Truby notes is yet more "mor"; Moria's caverns, where the heroes go underground; the Dead Marshes, a graveyard of the battle-slain). The communities follow suit: Lothlórien a utopia of trees, Rivendell a utopia of water and greenery, the Shire a village set in farmed, tamed country, all against fortresses of raw power (Mordor, Isengard, Helm's Deep).
- **Symbolic objects:** the plot turns on seeking and holding objects, mostly dug from the earth or forged in fire. The One Ring, forged in Mount Doom's fires, stands for the craving for total power and false values and corrupts whoever has it; the all-seeing Eye atop the Dark Tower is a second ring-shaped emblem of evil. Andúril ("flame of the west"), like Excalibur, is the sword of right action that only the true heir may carry; where Excalibur was fixed in stone, Andúril was shattered and must be reforged before Aragorn can defeat evil and reclaim his throne. His use of the healing herb athelas sets him apart as a warrior-king who, like Achilles in skill but unlike him in spirit, is attuned to nature and serves life. Truby adds that these are only a handful of Tolkien's symbols and urges close study of the book (Truby, PDF pp. 113-115).

## 14. Terms introduced

| Term | Meaning in Truby |
|---|---|
| **Symbol** | A word or thing that stands in for something beyond itself and recurs through the story; Truby's "technique of the small." |
| **Hidden language** | What symbols give the writer: an emotional channel to the audience that runs under the surface. |
| **Condenser-expander** | A device that compresses meaning so it expands in the audience's mind; symbol is the most compressed of all. |
| **Refer and repeat** | Truby's rule of thumb: link a feeling to a symbol, then bring the symbol back in altered forms to intensify the feeling. |
| **Resonance** | The ripple each appearance of a symbol sets off, widening with repetition, often below awareness. |
| **Symbol web** | A network of mutually defining symbols; the subsystem that makes meaning by pointing things toward other things. |
| **Story symbol** | One image that gathers a story's twists, central theme or overall structure. |
| **Symbol line** | One sentence linking all the main symbols, drawn from designing principle, theme line and story world. |
| **Symbolic character** | A character tied to a symbol for his defining principle or its opposite. |
| **Internal symbol opposition** | Giving one character two clashing symbols to make him more complex. |
| **God / animal / machine symbolism** | Category shortcuts that convey a mode and rank of being at once. |
| **Animal man** | The superhero type, literally part human and part beast, divided inside and isolated outside. |
| **Machine man (robot man)** | A strong, unfeeling mechanical character; good writers invert it so he proves the most human. |
| **Symbolic name** | A name that encodes a character's core principle (Dickens's method); best suited to comedy. |
| **Ready-made (prefabricated) symbols** | Symbols, genre objects or historical names whose meaning the audience brings with it. |
| **Symbol tied to character change** | A symbol the character becomes, placed in the opening and closing framing scenes. |
| **Framing scenes** | The structural scenes at the start (weakness, need) and the end (change). |
| **Symbolic theme** | A symbol standing for a moral argument: a chain of harmful acts, or two opposed moral chains. |
| **Structural order** | Placing symbols where they hit hardest in the structure (e.g., after the self-revelation), even out of time order. |
| **Story-world symbol** | An image that sums up a whole world or a set of social or supernatural forces. |
| **Symbolic action** | A single action given charged meaning by linking it to another action or object; it stands out from the plot. |
| **Web of symbolic objects** | Objects tied together by the designing principle into a pattern of meaning, usually serving the theme. |
| **Myth / horror / Western symbol webs** | The ready-made symbol sets of these three genres. |
| **Traveling angel story** | A hero arrives in a troubled community, helps set it right and moves on. |
| **Undercutting the genre** | Keeping a genre's expected symbols but twisting their meanings to overturn expectations. |
| **Character signposts** | Recurring references to the same symbolic figures, used to measure change across a long story. |
