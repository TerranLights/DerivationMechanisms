# Dan Harmon's Story Circle: overview and principles

**Source:** Dan Harmon's six "Story Structure" tutorials for Channel 101, read on the Channel 101 Fandom wiki mirror:
- 101 "Super Basic Shit" (cited here as SS101);
- 102 "Pure, Boring Theory" (SS102);
- 103 "Let's Simplify Before Moving On" (SS103);
- 104 "The Juicy Details" (SS104);
- 105 "How TV is Different" (SS105);
- 106 "Five Minute Pilots" (SS106).

Also: Harmon's blog answers of 9 and 10 August 2013 and 18 February 2014; StudioBinder's "Story Circle Workbook" (SB Workbook) and "Story Circle Worksheet" (SB Worksheet), both local texts. Secondary sources are listed in the log at the end.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## 1. The model in brief
Harmon's base statement has eight clauses, one per step (SS101):
1. a character starts out comfortable;
2. the character wants something;
3. they cross into an unfamiliar situation;
4. they adapt to it;
5. they get what they were after;
6. they pay heavily for it;
7. they go back to the familiar situation;
8. they come back changed.

In SS103 he boils the sentence down to eight single words: **You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change.** He says the result sounds like a caveman barking an order, and that this is the point. Under the culture-making part of the brain sits an older, louder one, and one of the few things it keeps repeating is "go, search, find, take, and come back changed." In his view that loop is how the species, and every human community, has survived.

Harmon's own one-line summary: underneath any good story the true shape is just a circle, going down into the unknown and eventually coming back up. He calls this **descent and return** (SS101; SS104). Everything more detailed, he says, is particular to the writer and the story (SS104).

## 2. Origin
- **Late 1990s.** Wired reported in 2011 that Harmon began doodling the circles in the late 1990s while he was stuck on a screenplay. He wanted to find a simple, symmetrical pattern beneath the stories he admired, and he studied *Die Hard* and Joseph Campbell to find it (Raftery, *Wired*, 22 Sept. 2011, as summarized by Wikipedia and SpoilerTV). ⚠️ The Wired body text could not be retrieved directly; see the log.
- **"Embryos."** The same article says Harmon calls his circles *embryos*, because each holds everything a satisfying story needs. That is the source of the alternate names "story embryo" and "plot embryo" in secondary writing. In his own tutorials Harmon speaks of "the circle," his "circular model," or his "story model." In 2013 he calls it the "8 point story structure." "Story Circle" is the name readers and the industry settled on.
- **Influences he names himself:**
  - Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, whose chapter names he places on the circle (SS104; SS106);
  - Christopher Vogler, for the phrases "friends, enemies and allies" and "Approach to the Innermost Cave" (SS104; SS106);
  - Syd Field, for Plot Point 1, the midpoint and Plot Point 2 (SS106).

  Wikipedia also lists Vogler's *The Writer's Journey* as an influence.
- **Channel 101.** Harmon and Rob Schrab set up Channel 101 around 2003 (precursor 2001). It is a monthly screening of five-minute pilots, where the audience votes on which shows continue (Wikipedia, "Channel 101").
  - Harmon wrote the tutorials for its filmmakers. Many of them said they couldn't write plots, and his series ends by applying the circle to the five-minute format (SS102; SS106).
  - The wiki copies were made in August 2009 from articles on channel101.com; the edit notes give article IDs 11 (SS101) and 29 (SS106).
  - The original posting dates aren't recorded. In SS106 Harmon says he has been obsessed with structure for seven years, which, with a late-1990s start, places that tutorial in the mid-2000s. ⚠️ This is an inference.
- **Later use:**
  - *Community*: Wired describes circles on Harmon's whiteboards, wall sheets and a notepad in his car, used for everything from single gags to whole seasons.
  - *Rick and Morty*: the circle is used to break episodes in the writers' room (No Film School, 2017; Nerdist, 2020).
  - At Cornell in 2017 Harmon said he had never required the *Community* writers to use the method, though some former writers went on to preach it (*Cornell Daily Sun*, 12 Nov. 2017).

## 3. How to draw it
The instructions are in SS101, restated in SS106:
1. Draw a circle.
2. Split it with a vertical line, then with a horizontal one.
3. Moving clockwise from twelve o'clock, number the four places where the lines meet the rim **1, 3, 5, 7**.
4. Number the four quarter-sections between them **2, 4, 6, 8**.

So step 1 sits at the top, 3 at the right (east) end of the horizontal line, 5 at the bottom, and 7 at the left (west) end.

The odd-numbered steps are therefore **points on the axes**: moments of crossing or turning. The even-numbered steps are **spans that fill the quarters**: processes. Harmon's step-by-step text in SS104 matches this:
- 3 and 7 are thresholds;
- 5 is a "pivot point";
- 4 is a phase or road;
- 6 has its own road back up.

⚠️ The point/span reading is our synthesis of his numbering and wording. His five-minute arithmetic in SS106 also gives each of the eight steps an equal share of time (see section 7).

## 4. Why a circle: the three rhythms (SS102)
Harmon offers SS102 as optional theory, not tutorial. He asks why stories need certain parts in a certain order before an audience recognizes them as stories. His answer is that life, the mind and society all move in the same alternating rhythm, and a story that plays in that rhythm resonates. He illustrates each rhythm with a circle split by a horizontal line.

- **Biology: life above, death below.**
  - The universe as a whole is running down, but life on Earth runs the other way, growing and multiplying.
  - It manages this through death: an evolutionary arms race in which more advanced life eats less advanced life.
  - Death is unwelcome to the individual but essential to life as a whole, half of how anything gets done.
- **Psychology: conscious above, unconscious below.**
  - The mind is a house. The conscious upstairs is lit, tidy and sociable. The unconscious basement is older, darker and frightening. He nods to Freud and Jung on what is kept down there.
  - We avoid the basement, but we have to make regular round trips: to change the fuses, so to speak.
  - Seal the basement and the house becomes unstable: the ego covers the noise with neurosis, and in the extreme the door gives way (a breakdown).
  - The ego's periodic trips down and back keep the conscious mind working. Such trips can happen in many ways, therapy and meditation among them, and so can a good story.
- **Society: order above, chaos below.**
  - A society is a person writ large: where a mind has a conscious upper floor, a society has order, and where a mind has an unconscious cellar, a society has chaos.
  - It stays alive because real people go into the unknown and come back with new (or forgotten) ideas. At their most dramatic these people are heroes, but scientists, artists, teachers and priests do it every day.
  - A society that pretends its basement doesn't exist turns neurotic, much as a person would.
- **Resonance.** A story that keeps this beat, whether it is a great novel or a fart joke, takes the audience's ego briefly down into the unconscious and returns it. Audiences have an instinctive appetite for that trip.

SS103 adds a joke contrast. A hypothetical hyper-evolved spider species might prefer stories that run in a straight line, in which Jack builds his own beanstalk, eats a sandwich at the top, and that's the end. Humans want circles: Jack climbs, finds treasure, steals it, comes back down, and gives it to his mother. **Return** and **Change** matter most, because a community whose heroes never came back down would not have survived.

SS101 asks the writer to see the pattern of descent and return everywhere and to trust that it is built into the nervous system. Raised by wolves, Harmon says, you would still tell stories this way.

⚠️ The biological and social claims in SS102 are Harmon's framing argument, not research. We record them as his rationale only.

## 5. The horizontal line: the two worlds (top and bottom)
The horizontal line separates the **familiar world above from the unknown world below**. Harmon labels the pairs **life / death**, **conscious / unconscious** and **order / chaos** (SS102 diagrams). The SB Worksheet prints **ORDER** above the line and **CHAOS** below it.

In SS104 he shades the bottom half and calls it the **"special world."** This is where the story's "movie poster" (whatever you would advertise to draw an audience) starts to be delivered. The top half is the **"ordinary world."** He takes both terms from the Campbell/Vogler tradition.

What really happens below the line, he says, is a trip into the audience's own inner basement, where problems get worked through. The size of the story doesn't matter. What matters is how much the two worlds **contrast**.

The two places where the line meets the circle, 3 and 7, are the crossings: down into the unknown, and back out of it.

In 2013 Harmon notes that "order on top, chaos below" is true of almost everything, from *Star Wars* to a chewing-gum commercial. For building or diagnosing a particular story, he prefers a more specific, story-owned pair for the top and bottom (Harmon blog, 10 Aug. 2013).

## 6. The vertical line: going down and coming up (right and left)
The vertical line separates the **descent** (the right side, steps 1 to 5) from the **ascent** (the left side, steps 5 to 1).

- **Right side: reacting.** In the first half the protagonist responds to what the world does to him; he adjusts, transforms and hunts (SS104).
- **Left side: acting.** From step 5 on, movement becomes the protagonist's own choice. One doesn't get dragged past the goddess; one pauses there, chooses, and rises (SS104).
  - In step 6 the hero realizes that something matters more than himself. In Harmon's words he becomes the force that makes things happen, a "living God" scaled to the size of the story (SS104).
- **The ascent mirrors the descent.**
  - The climb has trials of its own, just as the descent did. The trials going down ready the hero for the goddess; the trials coming up ready him to rejoin the ordinary world.
  - Much of the upward half refers back to the downward half (SS104).
- **The *Community* room's shortcut.** When unsure, label the right side as lying and the left side as truth-telling. It almost always fits, because the midpoint is usually where things turn real.
  - It is as generic as order/chaos, though: combined with that pair, it sends nearly any story through the same four lying/truthful, ordered/chaotic quarters.
  - So again he advises finding a more specific pair (Harmon blog, 10 Aug. 2013).

**Choosing the axes.** In his general method, Harmon picks a pair for each line that gives the writer a personal "charge," like a battery: a distinction the writer finds exciting and wants to explore. If the charge fades, he backtracks or begins again (Harmon blog, 9 Aug. 2013).

## 7. The quadrants
**Timing (SS106).** A five-minute Channel 101 pilot is 300 seconds: 75 seconds per quarter, about 37.5 seconds per step. Each quarter has a job:
- **First quarter:** get the audience to attach to a character (or thing), give it a lack, and begin altering its situation (steps 1, 2 and the start of 3).
- **Second quarter:** let it cope with the altered situation and reach the thing it lacked (steps 4 and 5).
- **Third quarter:** make it pay for what it found and turn it homeward (steps 6 and the start of 7).
- **Fourth quarter:** show the starting situation transformed by all this (step 8).

So in percentage terms the odd steps fall at the quarter marks: 1 at 0%, 3 at about 25%, 5 at about 50%, 7 at about 75%. The even steps fill the quarters between. ⚠️ The percentages are our arithmetic from his timings.

**Quadrants as an arc.** Harmon treats the four quadrants as four combined states, which a character passes through clockwise. In 2013 he maps Jeff Winger's arc in *Community*'s Dungeons & Dragons episode this way. The axes are altruism/guilt (top/bottom) and control/lack of control (right/left). Jeff moves from controlled altruism to controlled guilt, to guilt he can't control, and finally to altruism he can't control (Harmon blog, 10 Aug. 2013). This is what he means by checking for "arcs": walk through the story in one character's shoes and ask what changes top to bottom and right to left.

**Act breaks.** He recalls that the *Community* room found act breaks with two questions: which new world the characters cross into at step 3, and the worst outcome that world can produce at step 6 (Harmon blog, 10 Aug. 2013).

**Three-act reading.** In SS106 Harmon equates:
- step 3 with Syd Field's Plot Point 1;
- step 5 with the midpoint;
- step 6 with Plot Point 2.

Many secondary sources read the circle as three acts: the top-right quarter as Act 1, the bottom half as Act 2, the top-left quarter as Act 3. ⚠️ That is a common secondary reading, not Harmon's own wording.

## 8. Opposites across the circle (SS104)
Harmon stresses that a circular model is full of symmetry, and he pairs steps that face each other.
- **1 and 5 (maternal, vulnerable):**
  - Step 1 is the comfort of the mother's arms, however imperfect she is.
  - Step 5 is a new, unconscious version of that mother, which is why there is often a pull to stay there.
  - Going on past 5 is the hero's choice. Characters who are not heroes may be destroyed by the goddess, or lulled into staying forever. He cites the sirens' wrecked ships, and the elf's house in *The Lord of the Rings*.
- **2 and 6 (paternal, masculine, active,** whatever the protagonist's gender**):**
  - Step 2 is the pull away from comfort: things were fine but not good enough.
  - Step 6 is the confrontation with the father-like force, Campbell's Atonement with the Father.
- **3 and 7:** the two threshold crossings, down and back up.
- **4 and 8:** what the hero gains during the trials pays off at the end.
  - The ally made, the object received, the skill learned at 4 is what saves the day at 8.
  - That is why a last-minute rescue at 8 is not a deus ex machina: it was earned at 4.

Practical use: when stuck on a step, look at the step across the circle.

## 9. Relation to Campbell's monomyth
Harmon's caption to his SS104 diagram calls Campbell a comparative mythologist, not a screenwriting guru. The diagram shows where Harmon himself thinks Campbell's chapters would fall if they were forced into the circle. His placements (SS104 diagram; SS106 lists):

| Step | Campbell stages Harmon places here | Other writers' terms Harmon gives |
|---|---|---|
| 1 You | none; the diagram labels it "balance" | none |
| 2 Need | Call to Adventure; Refusal of the Call; Supernatural Aid | none |
| 3 Go | Crossing of the (First) Threshold | Field: Plot Point 1 |
| 4 Search | Belly of the Whale; Road of Trials | Vogler: friends, enemies and allies |
| 5 Find | Meeting with the Goddess; the Ultimate Boon (diagram only) | Field: midpoint; Vogler: Approach to the Innermost Cave |
| 6 Take | Atonement with the Father; Apotheosis; "Death and Resurrection" (SS106) | Field: Plot Point 2 |
| 7 Return | Magic Flight; Rescue from Without; Crossing of the Return Threshold | none |
| 8 Change | Master of Both (Two) Worlds; Freedom to Live | none |

Notes on the fit:
- **Stages Harmon does not place.** Campbell's stages are 17, in three parts: Departure, Initiation, Return. Harmon's diagram leaves out **Woman as the Temptress** and **Refusal of the Return**. ⚠️ His step-5 discussion of the temptation to linger with the goddess touches both themes, but he doesn't name them.
- **"Death and Resurrection"** is not one of Campbell's chapter titles. ⚠️ It reads like a general theme, or like Vogler's later "Resurrection."
- **The Ultimate Boon.** Campbell's order puts the Boon after Atonement and Apotheosis. Harmon's diagram lifts it to step 5, before them. ⚠️
- **Campbell's three parts on the circle.** ⚠️ Our alignment: Departure falls roughly in the top-right quarter (1 to 3), Initiation in the bottom half (3 to 7), and Return in the top-left quarter (7 to 1).
- **Stage counts in secondary sources.** Some secondary sources call Campbell's monomyth an 11-step model (Looper; Reedsy). Campbell's own scheme has 17 stages, and Vogler's screenwriting version has 12. ⚠️ Treat those secondary counts with caution.

**Harmon on Campbell, 2014.** A reader asked why the circle seemed to fit Campbell until steps 6 and 7. Harmon answered:
- He doesn't think Campbell meant that every story includes a magic flight and a rescue from without. Campbell was describing recurring ways that heroes get home, before discussing the more basic idea of the return threshold.
- Campbell warned against reading myth too literally. He would not have wanted his analysis turned into a paint-by-numbers kit, and Harmon counts his own internet summary as an example.
- What matters in Campbell, Harmon says, is that "Heroes go Somewhere Else and Heroes Come Back Different" (Harmon blog, 18 Feb. 2014). Everything else is the writer's to interpret.

**Differences in emphasis** (our synthesis of the sources above):
- Harmon generalizes from the mythic hero to any protagonist at any scale: a raccoon, a homeless man, the President (SS104).
- He grounds the pattern in audience psychology: identification, and the trip to the unconscious.
- He compresses Campbell's many stages into eight slots that can be timed.

Secondary explainers describe the result as a simpler, less fantasy-bound version of the Hero's Journey (StudioBinder blog; Reedsy).

## 10. Scale: why it suits short arcs
Harmon gives several reasons the circle works at small sizes. Note that he never says it is *only* for short or minor arcs: he applies it to features, TV and anecdotes alike.

- **It was built for five minutes.** The whole tutorial series exists to help people make five-minute pilots, and SS106 times each step in seconds.
- **"Major" is relative.**
  - Harmon's running example is a driver whose tire blows out in a downpour. He gets out, empties his trunk, finds the spare, fixes the wheel, and drives off with an anecdote for his wife (SS104).
  - Each turn is "major" only in relation to the size of the circle; a tiny sigh of relief can be the whole midpoint.
  - SS106's minimal example, a thirsty man who gets a soda from the fridge, has flawless structure and is still a bad story. Structure is necessary, not sufficient.
- **It is fractal.**
  - Any single step can be told as its own little circle of eight substeps. SS106 tells the "Need" step of a soda story as a mini-circle in which the man discovers the drink is poisoned and dies.
  - Harmon doesn't recommend computing everything down to the second, but says that going down a level can help with decisions and blocks.
- **Steps can be implied.**
  - When time is short a step can be skipped and left implied. In his own Channel 101 show *Call Me Cobra*, the road of trials is just a man sitting down at a table (SS106).
  - A roughly drawn, slightly egg-shaped circle is round enough. Time can be shifted between sections without anyone noticing (SS106).
- **TV runs a full circle every episode** (SS105).
  - Television differs in one practical way: it has to bring the audience back forever. So step 8 in TV leans toward putting things back where they started rather than changing the world.
  - A sitcom *Star Wars* would keep the Death Star.
  - Seen across a whole series, a pilot delivers steps 1 to 3, and the rest of the run lives in step 4. But each episode still completes all eight steps.
  - In his TV version of the eight-step sentence, the closing step amounts to admitting that change was pointless. He treats that as a commercial convention, not a structural truth.
  - In SS106 he adds that a pilot's last quarter should show that the protagonist, now a "situation-changer," can go on more journeys.
- **Every character can have a circle.**
  - His 2013 breaking method ends by creating more characters as needed and giving them their own stories.
  - In the D&D episode, he says, Neil, Pierce and the group as a whole each have quadrants of their own, which may differ from Jeff's or overlap with them (Harmon blog, 9 and 10 Aug. 2013).
  - Secondary accounts of *Rick and Morty* describe separate circles for the A-story and B-story characters within one episode, for example Morty's and Jerry's in "Mortynight Run" (Nerdist, 2020). ⚠️ Secondary.

## 11. Structure, quality and universality
- **Structure is geometry** (SS106).
  - Structure is one narrow aspect of a work, like its geometry. It is separate from lighting, acting, editing and coming up with good ideas.
  - Perfect structure doesn't make a good show, and a string of exciting events with no structure doesn't make a story either. You want both.
  - Some structure helps somewhat, more helps more, and none at all can cripple a piece.
- **Exceptions.** There are exceptions to everything, but Harmon calls those "style, not structure" (SS104).
- **Iconography** (SS106).
  - Learned from cartooning with Rob Schrab: an audience recognizes a drawing as a banana only when it has enough banana features, and a story only when it has enough of the eight steps.
  - Stories are food: audiences need to recognize the nourishing ones for individual and community health.
- **Change, not morality** (SS106).
  - The model is not about good guys beating bad guys. It is about a protagonist going down and coming back.
  - Harmon avoids ethical positioning: stories are about change, not about lessons or keeping order.
  - Good structure costs nothing and is instinctive, which makes it the independent writer's best weapon against corporate product.
- **Why teach an instinct?** Modern people have fallen out of touch with their instincts, the way adults can forget how to swim (SS106).
- **Self-description.** Harmon calls himself someone obsessed with structure, not a great writer. The model describes what audiences recognize as a story, not how to write well (SS106).

## 12. The StudioBinder worksheets (local texts)
**SB Workbook.** A fill-in Google Doc template. It gives each step a one- or two-line definition and an example from *The Dark Knight*.

Its definitions borrow vocabulary that Harmon doesn't use: the "Point of Attack," "Defining the Main Tension," a first attempt to resolve the tension, the main tension resolved "yes or no," a "false resolution," a "twist," and an "ultimate resolution." ⚠️ These terms match Frank Daniel's sequence approach as written up by Paul Gulino, in which the point of attack ends the first sequence, the main tension is set at the end of Act 1, and a twist arrives in the seventh sequence. The identification is ours.

The Workbook differs from Harmon in four places:
- **Step 3:** it asks the writer to explain why reaching the goal will be hard. Harmon's step 3 is the crossing into the unfamiliar world.
- **Step 5:** it puts the devastating price inside Find. Harmon puts the price at step 6.
- **Step 6:** it frames Take as the all-out assault on the main tension, settled one way or the other "for now."
- **Step 7:** it treats Return as a false resolution or exposition sequence leading to a twist. Harmon's Return is the escape and crossing back.

**SB Worksheet.** A blank circle with the eight step names around it, **ORDER** above the horizontal line and **CHAOS** below, and a "story notes" box. In the text extraction the word "RETURN" also appears next to step 3 ("GO!"). ⚠️ This is probably a layout artifact of the PDF.

## 13. Exercises Harmon gives the writer
- **Test the pattern** (SS101). Check as many favorite films as you can against the pattern. Then check party anecdotes, vivid dreams, fairy tales and the music (not the lyrics) of popular songs, until the pattern feels ordinary and visible everywhere.
- **Try to defy the model** (SS106). Write something with no protagonist, a protagonist with no need, a need with no change of circumstance, a failure to adapt or find, or no return. He predicts this is hard, and that writing freely tends to land on the steps by accident.
- **Break a story his way** (Harmon blog, 9 Aug. 2013):
  1. Start from loose ideas and notice which ones cluster into an "area" that moves you.
  2. Draw a circle for the area.
  3. Split it top/bottom by a pair of ideas that gives you a charge, then left/right by a second such pair.
  4. Name the four quadrants clockwise and try protagonists moving through them.
  5. Then add detail with the eight steps.
  6. Give other characters their own stories as needed.
  7. Whenever the charge fades, backtrack or begin again, even if collaborators think the idea was already fine.
- **Optional subdivision** (SS106). Break any step into an eight-substep mini-circle when stuck.

## 14. The author's usage (recorded, not redefined)
In the author's composite system, the Harmon structure is used for **minor characters**. Under the author's standing rule, it is reserved for **human characters** and for **very minor or background robots**. Any robot in the foreground always receives the full composite structure (Snyder, Truby, Weiland, Campbell, Bell) instead.

The master names the eight steps there but does not define them. Pointer: `story-structure-definitions.md`, section "Which Structure Applies to Which Character — Standing Rule."

## 15. Terms introduced
- **You / Need / Go / Search / Find / Take / Return / Change:** Harmon's eight one-word steps (SS103). Each has its own file in `8 Steps/`.
- **Story Circle; story (or plot) embryo; 8 point story structure:** the names for the model, from readers, Wired, and Harmon (2013) respectively.
- **Descent and return:** Harmon's name for the underlying shape of every story (SS101; SS104).
- **Zone of comfort:** the protagonist's starting state (SS101).
- **Ordinary world / special world:** the top and bottom halves of the circle (SS104).
- **Order / chaos; conscious / unconscious; life / death:** the three pairs the horizontal line stands for (SS102).
- **The three rhythms:** biology, psychology, society; Harmon's argument for why circular stories resonate (SS102).
- **Movie poster:** whatever you would advertise; it starts being delivered once the hero crosses into the bottom half (SS104).
- **The goddess:** Harmon's shorthand for whatever is found at step 5; it need not be a woman (SS104).
- **Living God:** the hero in step 6, once something matters more than the self; scaled to the story (SS104).
- **Situation-changer:** the hero at step 8, back in charge and now able to change things (SS106).
- **Charge / charged dichotomy:** a pair of ideas that excites the writer, used to label the axes (Harmon blog, 2013).
- **Quadrants:** the four combined states a character passes through clockwise (Harmon blog, 2013).
- **Substeps / mini-circle:** a full eight-step circle nested inside one step (SS106).
- **Shadow:** the villain as the hero's unconscious counterpart, who may follow him back up to the ordinary world (SS104, the *Die Hard* reading).
