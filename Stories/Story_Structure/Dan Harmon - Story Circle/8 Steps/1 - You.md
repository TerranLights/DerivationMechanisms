## What it is
Step 1 introduces the protagonist in a **zone of comfort**: the settled, familiar state the story starts from and will eventually come back to (SS101). Harmon's heading for it in SS104 is simply "establish a protagonist." His diagram of Campbell placements labels the top of the circle "balance."

The step answers the question "who are we?" The answer can be anything that can carry a point of view: an animal, the sun, a blood cell, a country (SS106). Its function is less about the character's situation than about **giving the audience a body to inhabit**. Until the story offers one, the audience drifts like a ghost with nowhere to land, and until it is inside a character it is not yet inside the story (SS104).

In the circle's symmetry, step 1 is the "maternal" pole. It is the comfort of a mother's arms, however flawed the mother. It faces step 5 across the circle, the unconscious counterpart of that comfort (SS104).

## How it works
- **Showing someone is enough.** Harmon calls this the easy part. Fade in on a character and the audience will attach to it, raccoon, homeless man or President alike. The audience will *be* that character until a better candidate appears. You'd have to work to stop the audience from attaching (SS104).
- **Pity leads to identification.** Given several candidates, viewers pick the one they relate to, and when unsure they follow their sympathy.
  - A raccoon fleeing a bear becomes "us."
  - In a room of diplomats, the President who stumbles on the rug becomes "us."
  - Harmon claims that feeling sorry for someone uses the same mental machinery as identifying with them (SS104).
- **Free-floating openings are allowed, briefly.** A film can hold the audience unanchored for a while, for example drifting toward Earth or panning across a shed, but sooner or later it has to settle on someone (SS104).
- **Limit hopping between characters.**
  - Many modern stories pass the viewpoint around before settling. This can work.
  - But if it goes on for more than about **a quarter of the story**, the audience is lost.
  - Identification is like adhesive: it weakens each time it is peeled off and re-stuck, and the longer it has held, the more jarring the switch (SS104).
- **Safest choice: a protagonist who acts as the audience would.** The character can be an assassin or a parasite. What matters is that in this situation they do what a viewer would plausibly do. Harmon's example is the opening of *Die Hard*: John McClane, a nervous flyer on a plane (SS104).
- **What the StudioBinder Workbook says.**
  - Step 1 is the protagonist's undisturbed status quo, which leads into the "Point of Attack."
  - Its example is Bruce Wayne's routine as Batman, fighting Gotham's criminals, in *The Dark Knight* (SB Workbook, step 1).
  - ⚠️ "Point of Attack" is sequence-approach vocabulary that Harmon doesn't use; see the overview, section 12.
- **TV version.** In SS105's sitcom version of the circle, step 1 is reduced to the bare first-person "I" (SS105).
- **Scale.** In the flat-tire anecdote, step 1 is just a man driving his car (SS104, step 2 example). In the minimal example, it is a thirsty man on a couch (SS106).
- **Payoff at the end.** The story returns to this spot at step 8. What the hero lacked or feared at 1 is what the ending measures against: McClane, scared of flying at 1, leaps off a building at 7 (SS104).

## Placement
- **On the circle:** the twelve o'clock point, where the vertical line meets the top of the circle. It is the start and end of the loop, in the upper (ordinary, ordered, conscious) half (SS101; SS104).
- **In time:** Harmon wants the audience to know who "you" is within the first eighth: about the first 37 seconds of a five-minute pilot (SS106). ⚠️ So about 0-12.5% of the running time, by our arithmetic.
- **Before the need:** step 1 is a point, and step 2 fills the first quarter after it (SS101 numbering).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** none named. Harmon's diagram puts no Campbell stage at 1 and labels it "balance" (SS104 diagram). ⚠️ It corresponds to the hero's everyday world before the Call to Adventure.
- **Snyder:**
  - Opening Image (p. 1) and Set-Up (pp. 1-10), with Theme Stated (p. 5) inside it (Snyder, ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!").
  - Snyder's "before" snapshot of the hero, and his Act 1 "thesis" world, describe the same starting state.
  - Snyder's "Save the Cat" scene (making the audience like the hero early) and Harmon's pity-equals-identification point aim at the same goal by different means. ⚠️
- **Truby:** ⚠️ no exact match.
  - Nearest: step 2, Ghost and story world (PDF p. 121), and step 3, Weakness and need (PDF p. 122). Truby establishes the hero's past wound and present lack at the start.
  - Harmon's step 1 asks only for an identifiable person, and he puts the lack at step 2.
- **Bell:** ⚠️ Bell's opening material, as the composite master lists it, is Set-Up with the Care Package inside it. The master gives no Bell beat for the hero's introduction as such.
- **Weiland:** ⚠️ the Normal World, the first node of her plot skeleton as named in the composite master.
- **Others Harmon names:** none for this step. StudioBinder links it to the sequence approach's "Point of Attack." ⚠️
