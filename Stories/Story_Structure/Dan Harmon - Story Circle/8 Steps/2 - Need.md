# Need (Dan Harmon's Story Circle, step 2)

**Source:** Harmon:
- SS101, clause 2;
- SS103;
- SS104, section "2. 'Need,'" plus the symmetry passages in sections 5 and 6;
- SS105, the TV version;
- SS106, entry 2.

Also the StudioBinder Workbook (step 2) and Worksheet. Short citations follow the key in `../01 - Overview and principles.md`.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
Step 2 shows that **something is wrong or missing**. The world, at whatever scale the story works in, is out of balance. That imbalance is the reason a story is about to happen at all (SS104; SS106).

Harmon's own wording moves between "want" and "need":
- the base sentence says the character *wants* something (SS101);
- the one-word list calls it a *need* (SS103).

He does not separate a conscious goal from a hidden lack, the way some other frameworks do. The need can come from inside the character or from outside. It can be spoken or not. It can be something the character knows about or not. Step 5 will satisfy it "whether it was the direct, conscious goal or not" (SS106).

In the circle's symmetry, step 2 is the first of the two "paternal," active, masculine moments, whatever the protagonist's gender. Step 1 was the mother's arms. Step 2 is the father's pull: things were fine, but not quite good enough, and that dissatisfaction is how the whole adventure starts (SS104). It faces step 6 across the circle.

## How it works
- **The ride starts climbing.** Harmon compares this step to a roller-coaster car clanking up its first hill (SS104).
- **Show the imbalance at the story's own scale.**
  - In an Earth-versus-Mars war story, this is where the Martian fleet heads for Earth.
  - In a romantic comedy, it may be the heroine stuck on a bad blind date (SS104).
  - SS106's quick examples: the protagonist is an alcoholic; a body lies on the floor; a biker gang rides into town.
- **A wish or a call.** The need can appear in two ways (SS104):
  - as the character's own wish, often visible only on his face, to be cooler, richer, faster or a better lover;
  - or as an outside **Call to Adventure**, delivered by a messenger. Harmon's example is a dry cleaner informed that a spy agency has conscripted him.

  A call doesn't require a literal herald, and a wish doesn't have to be voiced.
- **The wish will be granted, but not as expected.** Harmon notes that the character's wish here will come true in a way they couldn't have predicted (SS104). Two of his examples:
  - In *RoboCop*, Murphy's unconscious wish at this step, to be an invincible TV-style hero, is granted by his transformation, and it later becomes a trap (SS104, step 6).
  - In *Big*, a boy's wish to be big is granted at the next step (SS104, step 3).
- **Refusal of the Call is optional.** The protagonist often resists going to step 3; he thinks he is happy as he is. Harmon calls this a common device for keeping the audience locked into identification (everyone fears change), not a required part (SS104).
- **It can be wordless.** His flat-tire example: a timid-looking man is driving in heavy rain when a tire blows. He fights the car to the shoulder and stops, and he looks out at the downpour with fear on his face (SS104).
- **The *Die Hard* example** (SS104).
  - McClane's marriage is in trouble: his wife moved to Los Angeles for a good job, he refused to come, and she now lists herself under her maiden name.
  - They bicker. Things are wrong.
  - Could we read his mind, we would find him wishing he could fix the marriage.
- **What the StudioBinder Workbook says.** Step 2 is the "creation of the story," which leads to "defining the main tension." In its *Dark Knight* example, Bruce Wayne wants to retire Batman so he can be with Rachel, and wonders whether Harvey Dent could protect Gotham in his place (SB Workbook, step 2). ⚠️ "Main tension" is sequence-approach vocabulary, not Harmon's.
- **TV version.** In the sitcom version, the protagonist merely notices a small problem (SS105).

## Placement
- **On the circle:** the upper-right quarter, between the top point (1) and the right-hand crossing (3). It is still in the ordinary, ordered upper half (SS101).
- **In time:** within the first quarter. In five minutes, roughly seconds 37 to 75 (SS106). ⚠️ About 12.5-25%, by our arithmetic.
- **Within the first quarter's job:** the first quarter has to make the audience identify with someone, give that someone a need, and begin changing the situation (SS106).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** Harmon places three stages here: Call to Adventure, Refusal of the Call, and Supernatural Aid (SS104 diagram; SS106).
- **Snyder:** ⚠️ Catalyst (p. 12) and Debate (pp. 12-25) (Snyder, ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!").
  - Snyder's Set-Up "Six Things That Need Fixing" list is also close in spirit: show the audience what is lacking in the hero's life.
  - One difference: Snyder makes the Debate a necessary section posing a question. Harmon treats refusal as optional.
- **Truby:** ⚠️
  - Step 3, Weakness and need (PDF p. 122), step 4, Inciting event (PDF p. 123), and step 5, Desire (PDF p. 124).
  - Harmon's "need" behaves more like Truby's **desire** (the goal) or the inciting event that triggers it than like Truby's **need** (an inner lack the hero is unaware of).
  - Harmon's notion that the wish is granted in unexpected ways does touch Truby's need.
- **Bell:** ⚠️ the Disturbance and the Argument Against Transformation, which the composite master lists as aliases of Catalyst and Debate.
- **Weiland:** ⚠️ the Inciting Event, from her skeleton as named in the master.
- **Others:** StudioBinder ties this step to the sequence approach's main tension. ⚠️
