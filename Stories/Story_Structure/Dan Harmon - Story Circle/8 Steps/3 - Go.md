# Go (Dan Harmon's Story Circle, step 3)

**Source:** Harmon:

- SS105, the TV version;
- SS106, entry 3;

Also the StudioBinder Workbook (step 3) and Worksheet. Short citations follow the key in `../01 - Overview and principles.md`.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
Step 3 is the **crossing of the threshold**: the protagonist enters an unfamiliar situation (SS101). Until now the "you" of step 1 was in one situation; at step 3 that situation changes (SS106). Whatever the story is *about* begins here. If it is about a woman fleeing a killer cyborg, she hasn't been fleeing one until now; from this point on she is (SS104).

On the circle, this is where the path drops below the horizontal line, out of the **ordinary world** and into the **special world** (SS104). In Harmon's psychological reading, that means:
- out of order and into chaos;
- out of the conscious mind and into the unconscious;
- out of life and toward death (SS102).

What is really happening, he says, is a trip into the audience's own unconscious, where its problems can be worked out (SS104). Step 3 faces step 7, the return crossing, across the circle (SS104).

## How it works
- **Find your "movie poster."** Ask what you would advertise to get people to come hear this story: a monster, a setting, a crime world, a romance.
  - The whole lower half of the circle is where that poster starts to be delivered.
  - Step 3 is where delivery begins (SS104).
- **Genre examples** (SS104):
  - In a story about an infatuation, this may be the moment the hero first sees the person he will obsess over.
  - If the protagonist is the object of an obsession, the infatuation may already have been step 2. Step 3 is then the moment the admirer says something truly unsettling to her.
  - In a coming-of-age story, a first kiss or a first sign of puberty.
  - In a slasher film, the first killing or the discovery of a body.
- **A three-beat illustration.** A small-town sheriff (1) finds odd bite marks on a murder victim (2); at step 3 it turns out there really is a werewolf (SS104).
- **The wish meets its test** (SS104):
  - In *Big*, a boy wakes up as a grown man: his wish has come true.
  - In *Die Hard*, terrorists seize the Christmas party, which gives McClane a literal chance to rescue his marriage.
  - In *The Matrix*, Neo wakes up in a fluid-filled pod, in a world the machines control, and his ordinary-world urge to fight the system will now be tested.
  - A suicidal boy begins therapy, and we will learn why.
  - SS106 adds quick examples: a hiker walks into the woods; Pearl Harbor is attacked; a mob boss starts therapy.
- **Contrast matters, not scope.** The story can be tiny or vast. What counts is how sharply the two worlds differ.
  - In the flat-tire anecdote, the man was dry inside his car. Now he opens the door and steps out into the downpour.
  - However small or subtle, the adventure has begun (SS104).
- **Chosen or forced?**
  - Harmon's symmetry passage speaks of the hero being *drawn* to step 3 and *pulled* across into the unknown (SS104).
  - His TV version, by contrast, has the protagonist make a major decision here (SS105).
  - So the crossing may be imposed or chosen. ⚠️ This is our reading: Harmon doesn't insist on either.
- **In the writers' room.** Harmon recalls the *Community* room locating act breaks by asking, for step 3, which new world the characters are entering (Harmon blog, 10 Aug. 2013).
  - In his D&D-episode analysis, Pierce's arrival (he kills Chang and steals Fat Neil's sword) pushes Jeff across the line from altruism into guilt.
  - Jeff moves from a world where he was "fixing" Neil to one where he may be to blame for Neil's ruin.
- **What the StudioBinder Workbook says.** Step 3 asks the writer to explain why reaching the goal will be difficult. Its *Dark Knight* example: to carry out his plan, Batman must help Harvey Dent bring the criminal Lau to justice (SB Workbook, step 3; the Workbook spells the name "Lao"). ⚠️ This is closer to stating obstacles than to Harmon's threshold crossing. The SB Worksheet labels the step "GO!".

## Placement
- **On the circle:** the three o'clock point, where the horizontal line meets the right edge of the circle: the boundary between the upper and lower halves (SS101; SS106).
- **In time:** at the end of the first quarter, around 75 seconds into a five-minute piece. The first quarter's job ends with beginning to change the circumstances (SS106). ⚠️ About 25%, by our arithmetic.
- **Syd Field:** Harmon equates this step with Field's **Plot Point 1** (SS106).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** Crossing of the First Threshold (SS104 diagram, "threshold"; SS106).
- **Snyder:** Break into Two (p. 25) (Snyder, ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!").
  - Snyder's Act 2 is the "antithesis," an upside-down version of the opening world. It corresponds closely to Harmon's lower half.
  - Snyder's Fun and Games delivers the "promise of the premise," the part that matches the poster. That matches Harmon's poster idea, though Harmon starts delivery right at the crossing.
  - One difference: Snyder insists that the hero must *decide* to enter Act 2 and cannot be lured or tricked into it. Harmon allows the hero to be pulled across.
- **Truby:** ⚠️ no threshold step.
  - The nearest are step 4, Inciting event (PDF p. 123), and step 5, Desire (PDF p. 124), where the goal and the action begin.
  - Truby's idea of a subworld or new arena (ch. 6) may touch the special world. ⚠️
- **Bell:** ⚠️ Doorway of No Return #1, given in the composite master as Bell's name for Break into Two.
- **Weiland:** ⚠️ the First Plot Point, which the master equates with Doorway #1.
- **Syd Field:** Plot Point 1 (Harmon's own mapping, SS106).
- **Sequence approach:** ⚠️ the end of Act 1, where the main tension is set, per StudioBinder's vocabulary.
