## What it is
At step 8 the protagonist is back where they began, **having changed** (SS101). In SS104's list they are now capable of change; in SS103's shorthand they come back and change things.

SS106 puts it most precisely: the protagonist from step 1 is once more in control of their circumstances, but is now a **"situation-changer."** Nothing will go back to how it was.

Harmon titles the section with Campbell's **Master of Both Worlds**. The hero has been to the strange place, adapted to it, found real power, and is now home, permanently altered and permanently able to alter things (SS104). The lover is now capable of love, the martial artist has mastered the art, and the slasher's prey can now turn on the killer (SS104).

Harmon's distilled version of Campbell, given in 2014, ends here: "Heroes go Somewhere Else and Heroes Come Back Different" (Harmon blog, 18 Feb. 2014).

## How it works
- **The genre's big scene goes here** (SS104):
  - In an action film, the showdown.
  - In a courtroom drama, the cross-examination that breaks the murderer.
  - In a romance, the hero chases down the plane on the tarmac and makes an imperfect, all-in declaration of love. Harmon spoofs this at length.
- **Bystanders recognize the change.** Harmon jokes about the stock stranger seated beside the love interest who urges her to go to him. The reason for such reactions, he says, is that the protagonist is now a force who can alter the world, at whatever scale (SS104).
- **Look across the circle to step 4** (SS104).
  - Harmon recommends a "really neat trick": show the audience that the hero's new ability comes from his time in the depths.
  - Across the circle from step 8 sits step 4, so the payoff draws on what was gained during the trials: a lighter given by a tramp stops the bullet; a stammering actor delivers a perfect speech; allies won in the trials gallop in to save the day.
  - Harmon admits the lighter gag is a cliché, but says it is a cliché because it works, and urges writers to take it apart and build their own version.
  - Because it was earned at 4, it is not a deus ex machina.
- **The shadow may follow the hero home** (SS104).
  - The ordinary world can be invaded by what lived below: a pursuing lawman who ignores the jurisdiction line, a stowaway alien aboard the escape craft, a dinosaur loose in the suburbs.
  - This is likely in action stories whose return was rough. Like Odysseus finding his house full of suitors, the hero must clean house.
  - In *Die Hard*, the lead terrorist is McClane's unconscious **shadow**, who has followed him up into the ordinary world and holds his wife at gunpoint.
  - The real McClane has spent the story learning new behavior; the shadow has spent it clinging to a crumbling ego. The shadow now holds what McClane craved at step 1: control.
  - McClane has learned that surrender can be the best attack. He gives up his empty weapon and uses the small pistol he carried from the ordinary world, taped to his back.
  - He frees his wife from the villain, and symbolically from her yuppie life, by unclasping the gift watch the villain is clinging to.
- **Minimal versions show what structure is for** (SS104).
  - The driver with the flat starts the engine and heads home, now owning an anecdote.
  - Compare an ending where the man pulls over and a werewolf simply eats him. Harmon says everyone knows instinctively that only the first is a story, however cool werewolves are.
- **A whole film in eight steps** (SS104). Harmon's *Matrix* summary:
  - an ordinary man (1) receives an odd summons (2);
  - answering it, he discovers his reality is fake (3);
  - he is trained (4) and consults the oracle (5);
  - his mentor is lost (6), he goes back in (7), and he wins (8).

  He contrasts this with the sequel, where he says the steps are hard to find.
- **Quick examples** (SS106): the Death Star is destroyed; the couple is in love; a time-machine project is finished; the heroine heads into the jungle with her partner.
- **TV is different** (SS105).
  - A film's job is to send the audience out on a high. Television's job is to keep the audience watching for life.
  - So TV's step 8 is less about stirring things up and more about putting things back where they began. Movies can blow up the Death Star; a TV version would have to keep it, or the show would end.
  - In the sitcom version of the circle, the last step amounts to admitting that change was pointless.
  - Harmon attributes this to budgets and modular scripts, not to any structural truth.
  - Even so, a TV pilot's last quarter should signal that the protagonist, as a situation-changer, has many more journeys (episodes) ahead (SS106).
- **What the StudioBinder Workbook says.**
  - Step 8 is the ultimate resolution: what happens once the character has lived through these events and made these choices.
  - Its *Dark Knight* example: Batman defeats the Joker; Two-Face remains, and Batman breaks his one rule by killing him; to protect Harvey's legacy he takes the blame for Two-Face's crimes; and he commits to guarding Gotham while hunted and despised. In doing so he becomes the Dark Knight (SB Workbook, step 8).

## Placement
- **On the circle:** the upper-left quarter, from the left-hand crossing (7) back up to the top point (1), where the loop closes. It is back in the ordinary, ordered, conscious half (SS101; SS106).
- **In time:** the fourth and final quarter. Its job is to show the starting situation transformed by everything that happened. In five minutes, roughly seconds 225 to 300 (SS106). ⚠️ About 75-100%, by our arithmetic.
- **Flexible proportions.** Harmon notes that modern audiences may not need a proportionate amount of "happily ever after," so time can be borrowed from this quarter for more action (SS106).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** Master of the Two Worlds (Harmon: "of Both Worlds") and Freedom to Live (SS104 diagram; SS106).
- **Snyder:** Finale (pp. 85-110) and Final Image (p. 110) (Snyder, ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!").
  - Snyder's Finale has the hero apply the lessons learned, dispatch the bad guys in rising order, and create a "new world order." That matches Harmon's showdown and his situation-changer.
  - Snyder's Final Image, the opposite of the Opening Image and proof that change is real, matches Harmon's return to step 1, changed.
- **Truby:** ⚠️ step 19, Battle (PDF p. 132); step 20, Self-revelation (PDF p. 132); step 21, Moral decision (PDF p. 133); step 22, New equilibrium (PDF p. 133).
  - Truby's new equilibrium (everything returns to normal, but the hero is at a higher or lower level because of the self-revelation) is especially close to Harmon's "back where they started, changed."
  - Harmon's McClane, however, has his main self-insight at step 5, not here.
- **Bell:** ⚠️ Final Battle and Transformation, as listed in the gap analysis. Their meanings are not checked here.
- **Weiland:** ⚠️ Climax, Climactic Moment and Resolution, as named in the master.
- **Sequence approach:** ⚠️ the resolution (StudioBinder's vocabulary).

## In the composite

The matching defined beats are **Finale** (the five-point finale) and **Closing Image** (Snyder). The gap analysis records Campbell's Freedom to Live as an alias of Closing Image. Master of Two Worlds is not defined in the master.
