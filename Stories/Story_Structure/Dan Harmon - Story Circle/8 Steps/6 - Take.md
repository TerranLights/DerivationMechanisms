## What it is
At step 6 the protagonist **pays a heavy price** for what they found (SS101). Harmon's SS104 list says simply that they pay its price, and the section heading is "Meet Your Maker." In SS106 he calls it **"the hardest part,"** for the characters and for whoever has to describe it alike.

The word "Take" carries two senses:
- the hero seizes the prize: Jack *steals* the treasure at the top of the beanstalk (SS103);
- the hero takes the consequences.

SS106 makes the two-sidedness explicit. On one side is **the cost of the journey**: a boat lost to the shark, a crucifixion, a beloved elder struck down. On the other is **a goal achieved that no one knew existed**, often inside the same event:
- the shark has swallowed the scuba tank that will destroy it;
- the crucifixion reveals that the body is not what counts;
- the dying elder passes on a keepsake that inspires a win at the rodeo.

In the circle's symmetry, step 6 pairs with step 2. Both are "paternal," active, masculine moments, whatever the protagonist's gender (SS104).
- At step 2 things were fine but not good enough, and that set everything off.
- At step 6 the hero meets the father-force itself.

By the time he reaches step 7, Harmon says, the last scraps of ego are gone and the hero has achieved Campbell's **Atonement with the Father**. That father figure stands for the impersonal, no-nonsense universe. In action films it is usually embodied by the villain, the kind who says it's nothing personal, just business (SS104).

## How it works
- **A second, truer call.** *Real Genius* (SS104):
  - A nerdy student (1) is recruited into a program building a powerful laser (2), rooms with a party-loving genius (3), and the two teach each other focus and fun (4), until the laser works and wins them praise (5).
  - Then a stranger living in the steam tunnels asks what the laser is *for*.
  - The heroes could stay comfortable, but they choose to head back up and set things right.
  - That choice has serious consequences.
- **In action films, the hero gets beaten here** (SS104).
  - In *RoboCop*, armed with a confession, the hero tries to arrest the company boss who owns him, and finds his programming won't let him.
  - The human Murphy might have managed it; the indestructible machine cannot. This is ironic, since being an invincible hero was Murphy's unconscious wish at step 2.
  - Attacked by the machine brother ED-209 and by his misled human brothers, the police, he barely escapes "the father's castle."
- **Not just action.** In *Network*, the newsman-turned-prophet Howard Beale is summoned before the network owner's CEO. In a celebrated monologue, the CEO explains that capital is the true god, and that Beale, having crossed it, must atone. No robots, no explosions: the same structure (SS104).
- **The road back up has its own trials.** The downward road prepared the hero for the goddess. The upward road prepares him to rejoin the ordinary world, and much of it echoes the descent (SS104).
  - In *Die Hard*, having made peace with his marriage at 5, McClane wonders why the lead terrorist wanted the detonators so badly. He finds the top of the building rigged to explode.
  - That discovery brings the cost: the huge blond henchman, the ED-209 to McClane's RoboCop, attacks, and they fight to the death.
  - That fight is only the first of rapid-fire trials (SS104).
- **Love stories put the breakup here**, followed by unshaven days, piled dishes, drawn blinds and deep depression, perhaps a dull rebound relationship, and at last the realization that nothing ever mattered more than the lost partner (SS104).
- **Becoming the force that makes things happen** (SS104).
  - When the hero realizes something matters more than himself, he gains full control of his fate.
  - In the first half he was responding to the universe. Now he has, in Harmon's phrase, become a "living God."
  - Scaled to the story, that might just mean a man able to complete the roadside tire repair in a storm. Or it might mean McClane on the roof, dispatching terrorists and herding hostages to safety under helicopter fire.
- **Finding the step in the room.** Harmon recalls that the *Community* writers located this act break by asking what the worst thing that could happen in the new world would be (Harmon blog, 10 Aug. 2013).
- **What the StudioBinder Workbook says.**
  - Step 6 is the all-out assault on the main tension, which ends with it resolved yes or no, "for now."
  - Its *Dark Knight* example: Batman captures the Joker, but the Joker springs a trap that costs Rachel her life and Bruce his future (SB Workbook, step 6).
- **TV version.** The sitcom version is "there are consequences," which must then be undone at step 7 (SS105).

## Placement
- **On the circle:** the lower-left quarter, from the bottom point (5) up to the left-hand crossing (7). It is still in the special world, but now on the ascending, active side (SS101; SS104).
- **In time:** the third quarter, together with the start of step 7. Its job is to make the protagonist pay for what was found and turn homeward. In five minutes, roughly seconds 150 to 225 (SS106). ⚠️ About 50-75%, by our arithmetic.
- **Syd Field:** Harmon equates this step with **Plot Point 2** (SS106). ⚠️ Field's Plot Point 2 is usually a single turn near the three-quarter mark. Harmon's step 6 is the whole quarter that leads up to it.

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** Atonement with the Father and Apotheosis (SS104 diagram; SS106). SS106 also lists "Death and Resurrection." ⚠️ That is not one of Campbell's chapter titles. The SS104 diagram sets Rescue from Without on the arc between 6 and 7, but the text assigns it to 7.
- **Snyder:** ⚠️ Bad Guys Close In (pp. 55-75), All Is Lost (p. 75) and Dark Night of the Soul (pp. 75-85) (Snyder, ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!").
  - Snyder's "whiff of death" at All Is Lost, the old self dying and a mentor's death being typical, matches Harmon's "price."
  - Snyder even calls All Is Lost the "Christ on the cross" moment, the same image Harmon uses for step 6.
  - Snyder's Dark Night (hero beaten, then digging deep) parallels Harmon's love-story depression and realization.
- **Truby:** ⚠️ step 11, Opponent's plan and main counterattack (PDF p. 128); step 13, Attack by ally; step 14, Apparent defeat (PDF p. 129); step 15, Second revelation and decision (PDF p. 130); step 16, Audience revelation; step 17, Third revelation and decision (PDF p. 131).
  - Harmon's moment when "something matters more than me" resembles the change of desire and motive in Truby's second revelation and decision.
- **Bell:** ⚠️ the master's Q-Factor falls within this span. The gap analysis lists Bell's Mounting Forces and Lights Out as Act III signposts, but their meanings are not checked here.
- **Weiland:** ⚠️ the Second Pinch Point, the "Later False Peak" and the Third Plot Point, as named in the master.
- **Sequence approach:** ⚠️ the main tension resolved, for now (StudioBinder's vocabulary).
