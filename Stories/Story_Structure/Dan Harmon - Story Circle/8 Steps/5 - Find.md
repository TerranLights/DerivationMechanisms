# Find (Dan Harmon's Story Circle, step 5)

**Source:** Harmon:

- SS103;
- SS104, section "5. 'Find' - Meeting with the Goddess";
- SS105, the TV version;
- SS106, entry 5.

Also the StudioBinder Workbook (step 5) and Worksheet. Short citations follow the key in `../01 - Overview and principles.md`.

Research extraction, 2026-10-29. The source's ideas in our own words; not the author's adjusted version.

## What it is
At step 5 the protagonist **gets what they wanted** (SS101). It may not match what they consciously set out to get (SS104). Whether or not it was their conscious goal, the need from step 2 is now met (SS106).

Harmon names the step after Campbell's **Meeting with the Goddess**, deferring to Campbell's longer thought on the subject, and notes that Syd Field calls it the midpoint (SS104). The road of trials existed to prepare the hero for this meeting. He likens the moment to a single sperm reaching the egg (SS104).

It is a **pivot point** at the very bottom of the circle.
- The protagonist has tumbled from the top all the way down, and the pull that dragged him down has done its work.
- For a while there is **weightlessness**: anything can happen.
- It is the moment for big reveals and for the hero to be wholly exposed. In a twisty thriller, this is the place to twist, and twist hard (SS104).
- It is also a **second threshold**: everything after it heads the other way, upward. But the hero is not hauled through it. He lingers, chooses, and then rises (SS104).

In the circle's symmetry, step 5 faces step 1.
- Step 1 was the mother's arms. Step 5 is a new, unconscious form of that mother, and the hero is often tempted to remain. Harmon's example is the elf's house in *The Lord of the Rings*.
- Moving on is the protagonist's own act of will.
- The goddess can destroy non-heroes, or soothe them into staying forever, like the sirens whose waters were full of wrecks (SS104).

## How it works
- **It can be anything, good or bad, often both.** Harmon's lake parable (SS104):
  - You stand on a pier (1).
  - You notice something glinting under the water (2).
  - Leaning out to look, you fall in (3).
  - You sink deeper and deeper (4).
  - On the lakebed you finally see what was catching the light (5): a skull, a necklace, a tiny alien craft, or a quarter.
- **The goddess need not be a woman.**
  - In a detective story or a spy adventure, the meeting may be literal: an intimate encounter with a powerful, mysterious woman, a release after a stretch of fighting.
  - But in a single-sex stage play set at a card game, the "goddess" might be one player confessing he has been fired.
  - It can be an object, a place, a thought, a gesture, or a brief escape from whatever pursues the hero (SS104).
- **The *Die Hard* example** (SS104).
  - Having run across broken glass, McClane sits in a bathroom soaking his bleeding feet.
  - He finally understands how much he loves his wife, and what he has done wrong in the marriage: his stubbornness (the flaw of steps 1 and 2).
  - Using the walkie-talkie he picked up at step 4, he sends her a message through his gentle, married, gun-shy police contact on the ground: that she has never heard him apologize.
- **The hacking and slashing is over, at least for now.** Fighting through symbol after symbol is a process. Step 5 is a distinct turn that ends it (SS104).
- **"Major" is relative to the circle.** In the flat-tire anecdote, the man reaches the spare tire under the junk and gives a tiny sigh of relief. For a story that size, that is all the goddess required (SS104).
- **Make it a definable moment.** Harmon's non-romantic example (SS104):
  - A poor girl (1) dreams of wealth (2), is adopted by a millionaire (3), and grows used to luxury (4).
  - At 5 she has become a bit of a snob.
  - Show it in one concrete moment, for example riding past the orphanage in her limousine.
- **Quick examples** (SS106): the princess is found; a suspect reveals where the drug lab is; a nerd becomes popular.
- **What the StudioBinder Workbook says.**
  - At step 5 the protagonist finds what they think they need, but it doesn't turn out as expected, and they must pay a devastating price for it.
  - Its *Dark Knight* example: Bruce works out that he can stop the Joker by unmasking himself, but Harvey Dent does it first (SB Workbook, step 5).
  - ⚠️ StudioBinder moves the price into step 5. Harmon puts it at step 6.
- **TV version.** In the sitcom version, the change succeeds to some degree, which sets up the "but" of step 6 (SS105).

## Placement
- **On the circle:** the six o'clock point, the bottom of the vertical line. It is the deepest point of the special world, the unconscious and chaos, and the hinge between the descending (reactive) and ascending (active) halves (SS101; SS104).
- **In time:** the exact midpoint. It ends the second quarter, around 150 seconds into a five-minute pilot (SS106). ⚠️ 50%.
- **Syd Field:** the midpoint (SS106).

## Equivalents in other frameworks
- **Campbell:** Meeting with the Goddess (SS104; SS106). The SS104 diagram also puts **the Ultimate Boon** here. ⚠️ Campbell's own order places the Boon after Atonement and Apotheosis.
  - Harmon's talk of the temptation to linger with the goddess touches two stages he doesn't name: Woman as the Temptress and Refusal of the Return. ⚠️
- **Vogler:** Harmon gives "Approach to the Innermost Cave" (SS106). ⚠️ In Vogler's own sequence the Approach comes before the central Ordeal.
- **Snyder:** Midpoint (p. 55) (Snyder, ch. 4 "Let's Beat It Out!").
  - Snyder's "false victory" form, where the hero seems to get everything he wanted, is the closest match to Harmon's "get what they wanted."
  - Snyder also says the stakes are raised and the fun and games end here.
  - Snyder's false-defeat form fits the darker finds in Harmon's lake parable, such as the skull. ⚠️
- **Truby:** ⚠️ no midpoint step.
  - His variant of the apparent defeat, the "apparent victory," where the hero reaches a peak and enters a subworld of temporary freedom (PDF p. 130), resembles Harmon's tempting goddess.
  - Truby places it later, at about two-thirds to three-quarters.
  - McClane's insight at this step also resembles one of Truby's revelations (steps 9, 15 or 17, PDF pp. 126-131).
- **Bell:** ⚠️ the Mirror Moment. As the composite master describes it, the hero confronts who he has become. That fits Harmon's call for big reveals and exposure at this step, and McClane's bathroom realization.
- **Weiland:** ⚠️ the Midpoint.
- **Sequence approach:** ⚠️ the midpoint or first culmination.
