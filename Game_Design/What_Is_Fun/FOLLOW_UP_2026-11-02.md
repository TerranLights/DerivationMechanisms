This is the agenda for turning the research into the mechanic. The research round of November 1, 2026 is finished. The full evidence is in `research/00_synthesis.md`, which draws on the three job files and lists all 24 refinement options.

## 1. Verdict per claim

| The author's claim | Verdict | In short |
|---|---|---|
| **H1.** The smallest unit is an effortful challenge. | Partly supported | It explains contest, skill and puzzle fun well, but not all fun. Chance, the thrill of speed, curiosity, company, rest and humor are fun with no effortful challenge. In one study, only 22.6% of people describing something fun called it challenging. |
| **H2.** It is usually not damaging. | Partly as worded; strong if restated | What matters is *feeling* safe or in control: confidence in one's skill and gear, or stakes one chose. The author's rock-climbing point fits here. |
| **H3.** Overcoming it pays off. | Supported, two corrections | The payoff is inner: triumph, mastery, insight. Much of it comes during the effort. Close games beat blowouts, and suspense drives it. |
| **H4.** The payoff is proportional to the challenge. | Partly supported | Pride grows with difficulty almost automatically. Outside rewards like loot or money need their own rule: tie them to performance. Too little hurts more than too much. |
| **H4a.** Too little reward makes it work. | Not supported as the definition of work | What turns play into work is obligation, being watched, or an outside reward becoming the reason. Twain's *Tom Sawyer* says the same. Too little reward makes something "not worth it," which is a different failure. |
| **H4b.** Too much reward feels cheap. | Partly supported | Experienced or invested players feel it when they can *see* the win was unearned. New and casual players often enjoy easy wins, and hidden help felt earned. |
| **H5.** Lasting fun is a chain of units. | Supported, mostly by designers | The sources add variation, loops inside loops, fresh material, and rhythm: rising demand broken by short rests beat a constant level. |
| **H6.** It must fit the person. | Supported, with refinements | Effects are real but small. Current state, stakes, experience and age matter alongside personality. |

## 2. Two things the theory doesn't name
All three jobs kept finding these:
- **An outcome that is genuinely uncertain.** Enjoyment and motivation peak when the result is in real doubt.
- **Free choice to take part.** Choice, more than reward size, is what separates play from work.

## 3. The strongest refinements (options, not decisions)
The full list of 24 is in `research/00_synthesis.md`, section 3.
- Scope the unit to "challenge fun," and name the other kinds of fun beside it, each perhaps with its own unit.
- Or widen "challenge" to "a tension that resolves." Effort is then one way it resolves, and chance another.
- Replace "non-damaging" with feeling safe.
- Replace "proportional" with "felt as earned."

## 4. Questions for the author
1. **What is the unit for?** Is it meant to define what fun *is*, or to be a design tool that reliably produces one kind of fun? The evidence supports the second much better.
2. **Which word?** One research tradition calls distraction "fun" and absorption "pleasure." The theory reads closer to "pleasure." Which word should the mechanic use?

## 5. Other notes
- **No-level-scaling law:** the research turned up nothing that conflicts with it. Difficulty that adjusted itself to the player did not raise enjoyment in the tests found, and people given free choice moved up to harder tasks on their own.
