## 3. Findings

### Topic 1. Animal play

**1.1 Burghardt's five criteria name a low-stress setting as a condition of play.**
- Source: Gordon M. Burghardt, 2010, "The Comparative Reach of Play and Brain: Perspective, Evidence, and Implications," *American Journal of Play* 2(3): 338-356. https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/2-3-article-comparative-reach-play-and-brain.pdf (full text). The article restates the criteria from *The Genesis of Animal Play: Testing the Limits* (2005), which was not opened.
- Kind: THEORY (a definitional framework built on comparative observation).
- What it says: the fifth criterion is that play starts when an animal is "adequately fed, healthy, and free from acute or chronic stress (starvation, predator threat, harsh microclimate, social instability)." He adds that mild stress, "including boredom and social deprivation, may facilitate play." His one-line summary ends: play is "initiated voluntarily when the animal (or person) is in a relaxed or low-stress setting." The first criterion is that play is "not fully functional" in its context. He leaves pleasure and fun out of the criteria on purpose, because they are hard to judge in turtles or fish.
- Note: the phrase "relaxed field" from the brief does not appear in this article; it says "relaxed or low-stress setting." Whether "relaxed field" is Burghardt's own phrase was not verified.
- Bears on: H2 (also H1).
- Direction: **confirms H2, with a refinement.** The safety condition is about the player's state and surroundings (needs met, no real threat present), not about whether the act itself is gentle. Play fighting is rough, yet it meets the criteria.

**1.2 Real threat switches play off in rats, but play bounces back fast.**
- Source: Stephen M. Siviy, 2010, "Play and Adversity: How the Playful Mammalian Brain Withstands Threats and Anxieties," *American Journal of Play* 2(3): 297-314. https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/2-3-article-play-and-adversity.pdf (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (lab experiments, his own and those he reviews).
- What it says: in an experiment Panksepp reported in 1998, play "disappeared" the day a tuft of cat fur was placed in the test chamber, and rats did not play in that space for three to five days afterward. Cat odor suppresses play and raises defensive behavior. After 24 hours without food, play "virtually disappears," but after one 20-minute meal rats play at their old levels (Siviy and Panksepp 1985). In his studies about 75 percent of pinning happened inside a hide box, which suggests rats choose places that lower predation risk. Under milder disruption, rats keep playing while scanning for threat.
- Bears on: H2.
- Direction: **confirms H2.** Real danger, felt by the animal, ends play. It also refines it: what counts is the danger the animal perceives (a smell, with no cat present, is enough).

**1.3 Rats build loss of control into their play on purpose, inside a safe frame.**
- Source: Sergio M. Pellis, Vivien C. Pellis and Heather C. Bell, 2010, "The Function of Play in the Development of the Social Brain," *American Journal of Play* 2(3): 278-296. https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/2-3-article-function-play-development-social-brain.pdf (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (observational lab data) and THEORY (the "training for the unexpected" hypothesis of Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff 2001).
- What it says: juvenile rats favor a supine defense that hands the initiative to the partner. The rat on top often stands with all four feet on the partner, which makes a counterattack by the one below much more likely. The authors conclude that both players "diminish their dominance over the partner's actions," and that play fighting is "designed to ensure that juveniles frequently experience an unpredictable loss of control." Rats denied juvenile play grow up more fearful and stress-reactive.
- Bears on: H2, H1, H4.
- Direction: **refines H2.** Play is not the absence of difficulty or of loss of control. Animals add difficulty and unpredictability to it themselves (self-handicapping), but only inside a frame where nothing serious follows (nape contact is a gentle nuzzle; real fights target the rump).

**1.4 The "70/30" play-fighting rule: the popular version is not traced to a primary source.**
- Sources:
  - "Play-fighting in Male Norway Rats," ratbehavior.org (an unsigned, curated summary of the literature, copyright 2003-2004), https://ratbehavior.org/RatPlay.htm (full text, fetched despite an expired certificate). It summarizes Panksepp 1981 (*Developmental Psychobiology*) and Pellis and Pellis 1987, 1991 and 1992.
  - Pellis, Pellis, Ham and Achterberg, 2022, "The rough-and-tumble play of rats as a natural behavior suitable for studying the social brain," *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* 16:1033999. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9623181/ (full text).
  - Jordan Peterson, *12 Rules for Life* (2018), Rule 1 (search summary only; the book was not opened).
- Kind: MEASURED for the pinning data; CLAIM for the popular rule.
- What they say:
  - The 70 percent figure is real but means something else. Panksepp (1981) found that from weaning to sexual maturity pinning between paired males grew lopsided, until "one animal pinned the other on average 70% of the time" (ratbehavior.org, summarizing).
  - Pellis and Pellis (1991) then found a twist: the rat pinned more as a juvenile tended to become the dominant adult, because it learned better counterattacks. As adults, subordinates start most play attacks and dominants tend to evade them (1992).
  - Pellis et al. (2022) say only that "the player with the advantage may voluntarily relinquish it," which allows a role reversal. They give no threshold.
  - A search summary (not verified in an opened source) gave about 30 percent role reversals at the juvenile peak, and a 30 versus 70 percent counterattack-success difference depending on whether the top rat anchors its hind feet. Pellis et al. 2010 (finding 1.3) confirm the direction of the feet effect without the numbers.
  - The popular rule, that the smaller rat stops inviting play unless it wins at least 30 percent of bouts, appears in Peterson (2018). No opened source states that refusal threshold.
  - Adam Omary, April 15, 2023, "Tickling Rats in the Name of Science," https://psychology.humanprogress.org/p/tickling-rats-in-the-name-of-science (full text; a popular article, first run in *Psychology Today* in September 2022), writes that he "read all of Panksepp's original research on rat play, and wasn't able to find evidence for this." He gives the 70 percent pinning figure from Panksepp et al. (1984) and describes dominant rats taking postures that make it easier for the subordinate to flip them (Pellis et al. 2005), which he calls self-handicapping.
- Bears on: H2, H4.
- Direction: **neutral on the claims; the popular attribution is marked unverified.** What does hold is that reciprocity and role reversal are part of play fighting, and that partners who play too roughly risk losing their partners (Pellis et al. 2022).

**1.5 Dogs and coyotes keep play safe with signals and self-handicapping; unfair players are shunned.**
- Source: Marc Bekoff and Jessica Pierce, March 19, 2010, "The Ethical Dog," *Scientific American Mind*. Read as a two-page course copy, https://jsc.cc.ntu.edu.tw/~karchung/DFLLfeF09/Dogfairness.pdf (full text). Bekoff 2001, "Social play behaviour: cooperation, fairness, trust, and the evolution of morality," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 8(2): 81-90, was found but only its bibliographic page opened.
- Kind: MEASURED (field and video data, reported in a popular article; the primary data were not opened).
- What it says: the play bow is "highly stereotyped," so its message is clear. After a bow followed by bites or growls, partners show submission or avoidance "only around 15 percent of the time." Animals "engage in self-handicapping and role reversing to create and maintain equal footing." Juvenile coyotes "who do not play fair" often leave the pack and are "up to four times more likely to die."
- Bears on: H2, H4, H6.
- Direction: **confirms H2, refined.** The frame that keeps rough play safe is made and kept by signals and trust between players. When one player breaks it, play stops. Self-handicapping also matches the challenge to the partner, a parallel to H4 and H6.

**1.6 Rats emit "laughter" calls during play and tickling.**
- Source: J. Panksepp and J. Burgdorf, 2003, "'Laughing' rats and the evolutionary antecedents of human joy?" *Physiology & Behavior* 79(3): 533-547. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12954448/ (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What it says: play- and tickle-induced ~50-kHz chirps "may have more than a passing resemblance to primitive human laughter," and the authors propose that they "reflect a type of positive affect." A search summary (not checked in the paper) adds that tickled rats ran mazes and pressed levers to be tickled.
- Bears on: H3, H1.
- Direction: **neutral on H2; a test case for H1** (see Topic 8). Being tickled is rewarding to the rat, yet it is hard to call it a challenge the tickled rat overcomes.

### Topic 2. The protective frame

**2.1 Reversal theory: the same arousal is pleasant inside a protective frame and unpleasant outside it.**
- Sources: Jonathan R. Males, John H. Kerr and Joanne Hudson, 2015, "Elaborating the Mastery State and the Confidence Frame," *Journal of Motivation, Emotion, and Personality* 4: 33-44. https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/1456/1/2015-v4-05-Males.pdf (full text). Apter's own books (1982; *The Dangerous Edge: The Psychology of Excitement*, Free Press, 1992; *Danger: Our Quest for Excitement*, Oneworld, 2007) were not opened. A university archive copy of *Danger* was found but blocked by a bot check.
- Kind: THEORY.
- What it says: in reversal theory, the playful (paratelic) state needs a "phenomenological protective frame." Inside it, high arousal is felt as excitement; in the serious (telic) state, the same arousal is felt as anxiety. The confidence frame is a kind of protective frame that Apter (1992) used for risk takers who are aware of immediate danger but "confident of avoiding trauma." Apter "made it clear that ability is a pre-requisite for confidence." Males et al. also criticize the idea: Apter "did not make clear just what a confidence frame is," nor what causes it. In an example from Kerr and Mackenzie (2012), a hang-glider pilot's confidence frame rested on "high personal skills," safety knowledge, "confidence in the structure and capability of his equipment," and a sense that the risks "were manageable."
- Bears on: H2, H6.
- Direction: **refines H2.** In this theory the condition is a felt frame of safety or confidence, not the absence of actual danger. The theory's critics within the field note the frame is loosely defined.

**2.2 Regular risk-sport athletes score as arousal seekers.**
- Source: John H. Kerr, 1991, "Arousal-seeking in risk sport participants," *Personality and Individual Differences* 12(6): 613-616. Record with abstract at https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU199502056839 (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (three samples, Telic Dominance Scale).
- What it says: Australian surfers and sailboarders scored lower in telic dominance than weight trainers; Dutch parachutists and motorcycle racers scored lower on arousal avoidance than marathon runners; British glider pilots scored lower than the general public. The author concludes that "individuals who perform risk sports on a regular basis are arousal seekers."
- Bears on: H6, H2.
- Direction: **confirms H6.** People who choose danger for fun differ in temperament from those who don't.

**2.3 An expert skydiver felt his sport was safe.**
- Source: John H. Kerr and Susan Houge Mackenzie, 2014, "Confidence frames and the mastery of new challenges in the motivation of an expert skydiver," *The Sport Psychologist* 28(3): 221-232. Record at https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201502001621/Details (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (a single-case qualitative study).
- What it says: the skydiver saw skydiving as safe rather than hazardous. "Maintaining control and mastery during skydiving was a key motivational element," and pursuing new challenges and skills kept him in the sport.
- Related, search summary only: Kerr (2007), "Sudden withdrawal from skydiving: A case study informed by reversal theory's concept of protective frames," *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 19: 337-351, used protective frames to explain an athlete quitting a risk sport suddenly. The details were not read.
- Bears on: H2.
- Direction: **refines H2.** The danger is real and on record (see 4.1), but the practitioner felt safe, through mastery.

### Topic 3. Safe threat

**3.1 Benign masochism: people enjoy negative feelings when they know they are safe, often up to just below what they can stand.**
- Source: Paul Rozin, Lily Guillot, Katrina Fincher, Alexander Rozin and Eli Tsukayama, 2013, "Glad to be sad, and other examples of benign masochism," *Judgment and Decision Making* 8(4): 439-447. https://journal.sjdm.org/12/12502a/jdm12502a.html (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (surveys of 243 University of Pennsylvania undergraduates and 147 Mechanical Turk adults).
- What it says:
  - The authors define benign masochism as enjoying negative experiences "that the body (brain) falsely interprets as threatening." The pleasure comes from "mind over body," which "can also be framed as a type of mastery."
  - Twenty-nine aversive activities gave pleasure to many people. Thrill rides were among the best liked (mean 56.5 of 100; 64 percent above 50).
  - 59 percent agreed that their favorite roller coaster "is the scariest I can put up with." Among those who liked thrill rides, 82 percent agreed.
  - Liking scores were not normally distributed: people clustered at both strong like and strong dislike.
  - The authors left out activities "that really do signal danger," those "without a fully established 'protective frame'," such as parachuting and cliff climbing. They suggest these are enjoyed "presumably for the same reason," which is a guess, not a result.
- Bears on: H2, H6, H4.
- Direction: **confirms H2 for this class of experiences:** the pleasure depends on knowing there is no real danger. It also **confirms H6** (the split into likers and dislikers) and fits a ceiling effect: the preferred dose sits just below the tolerable limit.

**3.2 Haunted house: enjoyment peaks at a middle level of fear.**
- Source: Andersen, Schjoedt, Price, Rosas, Scrivner and Clasen, 2020, "Playing With Fear: A Field Study in Recreational Horror," *Psychological Science* 31(12): 1497-1510. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734554 (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (field study at a commercial haunted house in Vejle, Denmark; 110 recruited, 92 analyzed; heart-rate monitors, self-reports, coded video).
- What it says: there was "a significant quadratic relationship between enjoyment and self-reported fear" at the three filmed scare locations: an inverted U. Small, second-scale heart-rate swings also related to enjoyment in an inverted U. The authors write that enjoyment was tied to "just-right forms of arousal dynamics." For the attraction as a whole there was no significant linear relationship.
- Bears on: H2, H4.
- Direction: **refines H2.** Too little fear is dull; too much spoils it. The threat in a haunted house is safe, so this tests the dose of safe threat, not real danger.

**3.3 Horror fans come in three kinds, and they get different things from it.**
- Source: Coltan Scrivner, Marc Andersen, Uffe Schjødt and Mathias Clasen, 2023, "The Psychological Benefits of Scary Play in Three Types of Horror Fans," *Journal of Media Psychology* 35(2): 87-98. https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/the-psychological-benefits-of-scary-play-in-three-types-of-horror/ (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (American horror fans, n = 256; Danish haunted-house visitors, n = 258).
- What it says: both samples showed the same three types, which the authors call Adrenaline Junkies, White Knucklers and Dark Copers. "Adrenaline Junkies reported immediate enjoyment, White Knucklers reported personal growth, and Dark Copers reported both." The authors conclude that horror's allure "may have as much to do with learning and personal growth as it has with high-arousal fun."
- Bears on: H6, H3.
- Direction: **confirms H6.** The same activity serves different people for different reasons. For one type (White Knucklers) the payoff is growth, not fun in the moment.

**3.4 Sensation seeking and imagination predict liking horror.**
- Source: Mathias Clasen, Jens Kjeldgaard-Christiansen and John A. Johnson, 2020, "Horror, Personality, and Threat Simulation: A Survey on the Psychology of Scary Media," *Evolutionary Behavioral Sciences* (volume and pages not confirmed). Read through PsyPost's report, https://www.psypost.org/study-suggests-the-paradoxical-enjoyment-of-horror-media-serves-an-evolutionary-function/ (secondary report), and a search summary of the abstract. The paper itself was not opened.
- Kind: MEASURED (online survey, n of about 1,070).
- What it says: sensation seeking strongly predicted enjoying horror, how often people watched it, and a taste for high-intensity horror. Intellect/imagination (the Big Five's fifth factor) also predicted liking. According to PsyPost, 55 percent said they enjoy the genre.
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **confirms H6.**

### Topic 4. Real danger chosen for fun

This topic got full weight after the author's note: rock climbing can kill a climber whose rope fails, yet many people call it fun. The question is whether H2 holds, or whether the real condition is perceived control, a protective frame, or confidence in skill and equipment. A second question is whether the risk is part of the fun or merely tolerated.

**4.1 The danger is real, and it varies a lot by activity.**
- Sources and kind (all MEASURED):
  - Schöffl, Morrison, Schwarz, Schöffl and Küpper, 2010, "Evaluation of injury and fatality risk in rock and ice climbing," *Sports Medicine* 40(8): 657-679. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20632737/ (abstract). A review of over 400 studies, scored per 1,000 hours. Indoor climbing, sport climbing and bouldering showed "a small injury rate, minor injury severity and few fatalities." Alpine and ice climbing, which face more "objective/external dangers," had higher injury rates, severity and death. Climbing overall had lower injury incidence and severity than basketball, sailing or soccer. Indoor climbing ranked lowest of all sports assessed.
  - Firth et al., 2008, "Mortality on Mount Everest, 1921-2006," *BMJ* 337: a2654. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2602730 (abstract). Of 14,138 mountaineers, "the mortality rate among mountaineers above base camp was 1.3%." On descent from the summit by standard routes, 2.7 percent of climbers died (43 of 1,585).
  - United States Parachute Association (Jim Crouch), April 6, 2025, "A Milestone in Safety: The 2024 Fatality Summary." https://www.uspa.org/a-milestone-in-safetythe-2024-fatality-summary (publisher page). There were nine U.S. deaths in 3.88 million skydives in 2024, or ".23 fatalities per 100,000 skydives." The rate in 1961 was about 11.1 per 100,000.
  - Tofler, Hyatt and Tofler, 2018, "Psychiatric Aspects of Extreme Sports: Three Case Studies," *The Permanente Journal* 22: 17-071. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5798939 (full text; a secondary table whose primary sources were not opened). It lists BASE jumping at "1 death/2317 jumps" (from the Kjerag study, Søreide et al. 2007) and rock climbing at "1 death/320,000 climbs" for 1998-2011. A hang-gliding figure in the same table (1 death per 560 flights) looks implausible and is not relied on.
  - Brymer and Schweitzer (2013a, see 4.4), citing Storry (2003), give a U.K. climbing death rate of 1 in 4,000, against 1 in 500 for motorcycle riding. The unit of that ratio was not stated in the passage read, and Storry was not opened.
- Bears on: H2.
- Direction: **refutes H2 as a statement about actual danger,** for a minority of activities people pursue for pleasure. Mountaineering above base camp (1.3 percent died) and BASE jumping are plainly lethal at times. It also shows that most climbing, the author's example, is far safer than its image: indoor and sport climbing are low-injury sports.

**4.2 Edgework: some people seek the edge where a mistake means death, and the skill to handle it is the point.**
- Sources: S. Lyng, 1990, "Edgework: A Social Psychological Analysis of Voluntary Risk Taking," *American Journal of Sociology* 95(4): 851-886, https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/229379 (the page returned 403; read only through secondary sources). Main secondary: Philip A. Mellor and Chris Shilling, 2021, "Edgework, Uncertainty, and Social Character," *Sociological Research Online* 26(4): 959-975, https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/180155/3/13607804211002841.pdf (full text).
- Kind: THEORY (sociological).
- What it says: in Mellor and Shilling's summary, Lyng's edgework covers pursuits like parachuting, rock climbing and BASE jumping, where failure to negotiate the edge would mean "death or debilitating injury" (Lyng 1990; Lyng and Snow 1986). The skills used to negotiate such risks appealed because they gave "identity-affirming breaks" from routine work and social conformity. A second secondary source, Irina Obeada, James Cronin and Maria Piacentini, 2025, "From Edgework to Edgeworking: The Interplay of Risk and the Life Course," *Sociology* (accepted preprint, https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/230262/, full text), quotes Lyng with page numbers: edgework is "controlling the seemingly uncontrollable" (p. 872); it involves "a clearly observable threat to one's physical or mental wellbeing or one's sense of an ordered existence" (p. 857); and "crowding the edge" (p. 860) means using skill to get as close to the edge as possible without going over. Obeada et al. add that edgework must be told apart from "nihilistic self-destruction," because it requires skills for keeping control. Mellor and Shilling also cite Elias and Dunning (*Quest for Excitement*, 1986) on leisure as the "controlled-decontrolling" of emotions.
- Bears on: H2, H6.
- Direction: **refutes H2 for edgeworkers, and refines it:** for them the possibility of death is not incidental; skilled control near it is what the activity is about.

**4.3 Skydivers sustain an "illusion of control."**
- Source: Jason Laurendeau, 2006, "'He didn't go in doing a skydive': Sustaining the illusion of control in an edgework activity," *Sociological Perspectives* 49(4): 583-605 (search summary of the abstract only; the university page opened without the abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (ethnography).
- What it says: skydivers build and keep the belief that they can stay in control while working their edge. When that belief becomes untenable, they put certain hazards down to "fate," as outside the sport, which keeps their sense of control intact.
- Bears on: H2.
- Direction: **refines H2.** What must hold is the belief that one is in control, kept up partly by how participants explain accidents. It need not match the actual odds.

**4.4 Many extreme-sport athletes say the risk is not what they seek; some say danger adds to the experience.**
- Sources: E. Brymer and R. Schweitzer, 2013a, "The search for freedom in extreme sports: A phenomenological exploration," *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 14(6): 865-873, https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/2606/ (full text, accepted version). Brymer and Schweitzer, 2013b, "Extreme sports are good for your health: a phenomenological understanding of fear and anxiety in extreme sport," *Journal of Health Psychology* 18(4): 477-487, https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/2607/ (full text, accepted version).
- Kind: MEASURED (interviews with 15 BASE jumpers, big-wave surfers, extreme mountaineers and skiers, free-solo climbers and waterfall kayakers).
- What they say:
  - The authors define extreme sports as those where "the most likely outcome of a mismanaged mistake or accident is death."
  - Motives "do not simply mirror traditional images of risk taking and adrenaline." Participants describe freedom, including "freedom as letting go of the need for control."
  - Participants prepare hard (skills, equipment, knowledge of the environment) and then accept that they have little control at key moments. One kayaker called it "managed non-control."
  - The authors write that "the scent of danger is experienced as contributing to the experience as opposed to detracting from the experience."
  - They quote Pain and Pain (2005): climbers "do not want to put their lives in danger by going beyond personal capabilities."
  - Fear is intense and present. One participant's slogan for these sports was "Oh please-don't let me die!"
- Bears on: H2, H6, H3.
- Direction: **refutes H2 in its plain form, and complicates the "perceived control" refinement.** These athletes know the activity can kill them. They do not deny the danger; they prepare for it and then surrender to it. For them danger is part of the experience, not just tolerated. Note that they rarely describe it as "fun"; they speak of freedom, clarity and meaning.

**4.5 Elite winter climbers: goal achievement inside a confidence frame, not thrill seeking.**
- Source: Gareth Jones, James Milligan, David Llewellyn, Adam Gledhill and Mark I. Johnson, 2017, "Motivational orientation and risk taking in elite winter climbers: A qualitative study," *International Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 15(1): 25-40. https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/1499/ (full text, accepted version).
- Kind: MEASURED (interviews with four elite male climbers, aged 42-49).
- What it says: two themes emerged, "enactive mastery" and "engendered disinhibition." "Goal achievement was their primary motive which was set within a confidence frame." Self-mastery included the sub-themes "perceived control" and "risk acceptance." The excitement "did not seem to relate to a form of thrill seeking"; it "seemed to stem from the inherently challenging nature of the activities." One climber returned to a route where a fall "could be fatal" and described considerable anxiety.
- Bears on: H2, H1.
- Direction: **refines H2.** For these men the danger is accepted, not sought, and the draw is the challenge. Confidence built from past success (the confidence frame) is what lets them go on.

**4.6 Different dangerous sports serve different motives.**
- Source: M. D. Barlow, T. Woodman and L. J. Hardy, 2013, "Great expectations: Different high-risk activities satisfy different motives," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 105(3): 458-475. https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/great-expectations-different-high-risk-activities-satisfy-differe/ (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (questionnaire studies; development of the SEAS scale).
- What it says: "The motive for skydiving is strongly associated with sensation seeking," while "the motive for mountaineering is strongly associated with emotion regulation and agency but not with sensation seeking." The authors conclude that researchers "should no longer consider risk takers as a homogenous sensation-seeking group."
- Bears on: H2, H6.
- Direction: **refines H2 and confirms H6.** Whether the thrill of risk is itself the draw depends on the activity and the person. For skydivers it looks like part of the fun; for mountaineers the draw is agency and emotion regulation.

**4.7 People in high-risk sports differ in personality.**
- Source: McEwan, Boudreau, Curran and Rhodes, 2019, "Personality traits of high-risk sport participants: A meta-analysis," *Journal of Research in Personality* 79: 83-93. https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/personality-traits-of-high-risk-sport-participants-a-meta-analysi/ (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (meta-analysis, 149 effect sizes from 39 articles).
- What it says: high-risk participants scored higher on "sensation seeking, extraversion, and impulsivity." Comparison groups scored higher on "neuroticism, telic dominance, and sensitivity to punishment." There was no difference in agreeableness, conscientiousness or openness.
- Bears on: H6, H2.
- Direction: **confirms H6.** The taste for dangerous fun runs with temperament. Lower neuroticism and lower sensitivity to punishment may be part of why the danger feels acceptable.

**4.8 Sensation seeking is defined by willingness to take risks for intense experience.**
- Source: M. Zuckerman's definition, from *Behavioral Expressions and Biosocial Bases of Sensation Seeking* (Cambridge University Press, 1994), as quoted in secondary sources (search summary only, citing Wikipedia, "Sensation seeking"). The book was not opened.
- Kind: THEORY.
- What it says: sensation seeking is "the seeking of varied, novel, complex, and intense sensations and experiences, and the willingness to take physical, social, legal, and financial risks for the sake of such experience."
- Bears on: H6, H2.
- Direction: **refines H2.** By definition, for high sensation seekers risk is a price they will pay for intensity, not the goal itself. Jones et al. (4.5) note that some scales are biased for risk-sport samples, because their items ask about the very activities being studied.

**4.9 On a Himalayan expedition, risk played a minor role and flow came from challenge matched to skill.**
- Source: Delle Fave, Bassi and Massimini, 2003, "Quality of experience and risk perception in high-altitude rock climbing," *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 15(1): 82-98 (search summary only; the repository returned 403).
- Kind: MEASURED (experience sampling of six climbers over 39 days).
- What it says (per the summary): flow was reported when both challenges and skills were high. "Risk played a minor role," and the authors advise separating risk from the search for challenge.
- Bears on: H2, H1.
- Direction: **refines H2.** The climbers sought challenge; the risk came with it.

**4.10 Adventure athletes have many motives; risk taking is one of them.**
- Source: John H. Kerr and Susan Houge Mackenzie, 2012, "Multiple motives for participating in adventure sports," *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 13(5): 649-657 (search summary of the abstract only).
- Kind: MEASURED (qualitative interviews with very experienced river surfers, mountain bikers, kayakers, mountain climbers and hang-glider pilots).
- What it says (per the summary): motives included goal achievement, risk taking, social motivation, escape from boredom, pushing personal boundaries, overcoming fear, and connecting with nature. The authors argue against reducing adventure sport to thrill seeking.
- Bears on: H2, H6.
- Direction: **refines H2.** Risk is one ingredient among several, and its weight varies by person.

**4.11 Recreational rock climbers name nature, adventure, personal challenge and fun, not risk, as their main motives.**
- Source: I. López Fernández, L. García Bravo and F. J. Garrido González, 2013, "Study about the Motivations for Going Rock Climbing," *Apunts. Educación Física y Deportes* 113: 23-29. https://revista-apunts.com/en/study-about-the-motivations-for-going-rock-climbing/ (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (questionnaire, 146 climbers: 89 men, 57 women, aged 18-54).
- What it says: the main motives were intrinsic: "contact with nature, the search for adventure, a personal challenge and fun." The least named were image enhancement, competition and social recognition. "Women climbers were more inclined to chose fun and image enhancement as reasons for climbing."
- Bears on: H2, H1, H6.
- Direction: **refines H2.** It supports the author's premise that climbing is widely experienced as fun. It also suggests risk is not what draws most recreational climbers; "personal challenge" is. Risk did not appear among the motives in the abstract.

**4.12 The Adventure Experience Paradigm sets peak adventure where risk and competence balance.**
- Source: Martin and Priest (1986), the Adventure Experience Paradigm (search summary only; the original was not opened, and its exact title was not confirmed). A field test, C. D. Jones and S. J. Hollenhurst (as the repository spells it), 1998, "Validating Predictors to Determine Optimal Adventure in Whitewater Kayaking," *Research in Outdoor Education* 4(1): 77-78, https://digitalcommons.cortland.edu/reseoutded/vol4/iss1/9, was found, but only its stated aims were read, not its results.
- Kind: THEORY (outdoor-education practice model).
- What it says (per the summary): five levels set by risk against competence: exploration and experimentation (low risk, high competence), adventure, peak adventure (risk and competence in balance), misadventure (risk a little above competence, with minor injuries such as a fall while climbing), and disaster and devastation (major injury or death).
- Bears on: H2, H4.
- Direction: **refines H2.** This model from outdoor-education practice puts the best experience at a balance of real risk and competence, not at zero risk. It also treats some damage (misadventure) as part of the learning range.

**Topic 4 summary: is the risk part of the fun or merely tolerated?** The evidence splits by person and by activity.
- **Tolerated, or not even named:** for elite winter climbers (4.5), for climbers on expedition (4.9), and for mountaineers generally (4.6), risk is accepted as the price of a hard challenge. Recreational climbers do not name risk among their main motives at all (4.11).
- **Part of the experience:** for skydivers (4.6), for edgeworkers (4.2), for extreme athletes who say danger "contributes" (4.4), for adventure athletes who list risk taking among their motives (4.10), and for roller-coaster lovers whose favorite ride is the scariest they can bear (3.1).
- **What it rests on:** in every case, people keep a felt sense that they will come through. They get it from skill, preparation and equipment (2.1, 4.5), from belief in control (4.3), or from accepting fate after full preparation (4.4). Real danger that the person feels is out of their control ends play, in rats (1.2) and in people (Rozin's exclusion, 3.1).

### Topic 5. Player types and motivations

**5.1 Bartle: four kinds of players, each enjoying something different.**
- Source: Richard A. Bartle, April 1996, "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs." https://mud.co.uk/richard/hcds.htm (full text).
- Kind: PRACTITIONER (drawn from a long online debate among senior players of one commercial MUD, November 1989 to May 1990, of "several hundred" posts; not a survey).
- What it says: achievers set game goals and "vigorously set out to achieve them"; explorers try to learn as much as they can about the world; socializers use the game as a place "to converse with their fellow players"; killers use the game's tools "to cause distress to" other players. "Most players leaned at least a little to all four, but each tended to have some particular overall preference," and players drift between styles.
- Bears on: H6, H1.
- Direction: **confirms H6** (the same game pleases people for different reasons). For H1: socializers' fun is talk, not a challenge they overcome; killers' fun is power over others.

**5.2 Yee: motives are separate dimensions that people mix, not exclusive types.**
- Source: Nick Yee, 2006, "Motivations for Play in Online Games," *CyberPsychology & Behavior* 9(6): 772-775. Course copy at https://eclass.uniwa.gr/modules/document/file.php/EEE195/2023-2024/Online%20gaming%20addiction/yee%202006.pdf (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (factor analysis of a survey of about 3,000 MMORPG players).
- What it says: ten motives grouped into three components: achievement, social and immersion. Motivations "do not suppress each other as Bartle suggested": the three components correlate weakly (r < 0.10). Men scored higher on all achievement subcomponents and women on the relationship subcomponent, but "the variation in the achievement component is in fact better explained by age than gender" (standardized coefficients 0.32 for age, 0.16 for gender). Men and women socialized equally, but sought different things in relationships. Escapism was the strongest predictor of problematic use.
- Bears on: H6, H1.
- Direction: **confirms H6, and refines it.** People differ along several independent dimensions at once; a single "type" per person is not supported.

**5.3 Quantic Foundry: twelve motives in three clusters, tied modestly to the Big Five.**
- Source: Quantic Foundry, 2016, gaming-motivation talk slides (file name: "Quantic Foundry 2016 ECGC Gaming Motivation Talk"). https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Quantic-Foundry-2016-ECGC-Gaming-Motivation-Talk.pdf (full text of the slides). The company's blog posts would not load (403).
- Kind: MEASURED (a large self-selected online survey; industry work, not peer reviewed).
- What it says:
  - Sample: 222,964 gamers; 81 percent male; median age 25.
  - Twelve motives in six pairs: Action (Destruction, Excitement), Social (Competition, Community), Mastery (Challenge, Strategy), Achievement (Completion, Power), Immersion (Fantasy, Story), Creativity (Design, Discovery). They form three clusters.
  - "This motivation structure is stable across all the regions we have data for."
  - Big Five links: Excitement (.13), Competition (.15) and Community (.20) with Extraversion; Strategy (.20) with Conscientiousness; Fantasy (.21), Story (.21), Design (.19) and Discovery (.25) with Openness. The slides give no personality correlation for Challenge.
  - The slides' summary: video games "help us to become more of who we really are."
- Bears on: H6, H1.
- Direction: **confirms H6, with small effects.** The correlations are real but modest (none above .25). Challenge is one of twelve motives; Completion (collecting everything) and Community are separate ones.

**5.4 BrainHex: a seven-type model with weak psychometric support.**
- Sources: Nacke, Bateman and Mandryk, 2014, "BrainHex: A neurobiological gamer typology survey," *Entertainment Computing* (search summary only). Busch et al., 2016, initial validation of the BrainHex scale (search summary only).
- Kind: THEORY (the model) and MEASURED (the validation).
- What they say (per the summaries): seven archetypes (Seeker, Survivor, Daredevil, Mastermind, Conqueror, Socialiser, Achiever), surveyed in over 50,000 players. Two validation studies (n = 592 and 243) found the scale's psychometric properties "could be improved."
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **neutral.** The typology fits H6 in spirit, but its measurement is weak.

**5.5 Hexad and the move from player types to player traits.**
- Sources: Gustavo F. Tondello et al., 2016, "The Gamification User Types Hexad Scale," *CHI PLAY Companion '16*, https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/12788 (abstract). Tondello, Valtchanov, Reetz, Wehbe, Orji and Nacke, 2018, "Towards a Trait Model of Video Game Preferences," *International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction*, https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/13718 (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What they say: the 24-item Hexad scale showed "significant associations of the Hexad user types with the Big Five personality traits." The 2018 study, with over 50,000 respondents, found "three player traits: action, esthetic, and goal orientation." It criticizes earlier models for using "type models instead of trait models, contrary to the latest personality research." The traits, with gender and attitudes to story, "partially explain" game preferences.
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **confirms H6 and refines it:** fit is a matter of degree on several traits, and those traits explain only part of what people prefer.

### Topic 6. Personality and the fun of challenge

**6.1 Challenge-skill balance predicts flow only in low-stakes activities, and more for people high in achievement motive.**
- Source: Stefan Engeser and Falko Rheinberg, 2008, "Flow, performance and moderators of challenge-skill balance," *Motivation and Emotion* 32(3): 158-172. https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/bilder/Engeser___Rheinberg_2008.pdf (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (three studies: learning statistics, learning French, playing Pac-Man).
- What it says: the balance assumption "could only be partially supported." It was moderated "by the (perceived) importance of the activity and by the achievement motive." For Pac-Man, which mattered little, flow "was highest when balance was present and low when the demand was too low or too high." For learning statistics, which mattered a lot, "flow was still high when the demand was low." The importance measure included worries about mistakes and failure, so the authors read it as capturing threat. They draw on Atkinson's risk-taking model: people high in hope of success prefer medium difficulty, while people with a strong fear of failure avoid it.
- Bears on: H6, H2, H4.
- Direction: **confirms H6 and links it to H2.** When failure has real consequences, people enjoy tasks where their skill exceeds the challenge. The matched challenge works best when little is at stake. Who enjoys a matched challenge also depends on achievement motive.

**6.2 People with an internal locus of control are most sensitive to challenge-skill fit.**
- Source: J. Keller and F. Blomann, 2008, "Locus of control and the flow experience: An experimental analysis," *European Journal of Personality* 22: 589-607 (search summary only).
- Kind: MEASURED (experiment).
- What it says (per the summary): skills-demands compatibility caused flow, and participants with a strong internal locus of control "were most sensitive" to that effect.
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **confirms H6.** A personality trait changes how much the fit of the challenge matters.

**6.3 The autotelic personality: a rich idea, poorly measured.**
- Source: Nicola Baumann, "Autotelic Personality," Chapter 9 in a Springer volume on flow research (DOI 10.1007/978-1-4614-2359-1_9; year not shown in the author's copy). Author's copy at https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/bilder/Baumann_Flow_Chapter_9_final.pdf (full text).
- Kind: THEORY (review).
- What it says: Csikszentmihalyi defined the autotelic personality as "a disposition to actively seek challenges and flow experiences." He wrote that its mark is "the ability to manage a rewarding balance between the 'play' of challenge finding and the 'work' of skill building" (Csikszentmihalyi et al. 1993, p. 80, as quoted). Baumann judges that "the operationalization of the construct is rather poor."
- Bears on: H6, H1.
- Direction: **confirms H6 in theory; the measurement is weak.** Note for Job 2: in this definition, finding challenges is the "play" and building skill is the "work."

**6.4 Flow proneness runs with low neuroticism and high conscientiousness, and is partly heritable.**
- Sources: Ullén et al., 2012, "Proneness for psychological flow in everyday life: Associations with personality and intelligence," *Personality and Individual Differences* (search summary only). Mosing, Pedersen, Cesarini, Johannesson, Magnusson, Nakamura, Madison and Ullén, 2012, "Genetic and environmental influences on the relationship between flow proneness, locus of control and behavioral inhibition," *PLoS ONE* 7(11): e47958, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3487896 (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What they say: flow proneness was associated with "low neuroticism and high conscientiousness" (Ullén et al., per summary; n = 137), not with intelligence. In a Swedish twin sample (3,375 full pairs), the heritability of flow proneness was estimated at .41 (Mosing et al.).
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **confirms H6.** The capacity to enjoy absorbing challenge is partly temperament, and partly inherited.

**6.5 Kinds of fun people report vary with personality, sex and age.**
- Source: I. C. McManus and Adrian Furnham, 2010, "'Fun, Fun, Fun': Types of Fun, Attitudes to Fun, and their Relation to Personality and Biographical Factors," *Psychology* 1: 159-168. https://file.scirp.org/Html/2392.html (full text).
- Kind: MEASURED (about 1,100 respondents, recruited by students in a stratified convenience sample; the authors warn against reading absolute percentages too closely).
- What it says: five types of fun emerged from how people described a fun situation: Sociability, Contentment, Achievement, Sensual and Ecstatic. Achievement fun loaded on "focussed, challenged, accomplished, absorbed and engrossed," which the authors liken to flow. It correlated with openness (.148) and with being male (r = -.115 with being female). Ecstatic fun correlated with extraversion (.253). Contentment fun ("warm relaxed fun") went with being "female, open, agreeable, introverted and stable." The authors sum up: "older, open males" linked fun more with "flow-type achievement activities." Their conclusion: fun "has different meanings for different types of people."
- Bears on: H6, H1.
- Direction: **confirms H6.** Effects are modest (mostly r below .26), so the differences are of degree.

**6.6 Across topics, the same activity divides people by temperament.**
This collects measured cases already listed, as direct tests of H6's "same thing, fun for one, a slog for another":
- Rozin et al. 2013 (3.1): liking for aversive pleasures clusters at both strong like and strong dislike.
- Scrivner et al. 2023 (3.3): three kinds of horror fans get different payoffs from the same haunted house.
- Clasen et al. 2020 (3.4): sensation seeking predicts liking horror.
- Kerr 1991 (2.2) and McEwan et al. 2019 (4.7): risk-sport athletes differ from others in arousal seeking, sensation seeking, neuroticism and sensitivity to punishment.
- Barlow et al. 2013 (4.6): skydiving and mountaineering serve different motives.
- Engeser and Rheinberg 2008 (6.1): achievement motive changes who enjoys a matched challenge.
- Direction: **confirms H6.**

### Topic 7. Culture, age and sex differences

**7.1 Sensation seeking: men score higher on average; the thrill gap has narrowed.**
- Source: C. P. Cross, D. M. Cyrenne and G. R. Brown, 2013, "Sex differences in sensation-seeking: a meta-analysis," *Scientific Reports* 3: 2486. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3757272 (abstract and results table).
- Kind: MEASURED (meta-analysis of Zuckerman's Sensation Seeking Scale V, about 67 studies, roughly 7,400 men and 9,500 women).
- What it says: men scored higher on the total scale (d = 0.46), Thrill and Adventure Seeking (d = 0.42), Disinhibition (d = 0.46) and Boredom Susceptibility (d = 0.35). There was no difference on Experience Seeking (d = 0.04). The Thrill and Adventure Seeking gap fell from about 0.48 to about 0.28 over 1978-2012, "due to a decrease in men's scores." The total-scale gap stayed stable.
- Bears on: H6, H2.
- Direction: **confirms H6** at the group level. A d near 0.4 means the male and female distributions overlap heavily; many women score above many men.

**7.2 Game motives: sex differences follow stereotypes but are smaller than age differences.**
- Sources: Quantic Foundry 2016 slides (as in 5.3; full text). Yee 2006 (as in 5.2; full text). Quantic Foundry, August 28, 2015, "Gender Differences in Gaming" (blog; would not load; search summary only).
- Kind: MEASURED (industry surveys and one peer-reviewed survey).
- What they say: "Gender differences align with stereotypes," but "Age explains twice the variance that gender does," with "87% overlap in terms of gender." With age, Competition falls most. Among men the whole Action-Social cluster declines (Competition -.29, Excitement -.26, Challenge -.23, Community -.18); among women, Excitement (-.25), Competition (-.20) and Story (-.19) decline. Strategy is the most stable motive across age. Per the search summary of the blog, Competition shows the largest gender gap (d = .33), and past age 45 there is "virtually no difference" between men and women on it. Yee (2006) likewise found age explained achievement motivation better than gender.
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **confirms H6 and refines it:** age and life stage shift what people find fun more than sex does.

**7.3 Culture shifts motives and even the size of sex and age differences.**
- Sources: Nick Yee (Quantic Foundry, with Niko Partners), January 8, 2019 (updated June 20, 2025), "How Chinese and American gamers differ," *GamesBeat*, https://gamesbeat.com/how-chinese-and-american-gamers-differ/ (full text). Kahn et al., 2015, "The Trojan Player Typology: A cross-genre, cross-cultural, behaviorally validated scale of video game play motivations," *Computers in Human Behavior* (search summary only; validated in a North American MOBA and a Chinese MMO).
- Kind: MEASURED (industry; U.S. data from over 350,000 self-selected respondents, Chinese data from 2,000 representative online gamers, so the samples differ in kind).
- What it says: "The average Chinese gamer cares more about Competition than 75 percent of those in the U.S." Chinese gamers scored below average on Immersion and Creativity motives. In China only three motives showed significant gender differences (men higher on Destruction, Discovery and Competition), against nine large gaps in the U.S. data. In China, "none of the correlations" with age exceeded 0.10, against seven in the U.S.
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **refines H6.** Culture shapes what is fun, and shapes how much sex and age matter. The same report's slides say the motive structure itself holds across regions (5.3).

**7.4 Play fighting: more common in boys, found in every culture studied, and riskier in adolescence.**
- Sources: Peter K. Smith and Jennifer M. StGeorge, 2022, "Play fighting (rough-and-tumble play) in children: developmental and evolutionary perspectives," *International Journal of Play*, https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/33029/ (full text). Marley et al., 2022, "A systematic review of sex differences in rough and tumble play across non-human mammals," *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* 76: 158 (search summary only).
- Kind: MEASURED (reviews).
- What they say: "play fighting is generally more common in boys, with less gender difference in play chasing." Some researchers report girls enjoy it as much as boys; others find girls' play fighting gentler and less competitive. It "appears to be a cross-cultural, universal aspect of human behavior, although its prevalence can vary." In adolescence "the boundary between play fighting and real fighting becomes more blurred," and play can be used to show dominance. Across non-human mammals, per the search summary, male bias in play fighting is "not as consistent as predicted."
- Bears on: H6, H2.
- Direction: **confirms H6 (a sex difference in rough play in humans) and H2:** when the frame blurs in adolescence, play can turn into real dominance contests.

**7.5 Women are less likely to see risk as part of fun; men link fun more with achievement.**
- Sources: McManus and Furnham 2010 (as in 6.5; full text). Lucas and Sherry, 2004, "Sex differences in video game play: A communication-based explanation," *Communication Research* 31(5): 499-523 (search summary of the abstract only). Rozin et al. 2013 (as in 3.1; full text). López Fernández et al. 2013 (as in 4.11; abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What they say: "Female participants were less likely to consider risk-taking as important in having fun, as also were older subjects" (McManus and Furnham, p < .001). Lucas and Sherry (n = 534 young adults) found women less oriented to genres featuring competition and three-dimensional rotation, and less motivated to play in social situations. Rozin et al. found enjoyment of sadness "higher in females across domains." Among climbers, women more often chose fun and image enhancement as reasons to climb.
- Bears on: H6, H2.
- Direction: **confirms H6 at the group level,** with the usual overlap.

**7.6 Cross-reference: the men's-entertainment video extraction.**
- Source: `/home/kuroskalacs/Documents/Doll-Fi/media/y-files/writing stories for men/data/01 - 5 Things Men Want From Entertainment (video extraction).md` (read in full). It is a video extraction, not research.
- Kind: CLAIM.
- What it says: men respond to competence shown rather than awarded, to the problem treated as real, to strategy against a stronger opponent, and to "dangerous characters, and dangerous motivations, and dangerous ideas"; "Men are enthralled by the struggle itself."
- Fit with the measured data: the direction matches the group averages above: more achievement and competition motive (5.2, 7.2), more thrill seeking (7.1), more willingness to count risk as part of fun (7.5). The sizes do not support treating these as rules for all men: overlap is large (87 percent on Competition), age matters more than sex, and culture shifts the gaps.
- Bears on: H6.
- Direction: **neutral** (a cross-reference, not evidence).

### Topic 8. Fun without challenge?

This topic gathers cases for the H1 synthesis. It gives no verdict on H1.

**8.1 When people describe a fun situation, most do not call it challenging.**
- Source: McManus and Furnham 2010 (as in 6.5; full text, Table 1).
- Kind: MEASURED (adjective checklist; the authors caution about absolute percentages).
- What it says: share of respondents ticking each word for a situation they had found fun: "happy" 71.8 percent, "laughing" 62.2, "entertained" 51.6, "stress free" 47.9, "excited" 47.7, "relaxed" 46.6, "challenged" 22.6, "focused" 18.8, "accomplished" 12.0. Of five fun types, only Achievement involves challenge. Contentment fun covered "gardening," "just being at home," "swimming in the sea" and "listening to music." Sociability fun covered joking, laughing and talking. Ecstatic fun covered amusement parks and clubbing.
- Bears on: H1, H6.
- Direction: **refutes H1 as a universal unit, on this evidence;** the verdict is left to the synthesis. People call many low-challenge situations fun. A hidden challenge may still exist in some (in social banter, for example), but respondents did not describe one.

**8.2 Some writers separate "fun" (distraction) from "pleasure" (absorption).**
- Source: Mark Blythe and Marc Hassenzahl, 2004, "The Semantics of Fun: Differentiating Enjoyable Experiences," in *Funology: From Usability to Enjoyment*, pp. 91-100 (the chapter was not opened). Read through Michinari Kono and Koichi Araake, 2022, "Is it Fun?: Understanding Enjoyment in Non-Game HCI Research," arXiv:2209.02308 (full text), and a search summary.
- Kind: THEORY.
- What it says: per Kono and Araake, "fun is considered in terms of distraction, whereas pleasure is of absorption." Per the search summary, the chapter contrasts fun and pleasure along triviality versus relevance, repetition versus progression, and transgression versus commitment.
- Bears on: H1.
- Direction: **refines H1 (for the synthesis).** On this view, the author's unit (effortful challenge, earned reward, a progressing chain) describes absorbing "pleasure" or flow more than light "fun." It may be one kind of enjoyment rather than all of it.

**8.3 Thrill rides and spicy food: pleasure from surviving a false alarm.**
- Source: Rozin et al. 2013 (as in 3.1; full text).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What it says: thrill rides, spicy food and sad music are liked by many. The authors frame the pleasure as "mind over body," which "can also be framed as a type of mastery."
- Bears on: H1, H2.
- Direction: **neutral on H1.** These are vertigo and sensation pleasures (close to Caillois's ilinx, which Job 1 covers), but their own authors propose a hidden challenge: bearing an aversive signal.

**8.4 Being tickled: positive affect with no task to overcome.**
- Source: Panksepp and Burgdorf 2003 (as in 1.6; abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What it says: tickled rats emit the same ~50-kHz calls as in play, which the authors read as positive affect.
- Bears on: H1.
- Direction: **tests H1.** For the tickled rat there is no obvious challenge. Tickling resembles play fighting, so one could argue it is play fighting with a partner who always "attacks," but no source opened makes that argument.

**8.5 Games used to relax also involve mastery.**
- Source: Emily Collins and Anna L. Cox, 2014, "Switch on to games: Can digital games aid post-work recovery?" *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies* 72(8-9): 654-662. https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/switch-on-to-games-can-digital-games-aid-post-work-recovery/ (abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED (survey of 491 adults).
- What it says: hours of play per week correlated with overall recovery from work. "First person shooters and action games were most highly correlated with recovery." The recovery dimensions measured were psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery and control. Online social support mediated the link for gamers with online friends.
- Bears on: H1, H2.
- Direction: **neutral.** "Relaxing" play here is often demanding play; relaxation and mastery can come from the same activity. Being safe from work stakes (H2) may matter more than how easy the game is.

**8.6 Collecting and socializing are separate motives from challenge.**
- Sources: Quantic Foundry 2016 slides (as in 5.3; full text). Yee 2006 (as in 5.2; full text). Bartle 1996 (as in 5.1; full text).
- Kind: MEASURED and PRACTITIONER.
- What they say: Completion ("Get All Collectibles," "Complete All Missions") and Community are motives separate from Challenge. The model groups Completion with Power under Achievement, and Community with Competition under Social. Yee's social and immersion components are nearly uncorrelated with achievement (r < 0.10). Bartle's socializers play to talk.
- Bears on: H1, H6.
- Direction: **tests H1.** Collecting has a goal structure and so may hide a mild challenge (finishing the set). Socializing and immersion show no such structure in these sources.

**8.7 A questionnaire for fun treats challenge as one dimension of six.**
- Source: G. Tisza, 2021, "Assessing the Fun Experience with FunQ," *ICIS 2021-Fall* (IEEE/ACIS), pp. 144-148. https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/c72ba008-561c-48d8-98f2-f2bfbcabe65e (abstract). The six dimensions come from a search summary of the 2021 *Current Psychology* paper on FunQ, which was blocked.
- Kind: MEASURED (questionnaire development with adolescents).
- What it says: FunQ is an 18-item questionnaire for adolescents. Per the search summary, its six dimensions are Autonomy, Challenge, Delight, Immersion, Loss of Social Barriers and Stress.
- Bears on: H1.
- Direction: **refines H1 (for the synthesis).** A measure built to capture fun includes challenge but does not reduce fun to it.

**8.8 For some horror fans, the payoff is growth rather than fun.**
- Source: Scrivner et al. 2023 (as in 3.3; abstract).
- Kind: MEASURED.
- What it says: White Knucklers reported personal growth, not immediate enjoyment.
- Bears on: H1, H3.
- Direction: **refines H3 (for the synthesis):** the positive payoff after a challenge need not feel like fun at the time.

## 4. Conflicts between sources

1. **Control kept versus control let go.** Laurendeau (4.3), Kerr and Houge Mackenzie (2.3) and Jones et al. (4.5) put perceived control at the center of dangerous fun. Brymer and Schweitzer (4.4) report athletes seeking "freedom as letting go of the need for control." The two fit together if the sequence is: prepare and control everything you can, then accept the rest. One kayaker called it "managed non-control." Rats do something similar when they deliberately hand over control inside safe play (1.3).
2. **Risk sought versus risk tolerated.** Barlow et al. (4.6) tie skydiving to sensation seeking, and Brymer and Schweitzer (4.4) say danger "contributes." Jones et al. (4.5), Delle Fave et al. (4.9) and López Fernández et al. (4.11) find that the challenge draws climbers and the risk does not. The split seems to follow activity and person, not a single rule.
3. **Benign masochism needs no real danger; extreme sports have it.** Rozin et al. (3.1) define the pleasure as coming from knowing "there is no real danger," and they set real-danger sports aside, only guessing that the same mechanism applies. Brymer and Schweitzer's athletes know the danger is real (4.4). So the "false alarm" account fits roller coasters and horror, but it is not shown to fit BASE jumping or alpine climbing.
4. **The "70/30" rule versus the rat data.** The popular rule (Peterson 2018) says the smaller rat quits unless it wins 30 percent of bouts. The measured data give a 70 percent pinning asymmetry (Panksepp 1981, 1984 as cited), role reversals of uncertain frequency, and self-handicapping (1.4). No opened source contains the quitting threshold (Omary 2023 also could not find it).
5. **Types versus traits.** Bartle (5.1) and BrainHex (5.4) sort players into types. Yee (5.2) and Tondello et al. (5.5) find independent dimensions that people mix, and argue that type models run "contrary to the latest personality research."
6. **How strong is "fit"?** H6 says the challenge "must" suit the person. Measured personality links are real but small: r of about .1 to .25 (5.3, 6.5). Group differences overlap heavily (87 percent on Competition by sex, 7.2). Much of the variance in what people find fun is not explained by measured traits.
7. **Sex versus age.** Cross et al. (7.1) find a stable male lead in total sensation seeking (d = 0.46). Quantic Foundry (7.2) finds age explains twice the variance sex does in game motives, and Chinese data show fewer sex and age differences than U.S. data (7.3). Human children show a fairly consistent male lead in play fighting (7.4), but non-human mammals do not, per a review seen only in summary.
8. **Balance model versus stakes.** Csikszentmihalyi's balance model predicts most flow when challenge matches skill. Engeser and Rheinberg (6.1) find that holds only when little is at stake; when the stakes are high, flow stays high with low demand.
9. **"Relaxed setting" versus mild stress.** Burghardt (1.1) requires low stress, but adds that mild stress such as boredom "may facilitate play." Siviy (1.2) shows play is resilient to mild disruption. This is a matter of dose, not a contradiction.

## 5. Verdict per claim

### H2, safety ("usually non-damaging, and generally non-lethal")
- **Strongest evidence for:**
  - Real threat, as the animal perceives it, ends play: cat odor abolished rat play for days (Siviy 2010, 1.2). Burghardt's criteria require freedom from "predator threat" and acute stress (1.1).
  - People enjoy fear, pain and sadness when they know the body is "falsely" alarmed; the authors set real-danger activities aside (Rozin et al. 2013, 3.1). Haunted-house fear is enjoyed only up to a middle level (Andersen et al. 2020, 3.2).
  - When the stakes of failure are high, people need skill to exceed the challenge to feel flow (Engeser and Rheinberg 2008, 6.1).
  - Most of what people call fun is low-damage in fact. Indoor climbing had the lowest injury rate of all sports assessed by Schöffl et al. (4.1). U.S. skydiving now records about 0.23 deaths per 100,000 jumps (4.1).
- **Strongest evidence against:**
  - Some activities people choose for enjoyment are plainly lethal at times: 1.3 percent of mountaineers above Everest base camp died, 1921-2006 (Firth et al. 2008, 4.1); BASE jumping is listed at about one death per 2,317 jumps (4.1).
  - For edgeworkers, the threat of death or injury is the point, not a side effect: "controlling the seemingly uncontrollable" (Lyng 1990, via 4.2).
  - Extreme athletes say danger "contributes" to the experience (Brymer and Schweitzer 2013, 4.4). Skydiving motives tie to sensation seeking (Barlow et al. 2013, 4.6).
  - In a general sample, one of five attitude factors about fun was "Fun involving risk-taking," with items such as "Is an activity more fun if there is risk involved?" (McManus and Furnham 2010, 6.5 and 7.5).
- **Net reading: partly supported.** As a statement about frequency ("usually," "generally"), H2 holds: most fun is low in real danger. As a defining condition, it is contradicted for a minority of people and activities, the author's own climbing example among them. The condition that fits all the evidence is felt safety, or confidence in coming through: a protective frame (Apter, 2.1), a confidence frame resting on skill, preparation and equipment (2.1, 4.5), or a belief in control (4.3). Real danger the person feels is beyond their control ends the fun, in rats and in people. The risk itself is part of the fun for some (skydivers, edgeworkers, thrill-ride lovers) and merely tolerated by others (most climbers, mountaineers).

### H6, fit ("the challenge must suit the person's sensibilities, temperament, values, goals and personality")
- **Strongest evidence for:**
  - Personality predicts dangerous fun: risk-sport athletes score higher on sensation seeking, extraversion and impulsivity and lower on neuroticism and sensitivity to punishment (McEwan et al. 2019 meta-analysis, 4.7; Kerr 1991, 2.2).
  - The same activity serves different people differently: three kinds of horror fans with different payoffs (Scrivner et al. 2023, 3.3); liking for aversive pleasures split into strong likers and strong dislikers (Rozin et al. 2013, 3.1); skydiving and mountaineering satisfy different motives (Barlow et al. 2013, 4.6).
  - Achievement motive and locus of control change how much a matched challenge produces flow (Engeser and Rheinberg 2008, 6.1; Keller and Blomann 2008, 6.2). Flow proneness is partly heritable (Mosing et al. 2012, 6.4).
  - Types of fun reported vary with personality, sex and age (McManus and Furnham 2010, 6.5); game motives map onto the Big Five (Quantic Foundry, 5.3).
- **Strongest evidence against, or limiting:**
  - Effects are small (r of about .1 to .25) and distributions overlap heavily (5.3, 6.5, 7.2). Traits only "partially explain" preferences (Tondello et al. 2018, 5.5).
  - Some features look universal rather than personal: the motive structure is stable across regions (5.3); play fighting appears in every culture studied (7.4); across the whole haunted-house sample, enjoyment peaked at a middle level of fear at the filmed scare points (3.2).
  - The same person's fun changes with state, not just with stable personality: stress and hunger switch play off (1.1, 1.2); the stakes of the moment change the best challenge level (6.1); Bartle's players drift between styles (5.1); reversal theory holds that people switch between serious and playful states (2.1).
- **Net reading: supported, with refinements.** Who finds what fun does depend on temperament, motives and goals, which supports H6. But the differences are mostly of degree, not kind; age and culture matter as much as or more than sex; and the person's current state and stakes matter alongside stable personality.

### H1, from the safety and temperament side (evidence only; the verdict belongs to the synthesis)
- Cases that fit H1: achievement fun (6.5), climbers' "personal challenge" (4.11), rats' self-handicapped play fighting (1.3), benign masochism framed as "mind over body" mastery (3.1).
- Cases that strain H1: most people describing a fun situation did not call it challenging (22.6 percent did; 8.1); contentment and sociability fun (8.1); socializing and immersion motives independent of achievement (8.6); tickling (8.4); the fun-versus-pleasure distinction (8.2); FunQ's six dimensions (8.7).

## 6. Refinements the evidence suggests
These are options for the author, not decisions.

1. **H2, option A: replace "non-damaging" with felt safety.** For example: the player must feel able to come through, whether or not the danger is real. The feeling can rest on skill, preparation, equipment, trust in a partner, or the knowledge that it is fiction. Supported by 1.2, 2.1, 2.3, 3.1, 4.3 and 4.5.
2. **H2, option B: treat threat as a tunable ingredient with a personal sweet spot.** Many people like the most fear or burn they can stand (3.1); enjoyment peaks at a middle level (3.2). The sweet spot is higher for high sensation seekers (3.4, 4.7, 7.1).
3. **H2, option C: link stakes to the challenge-skill ratio.** The more real the consequences, the further skill must exceed challenge for the activity to stay enjoyable (6.1). Real danger then works as a multiplier on difficulty, a possible bridge to H4.
4. **H2, option D: keep H2 as a default for design, with a named exception.** "Usually non-damaging" holds for most fun, and for most game design. A separate class, edgework, covers people for whom handling real danger is the point (4.2, 4.4).
5. **H2, option E: in shared play, the frame is kept up by signals and fairness.** Play bows, self-handicapping and role reversal keep rough play safe; breaking them ends play (1.3, 1.5, 7.4).
6. **H6, option A: add state to trait.** Fit depends on the person's current state (stress, fatigue, stakes, mood) as well as on stable personality (1.1, 2.1, 5.1, 6.1, 8.5).
7. **H6, option B: think in dimensions, not types.** People mix motives (achievement, social, immersion, and others) in different amounts (5.2, 5.3, 5.5).
8. **H6, option C: add "what the person wants out of it."** The payoff can be thrill, mastery, calm, growth or company (3.3, 4.6, 4.10). This fits the brief's "goals" and "values" wording.
9. **For the synthesis on H1:** the author's unit may describe one kind of fun (achievement fun, close to flow) among several. Others (sociability, contentment, sensation, collecting) may need other units, or a search for a hidden challenge (8.1, 8.2, 8.6, 8.7).
