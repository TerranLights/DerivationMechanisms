# Job 1: theory of play and game design

## 1. Scope
This file tests the author's theory of fun (brief of November 1, 2026) against the theory of play and the game-design literature. Its main claims are H1 (the unit is an effortful challenge), H3 (overcoming it pays off) and H5 (consistent fun is a chain of units). It touches H2 (safety) through the classic definitions of play, which almost all set play apart from real consequence.

Eight topics, in the brief's order:
1. Classics on play and amusement.
2. Huizinga, *Homo Ludens*.
3. Caillois, *Man, Play and Games*.
4. Suits, *The Grasshopper*; Sutton-Smith, *The Ambiguity of Play*.
5. Game-design theories of fun.
6. The smallest unit, as designers define it.
7. Formal and computational theories.
8. Humor as a micro-unit test.

For books that could not be opened, the publication year comes from the brief, catalog listings or search results and was not checked against the book. ## 3. Findings

### Topic 1. Classics on play and amusement

**F1.1. Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* X.6 (Bekker 1176b), trans. W. D. Ross.** [MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html). Full text of Book X opened.
THEORY. Aristotle denies that happiness lies in amusement. He sides with Anacharsis: "To amuse oneself in order that one may exert oneself... seems right." Amusement is "a sort of relaxation, and we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously." So it is "not an end; for it is taken for the sake of activity." Working hard for the sake of amusement "seems silly and utterly childish."
Bears on H1, H3. **Refines.** Aristotle's amusement is the *opposite* of effort: rest between exertions, not an exertion. His view fits the author's theory only if the effortful part is placed on the serious side of life and the fun on the resting side. It also gives a second payoff besides reward: recovery for the next exertion.

**F1.2. Aristotle, *Politics* VIII.3 (Bekker 1337b) and VIII.5 (Bekker 1339b), trans. Benjamin Jowett.** [MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html). Full text of Book VIII opened. Bekker numbers checked against H. Rackham's translation on Perseus ([1337b](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D8%3Asection%3D1337b), [1339b](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D8%3Asection%3D1339b)). Rackham renders 1337b as "the activity of play is a relaxation of the soul."
THEORY. Play is a medicine for toil. In VIII.3, "from the pleasure we obtain rest." In VIII.5, "Amusement is for the sake of relaxation, and relaxation is of necessity sweet, for it is the remedy of pain caused by toil." Aristotle ranks leisure above occupation ("leisure is better than occupation and is its end") and keeps amusement apart from both. People err when they "make amusement the end."
Bears on H1, H3. **Refutes H1 as a universal**, for this class of pleasure: Aristotle's amusement is pleasant *because* it lacks the strain of work. H3 holds in a different form: the payoff is relief, not a reward for overcoming.

**F1.3. Plato, *Laws* II, 653d-654a, trans. R. G. Bury (Perseus edition: Harvard University Press, 1967-1968).** [Perseus, Laws 2.653](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0166%3Abook%3D2%3Apage%3D653). Full text of the section opened.
THEORY. Every young creature cannot keep still in body or voice; it is always leaping, skipping and delighting in dances and games. Only humans, Plato says, were given "the pleasurable perception of rhythm and harmony," that is, a sense of order and disorder in movement.
Bears on H1, H6. **Refines.** Play here starts as a drive to move, before any challenge. The pleasure the gods add is the perception of *order* (rhythm) in that movement. This is closer to pattern-pleasure (Koster, Topic 5; Schmidhuber, Topic 7) than to overcoming an obstacle.

**F1.4. Plato, *Laws* VII, 803c-e, trans. R. G. Bury.** [Perseus, Laws 7.803](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0166%3Abook%3D7%3Apage%3D803). Full text of the section opened.
THEORY. Man is "a plaything of God, and the best part of him is really just that." So the right life is lived "playing at certain pastimes," which Plato names as sacrificing, singing and dancing.
Bears on H1, H3. **Neutral to slightly refining.** Plato's best play is ritual and art, not contest. It has form and rules but no named obstacle or prize.

**F1.5. Friedrich Schiller, *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man*, Letter XV (1795).** In *Aesthetical Essays of Friedrich Schiller*, [Project Gutenberg #6798](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6798) (the translator is not named on the Gutenberg page). Letter XV read in full.
THEORY. The "play instinct" unites the sensuous impulse and the formal impulse; its object is "living form." Play is what is "neither subjectively nor objectively accidental, and yet does not impose necessity either externally or internally." The famous line: "man only plays when in the full meaning of the word he is a man, and he is only completely a man when he plays." Schiller also contrasts the Greeks' "bloodless athletic contests" at Olympia with the Roman crowd "gloating over the agony of a gladiator."
Bears on H1, H2, H6. **Refines.** Schiller's play is *freedom from compulsion* on both sides, the body's needs and reason's laws. That is a condition about the player's state, not about an obstacle or a prize. His Olympia/gladiator line is early support for H2: the noble contest is the bloodless one.

**F1.6. Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Judgment* (1790), §9 and §54, trans. J. H. Bernard (2nd ed., 1914).** [Project Gutenberg #48433](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/48433). §9 and §54 read in full.
THEORY. In §9 the pleasure of beauty is a feeling of "the free play of the Imagination and the Understanding," free "because no definite concept limits them." In §54, "All changing free play of sensations... gratifies, because it promotes the feeling of health." He sorts it into the "play of fortune [games of chance]," the "play of tone [music]" and the "play of thought [wit]." Of party games he says hope, fear, joy, wrath and scorn alternate "every moment" and enliven the body, "although nothing is gained or learnt thereby."
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Refutes H1 as a universal; refines H5.** Kant names games of chance as a full kind of play. They need no effort beyond staking an interest, and their pleasure comes from the *alternation* of emotions, not from overcoming. His "nothing is gained or learnt" also cuts against the learning theories of Topic 5 and Topic 7. On H5, Kant's unit of fun is a rapid swing of tension and release, repeated, which is a chain, but a chain of feelings rather than of challenges.

**F1.7. Zhuangzi, ch. 1 "Transcendental Bliss" (*Xiaoyao you*, "free and easy wandering") and ch. 3 "Nourishment of the Soul," trans. Herbert A. Giles, *Chuang Tzŭ: Mystic, Moralist, and Social Reformer* (London: Quaritch, 1889).** [Project Gutenberg #59709](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/59709). Chapters 1 and 3 read in full.
THEORY. Chapter 1 ends with the useless tree. Instead of fretting that it is good for no purpose, plant it in the wild and take "blissful repose beneath its shade." Chapter 3 is the parable of Prince Hui's cook. His cutting is "rhythmical like the dance," and he cuts with his mind, not his eye. When he hits a hard part he is "all caution," works slowly until it "yields like earth crumbling," then looks around "with an air of triumph."
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Mixed.** The useless tree is a classic case of enjoyment without any challenge (against H1 as a universal). The cook is the reverse: a near-perfect picture of the author's unit (hard part, careful effort, release, triumph), set inside a long, smooth run of mastered skill. Two points matter. The unit appears *inside work* (butchering), so the unit alone does not tell work from play. And the cook's run is mostly effortless mastery, with only occasional hard knots, which bears on how H5's chain is paced.

**F1.8. Karl Groos, *The Play of Man* (trans. Elizabeth L. Baldwin with the author's co-operation, New York: D. Appleton, 1901), pp. 39, 44 and 385.** [Internet Archive, Cornell copy cu31924055469252](https://archive.org/details/cu31924055469252). The OCR full text was searched and the passages read in context. (Groos was not on the brief's list. He is added as a classic of play theory because he states H1 almost word for word.)
THEORY. "Pleasure in overcoming difficulties is an essential feature of all play" (p. 39). He links it to "the fighting instinct." But his other main source of play pleasure is the "joy in being a cause," which he calls "so essential to play" and "well-nigh universal" (p. 385). A child enjoys a self-made clatter even when the sound is unpleasant, like a slate pencil scratching (p. 44). In violent movement, that joy becomes "pleasure in the mere possession of power," and it "is proportionate to the magnitude of the results" (p. 385).
Bears on H1, H3, H4. **Confirms H1 (one of two roots); refines H3 and H4.** Groos is one of H1's strongest classic backers, yet he puts a second root beside it: the bare pleasure of making something happen, which needs no obstacle. His remark on proportion is worth the author's notice. Groos ties the pleasure of power to the *size of the effect produced*, not to the size of the challenge overcome. That is a different proportion from H4's (Job 2 tests H4).

### Topic 2. Huizinga, *Homo Ludens* (1938)

**F2.1. Johan Huizinga, *Homo Ludens: A Study of the Play-Element in Culture* (Dutch 1938; English ed. New York: Roy Publishers, 1950), ch. 1, pp. 10-13.** [Internet Archive, homoludensstudyo1950huiz](https://archive.org/details/homoludensstudyo1950huiz). Chapter 1 read in the OCR full text. The OCR is damaged in places, so only clean passages are quoted.
THEORY. Huizinga sums up play as "a free activity standing quite consciously outside 'ordinary' life as being 'not serious', but at the same time absorbing the player intensely and utterly." It is "connected with no material interest, and no profit can be gained by it." It "proceeds within its own proper boundaries of time and space according to fixed rules and in an orderly manner," and it forms social groups.
Bears on H2, H3. **Refines H3; partly confirms H2.** Huizinga's "no profit" condition rules out *material* reward as part of play. If the author's "positive benefit" means anything material, Huizinga's play excludes it; only an inner reward fits. His "outside ordinary life" and "not serious" frame is the classic ground for H2.

**F2.2. Huizinga, same edition, ch. 1, pp. 10-11, on tension.** Same source and reading.
THEORY. Among play's features is tension. The player wants to "succeed" by his own exertions. A baby reaching for a toy, a cat patting a bobbin, a girl playing ball: "all want to achieve something difficult, to succeed, to end a tension." Tension and its solution govern "all solitary games of skill and application," such as puzzles, jigsaws and target shooting. The more competitive the play, the more fervent it is. Elsewhere (ch. 3, p. 47) he repeats that every game asks "will it come off?", even patience and crosswords.
Bears on H1, H3. **Confirms.** This is the closest classic statement of H1 and H3: effort against something difficult, ending in the release of a tension. But Huizinga lists tension as *one* feature among several, not as the whole of play. He names it "in gambling and athletics" at its height, so for him gambling has tension without needing skill (see Topic 3).

**F2.3. Huizinga, same edition, ch. 1, pp. 2-3, on "fun."** Same source and reading.
THEORY. Huizinga asks, "what actually is the fun of playing?" He answers that the fun element "resists all analysis, all logical interpretation." As a concept, "it cannot be reduced to any other mental category." Nature could have given us all of play's useful functions as mechanical exercises, "But no, she gave us play, with its tension, its mirth, and its fun."
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Refutes, as a method.** Huizinga holds that fun is primary and cannot be broken into parts. That cuts against the brief's search for an indivisible unit built from challenge plus reward. It does not refute the unit as a *design tool*; it says the unit may not be what fun *is*. He also lists tension and fun as two different things, which suggests the challenge unit is not the fun itself.

**F2.4. Huizinga, same edition, ch. 1, p. 1 (animal play), and ch. 5, "Play and War," p. 89.** Same source and reading.
THEORY. Young dogs "keep to the rule that you shall not bite, or not bite hard, your brother's ear," and they "plainly experience tremendous fun and enjoyment." But in ch. 5 he says fighting "for fun" has rules limiting violence, yet "the limits of licit violence do not necessarily stop at the spilling of blood or even at killing," as in the earliest tournaments.
Bears on H2. **Refines.** Huizinga supports H2 for ordinary play (rules that cap harm) but shows that cultures have counted blood-letting and even lethal contests as play. In his view, what makes it play is the rule-bound frame, not the absence of harm. Job 3 tests this further.

**F2.5. "Magic circle": Huizinga's phrase and its later use.** Huizinga, same edition, p. 10; Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman, "This Is Not a Game: Play in Cultural Environments," *Level Up: Digital Games Research Conference* (DiGRA/Utrecht University, 2003), selections from *Rules of Play* (MIT Press, 2003). [DiGRA Digital Library, PDF](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/73/73). Huizinga's chapter and the full DiGRA paper read.
THEORY. In Huizinga, "the magic circle" is one item in a list of play-grounds: "The arena, the card-table, the magic circle, the temple, the stage, the screen, the tennis court..." All are "temporary worlds within the ordinary world." A word search of the OCR text of the 1950 English edition finds the phrase four times, each time as one example of a hedged-off space. In ch. 4, "Play and Law" (p. 77), a court of justice "is still a magic circle," and "Whoever steps inside it is sacrosanct for the time being." Salen and Zimmerman say it plainly: "the magic circle is merely one of the examples in Huizinga's list," and they use it "as shorthand" for the special space and time a game creates. They add that this frame "creates a game's feeling of safety," citing Apter's reversal theory.
Bears on H2. **Confirms H2 as a frame condition.** The modern magic circle is a 2003 game-design term built on Huizinga, not Huizinga's own concept. Its link to safety goes through Apter (Job 3, Topic 2). Salen and Zimmerman's paper is about games built to blur that frame, which shows the boundary is designed and can leak.

**F2.6. Gregory Bateson, "A Theory of Play and Fantasy" (first published in a 1955 conference report, per search results; reprinted in *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, 1972).** Bateson's essay was not opened. Read through Stephen Nachmanovitch, "This Is Play," *New Literary History* 40 (2009): 1-24 ([PDF, freeplay.com](https://www.freeplay.com/Writings/Nachmanovitch.This.is.play.NLH2009.pub.pdf)), pp. 1-3 and 7 read in full. (Bateson was not on the brief's list. He is added because his play frame is the classic source for H2's "non-damaging" condition.)
THEORY. Watching monkeys play-fight at a zoo, Bateson asked how they know it is play. His answer, as Nachmanovitch quotes it: "The playful nip denotes the bite, but it does not denote what would be denoted by the bite." Play carries a metamessage, "this is play," that tells the other player how to read the act.
Bears on H2. **Confirms H2, and sharpens it.** On Bateson's account, a play act *stands for* a harmful act without carrying its harm. That is H2 stated as a feature of meaning rather than of outcome. It also shows what H2 depends on: a shared signal that the threat is not real. If that signal fails, the same act stops being play.

### Topic 3. Caillois, *Man, Play and Games* (1958)

No English full text of Caillois could be opened this session (the Internet Archive copy of the French 1967 edition is lending-only, and an Indiana University excerpt PDF failed to load). Caillois is therefore read through the quotations, with page numbers, in Henricks (2010). Page numbers refer to the University of Illinois Press reprint (2001) of Meyer Barash's 1961 translation.

**F3.1. Thomas S. Henricks, "Caillois's *Man, Play, and Games*: An Appreciation and Evaluation," *American Journal of Play* (Fall 2010), quoting Caillois 2001, pp. 9-10.** [ERIC EJ1070247, Internet Archive full text](https://archive.org/details/ERIC_EJ1070247). Full text read.
THEORY. Caillois's play has six features. It is free (not obligatory), separate, uncertain ("the results are not known beforehand"), unproductive ("an expenditure that does not create wealth or goods"), rule-bound, and make-believe ("a second reality or of a free unreality, as against real life").
Bears on H1, H2, H3. **Refines.** Caillois makes *uncertainty of outcome* a defining feature, where H1 makes *effort* the defining feature. Uncertainty is present in every game of chance; effort is not. His "unproductive" matches Huizinga's "no profit" (F2.1), which again limits H3's payoff to inner rewards.

**F3.2. Henricks (2010), quoting Caillois 2001, pp. 17-18, on *alea*.** Same source and reading. MIT OpenCourseWare lecture notes for CMS.300 (Fall 2011), session 2, give the same reading. [MIT OCW PDF](https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/cms-300-introduction-to-videogame-studies-fall-2011/e9f5a9af8b2f73de9c6999d6ce85d64e_MITCMS_300F11_session_2.pdf) (full text read; lecture notes, used only to cross-check).
THEORY. Where *agon* celebrates "willful assertion," *alea* is "willful surrender to external forces." In Caillois's words, "alea negates work, patience, experience, and qualifications." Players enjoy awaiting "the decision of destiny passively and deliberately."
Bears on H1, H3. **Refutes H1 as a universal.** Caillois names an entire category of play whose defining feature is the *absence* of effort and skill. Roulette and lotteries are played for fun by very many people. The tension and release of waiting for an outcome is present (Huizinga's "will it come off?", F2.2; Kant's alternating hope and fear, F1.6), but nothing is overcome by the player. Two readings are open. Either H1 is wrong for chance play, or the "challenge" in alea is bearing risk and suspense rather than effort. The second reading stretches the word "effort" a long way.

**F3.3. Henricks (2010), quoting Caillois 2001, p. 23, on *ilinx*.** Same source and reading.
THEORY. *Ilinx*, the pursuit of vertigo, is "an attempt to momentarily destroy the stability of perception and inflict a kind of voluptuous panic upon an otherwise lucid mind." Caillois's examples are whirling, high-wire acrobats, the Mexican *voladores* and, later, carnival rides. Henricks notes that Caillois sees *ilinx* as defying rule-bound structure: it is "the pursuit of disorderly uncertainty."
Bears on H1, H2, H3. **Refutes H1 as stated; refines H2.** On a carousel or a swing, the pleasure is the sensation itself, not a payoff after overcoming. Some *ilinx* does hold a challenge, such as courage on a high wire or holding on through a ride. But the brief's idea of a reward that comes *after* the challenge does not fit a pleasure that lasts only *while* the disorder lasts. On H2, *ilinx* is pleasure in a brief, contained loss of control. It is the "voluptuous panic" of a threat felt but not real (see Job 3).

**F3.4. Henricks (2010), quoting Caillois 2001, pp. 13 and 31, on *paidia* and *ludus*.** Same source and reading.
THEORY. All play lies "on a continuum between two opposite poles." *Paidia* is "diversion, turbulence, free improvisation, and carefree gaiety." *Ludus* binds that freedom with "arbitrary, imperative, and purposively tedious conventions." It calls on a "greater amount of effort, patience, skill, or ingenuity." Henricks reads *ludus* as limits players set themselves to challenge themselves further and even to "allay boredom." *Ludus* "disciplines" *paidia* and gives play "its purity and excellence."
Bears on H1, H5. **Refines.** H1 describes the *ludus* pole well: self-imposed, rule-made difficulty that calls for effort. It does not describe the *paidia* pole of carefree romping. On this reading, the author's unit is a theory of *ludic* fun, and *paidia* is a second source of fun that the theory does not yet cover. Caillois also says that *ludus* exists partly to "allay boredom," which ties challenge to the rise of boredom once free play goes stale (compare Koster, Topic 5).

### Topic 4. Suits, *The Grasshopper* (1978); Sutton-Smith, *The Ambiguity of Play* (1997)

**F4.1. Bernard Suits, "What Is a Game?", *Philosophy of Science* 34, no. 2 (June 1967): 148-156.** [JSTOR stable link](http://www.jstor.org/stable/186102). Full text read (a JSTOR copy).
THEORY. This is the article that *The Grasshopper* grew from. Its definition: to play a game is to engage in activity aimed at "a specific state of affairs," using only means the rules permit, where those means "are more limited in scope than they would be in the absence of the rules." The "sole reason for accepting such limitation is to make possible such activity." In games, Suits says, obeying the rules does not make the action right; "it makes the action." Once there is a goal and a meaning of winning, "a third end emerges: the activity of trying to win; that is, playing the game."
Bears on H1, H3. **Confirms H1; refines H3.** Suits's game is exactly an obstacle chosen for its own sake, which is H1's core. But the payoff in Suits's account is not only what comes *after* the obstacle. The *activity of trying* is itself one of the ends. That means the reward is partly *during* the challenge, not only on the far side of it.

**F4.2. Suits, *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia* (University of Toronto Press, 1978; Broadview Press, 2005), pp. 54-55: "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles."** The book itself could not be opened (the Internet Archive copy is lending-only). The wording and page were seen only in [Wikipedia, "Lusory attitude"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusory_attitude), which cites the 2005 Broadview edition, and in search summaries.
THEORY (wording not verified against the book). The "portable" definition is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles." The long form adds the four parts: the prelusory goal, lusory means, constitutive rules that forbid more efficient means in favor of less efficient ones, and the lusory attitude, in which the rules "are accepted just because they make possible such activity."
Bears on H1, H2. **Confirms H1; neutral on H2.** With Groos (F1.8), this is the strongest classic support for H1. It matches the author's unit almost word for word: an obstacle, effort, and voluntariness. Two limits apply. First, Suits defines *games*, not *fun*. The definition says nothing about whether the game is enjoyed. Second, the obstacles are "unnecessary," which is a different idea from "non-damaging." A mountain climb has an unnecessary obstacle and real danger. The 1967 article (F4.1) holds the same substance, so the substance is verified even though the 1978 wording is not.

**F4.3. Brian Sutton-Smith, "Play Theory: A Personal Journey and New Thoughts," *American Journal of Play* 1, no. 1 (Summer 2008): 80-123.** [The Strong museum, PDF](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/1-1-article-sutton-smith-play-theory.pdf). Full text read. (*The Ambiguity of Play*, Harvard University Press, 1997, could not be opened; the Internet Archive copy is lending-only. This later article by Sutton-Smith summarizes the book in his own words.)
THEORY. Sutton-Smith says scholars treat play as one of seven things: "progress," "power," "fate," "identity," "frivolity," "the imagination," or "personal experience." His argument "held that play was ambiguous," and much play is "intentionally ambiguous (as, for example, is teasing)." He came to see that "play is not just fun, not just pleasurable for its own sake." Its pleasure carries over into the rest of life, which he calls a "viability variable."
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Refines, and warns.** Sutton-Smith's whole case is that "play" is not one thing, so any single unit will capture only some of it. His list maps roughly onto the brief's claims: "progress" and "power" fit H1/H3, "fate" is Caillois's *alea*, and "frivolity" is *paidia*. On H3, he moves part of the payoff *outside* the activity: a lasting sense that life is worth living.

**F4.4. Sutton-Smith (2008), same source, pp. 114-122 (the "duality of play performances" and the closing summary).** Same reading. (F4.3's quotations are from pp. 95 and 112.)
THEORY. Play, he writes, "begins as a mutation of real conflicts." It is "a reactive itch of the amygdala" that responds to "shock, anger, fear, disgust, and sadness." It also has "a frontal-lobe counter, reaching for triumphant control and happiness and pride." He ties each primary emotion to a kind of play. Anger drives contests; fear drives risk-taking, whether "physical as in many kinds of sport or mental as in games of chance"; shock drives teasing.
Bears on H1, H2, H3. **Refines.** This is a two-step unit that fits the author's shape: a stirred-up negative emotion, then triumphant control over it. But the "challenge" here is an *emotion to be mastered*, not an obstacle that takes effort. Under that wording, chance games and scares fit the unit, and the effort clause does not. Note that Sutton-Smith's neurology is offered as speculation ("Perhaps some neurological support"); it is a theory, not a measured result.

**F4.5. C. Thi Nguyen, *Games: Agency as Art* (Oxford University Press, 2020), read through Thomas Hurka, "How Much Are Games Like Art?", *Analysis* 81, no. 2 (April 2021): 287-296 (book symposium).** [Hurka's PDF, thomashurka.com](https://thomashurka.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/nguyen-review.pdf). Full text read. Nguyen's book was not opened; an open copy of his related paper "The Arts of Action" (2020) could not be retrieved.
THEORY. Building on Suits, Nguyen defines "striving play." In it the player cares, even intensely, about the game's goal, but takes that goal on only for a while, so as to pursue it: "What you value ultimately is only that process." Hurka quotes Nguyen: "in most games striving is only desirable when the challenge is of appropriate difficulty" (book, p. 175). Hurka objects that many players want *real* stakes to feel pressure: "We need something extra at stake to feel seriously stressed." He cites golfers playing for money.
Bears on H1, H2, H3. **Refines H3; complicates H2.** In striving play, the "reward" of winning is a tool for having the struggle. That reverses H3's order: the payoff exists to make the challenge worth entering, and the challenge is what is valued. Nguyen's "appropriate difficulty" line supports a fit condition on H1. Hurka's objection bears on H2: some players seek out real, damaging stakes (lost money) because that gives the challenge its bite.

### Topic 5. Game-design theories of fun

**F5.1. Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (2004), as restated in his talk "Theory of Fun: 10 Years Later," GDC Online, Austin, October 2012.** [Slides, raphkoster.com (PDF)](https://www.raphkoster.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Theory-of-Fun-10-Years-Later.pdf), full slide text read. Event and date from Leigh Alexander, "Raph Koster's Theory of Fun, ten years on," *Game Developer*, October 9, 2012 ([link](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/raph-koster-s-theory-of-fun-ten-years-on)), read in full. The 2004 book itself was not opened.
THEORY / PRACTITIONER. Koster's slides quote the book: "Fun is just another word for learning," and "Fun in games arises out of mastery... With games, learning is the drug." He first phrased it as "fun is the emotional response to learning." The idea: we learn patterns, and "fun is a neurochemical reward to encourage us to keep trying." In 2012 he adds: "I intentionally excluded a lot of stuff we call 'fun' from the definition." He sets aside "delight" (an act of recognition, "transitory"), and says comfort "is comforting, not fun." He folds social play and mastery of one's own body back in, as patterns to learn.
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Confirms H1 and H5 in a narrowed form; refines H3.** Koster's unit is a pattern being learned, with fun as the payoff for progress in learning it. Effort fits (learning takes work), and so does a chain of patterns (H5). He also ties boredom to mastery, which is H5's reason a chain must keep moving. (His slides set boredom beside fun on a flow chart; the book's line "Boredom is the opposite of learning" was seen only in a search summary.) But Koster gets his clean theory by *excluding* delight, comfort and similar pleasures from "fun." The author's theory may need the same explicit exclusion, or a second category. The popular line "fun is just learning" is Koster's own wording, confirmed in his slides. The book's exact page was not checked.

**F5.2. Nicole Lazzaro, "Why We Play Games: Four Keys to More Emotion Without Story" (XEODesign, Inc., March 8, 2004; v2.01, 2005).** [PDF hosted by Kill Screen](https://killscreen.com/content/files/xeodesign_whyweplaygames.pdf). Full text read.
MEASURED (weak: an industry white paper, not peer-reviewed; 15 hardcore gamers, 15 casual gamers and 15 non-players; 45 hours of video plus questionnaires; coded for emotional cues). Lazzaro found four routes to emotion in play. **Hard Fun:** "For many players overcoming obstacles is why they play." It yields frustration and *fiero*, "Personal triumph over adversity." Fiero does not need anger first, "but it does require effort." **Easy Fun:** curiosity, wonder, awe and mystery, which "maintains focus with player attention rather than a winning condition." **Altered States** (named "Serious Fun" in her later "4 Keys 2 Fun" model, per a search summary): play to change one's inner state, such as "Clearing my mind by clearing a level." **The People Factor** (later "People Fun," per the same summary): amusement, *schadenfreude* and *naches*; some "play games they don't like so they can spend time with their friends." She also asks, "What makes failing 80% of the time fun?"
Bears on H1, H3, H6. **Confirms H1/H3 for one route; refutes them as the only route.** Hard Fun is the author's unit almost exactly, and fiero is the payoff the theory predicts. But players in her sample also sought curiosity, state change and company, and those do not need an obstacle. That some play games they dislike for the company is direct evidence that fun can sit outside the challenge itself.

**F5.3. Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc and Robert Zubek, "MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research" (2004).** [PDF at Northwestern University](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf). Full text read. (The PDF is dated May 2004, and its file name, "WS0404," points to AAAI workshop report WS-04-04. The PDF itself names only the GDC Game Design and Tuning Workshop, 2001-2004.)
THEORY / PRACTITIONER. The paper asks, "What makes a game 'fun'?" It proposes to move "away from words like 'fun'" to eight aesthetics: Sensation ("sense-pleasure"), Fantasy ("make-believe"), Narrative ("drama"), Challenge ("obstacle course"), Fellowship ("social framework"), Discovery ("uncharted territory"), Expression ("self-discovery") and Submission ("pastime"). "There is no Grand Unified Theory of games or formula" for fun. "Charades emphasizes Fellowship over Challenge."
Bears on H1, H6. **Refutes H1 as the single unit; supports H6.** In MDA, Challenge is one aesthetic in eight. Several of the others (Sensation, Submission, Fellowship) need no obstacle. The taxonomy is meant to explain "how and why different games appeal to different players, or to the same players at different times," which backs H6 and adds a time dimension: the same person wants different fun at different times.

**F5.4. Jesse Schell, *The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses* (2008), ch. 4.** The book was not opened. Definitions read as quoted in Carl Ji, "What is a game?", *Game Developer*, May 23, 2019 ([link](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/what-is-a-game-)), read in full.
PRACTITIONER. Schell's definitions are: fun is "pleasure with surprises"; play is "manipulation that indulges curiosity"; and a game is "a problem-solving activity, approached with a playful attitude."
Bears on H1, H3. **Refines.** Schell splits what the author joins. His *game* is the challenge (problem-solving). His *fun* is a different thing: pleasure plus surprise. A game can be a problem without being fun, and fun can be a surprise without a problem. If the author wants the unit to define *fun*, Schell suggests surprise is a missing ingredient. If the unit defines a *game*, Schell agrees with H1.

**F5.5. Jesper Juul, "The Game, the Player, the World: Looking for a Heart of Gameness," in *Level Up: Digital Games Research Conference Proceedings*, ed. Marinka Copier and Joost Raessens (Utrecht University, 2003), 30-45.** [jesperjuul.net](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/gameplayerworld/). Full text read. (Juul's *Half-Real*, MIT Press, 2005, builds its "classic game model" on this paper; the book was not opened.)
THEORY. Juul's game has six features: rules; a variable, quantifiable outcome; values assigned to outcomes; "player effort"; the player attached to the outcome; and negotiable consequences. Player effort "is another way of stating that games are challenging." It tends to attach the player to the outcome. But "A player may still feel happy when winning a game of pure chance." Consequences are negotiable: a game "can *optionally* be assigned real-life consequences."
Bears on H1, H2, H3. **Confirms H1 for games; refutes it for the joy of winning.** Effort is a defining feature of Juul's classic game, which supports H1. But Juul notes that happiness at winning does not need effort. That is the payoff of H3 without the challenge of H1. On H2, Juul's "negotiable consequences" is subtler than "non-damaging." Games *can* carry real stakes, such as money bets, and stay games. What matters is that the stakes are optional and agreed.

**F5.6. Jesper Juul, *The Art of Failure: An Essay on the Pain of Playing Video Games* (MIT Press, 2013), ch. 1.** Book page [jesperjuul.net/artoffailure](https://www.jesperjuul.net/artoffailure/) and the chapter 1 excerpt on [Salon, July 13, 2013](http://www.salon.com/2013/07/13/video_games_make_us_all_losers/), both read.
THEORY / PRACTITIONER. The "paradox of failure": "We generally avoid failure." "We experience failure when playing games." "We seek out games, although we will experience something that we normally avoid." Games "promise us a fair chance of redeeming ourselves." Juul tells of finishing a *Meteos* mode perfectly on his first try: "Naturally, this made me very angry," and he did not touch the game for over a year.
Bears on H1, H3, H4b. **Confirms H1; supports H4b.** The pain of failure is part of the price, and the fair chance to redeem it is part of the payoff. The *Meteos* story is a one-person anecdote, but it is a clean case of H4b: a reward with no challenge felt worthless, even insulting. Juul offers no data in this excerpt.

**F5.7. Steve Swink, "Game Feel: The Secret Ingredient," *Gamasutra* (now *Game Developer*), November 23, 2007; later the book *Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation* (Elsevier).** [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-feel-the-secret-ingredient). Article read in full; the book was not opened.
PRACTITIONER. Game feel is "the tactile sensation of manipulating a digital agent," "mostly subconscious." Of *Super Mario 64* he writes, "I love to just run and bounce and spin, experiencing the sheer kinetic joy of controlling Mario." He notes that most play time is spent "steering around, running, jumping," apart from the objectives.
Bears on H1, H3. **Refutes H1 as the only unit.** Swink describes a pleasure of moment-to-moment control that does not need a goal or an obstacle. Koster would call this the mastery of a bodily pattern (F5.1). But Swink's "thoughtless joy" comes *after* the controls are learned, which is the stage where Koster's theory predicts boredom.

**F5.8. Ian Bogost, *Play Anything: The Pleasure of Limits, the Uses of Boredom, and the Secret of Games* (Basic Books, 2016).** The book was not opened. Read through James D. Ivory's review, *American Journal of Play* (Fall 2017), 116-118 ([PDF](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/10-1-Book-review-1.pdf), full text read). The often-quoted definition ("Fun is not pleasure, it is novelty... the aftermath of deliberately manipulating a familiar situation in a new way") was seen only in search summaries and is unverified.
THEORY. Per Ivory, Bogost argues "that an important element of play is not that it is pleasurable and unstructured, but that it often involves the risk and frustration of the challenges of an artificially imposed set of constraints." Rules "provide structure for interesting and creative choices." Play "is not in us, but in the things with which we play and the constraints around them."
Bears on H1, H6. **Confirms H1 (constraints and frustration); challenges H6.** Bogost backs the effortful, rule-bound unit. But he locates play in the *material and its limits*, not in the person's temperament. That is a counterweight to H6: on his view, almost anything can be played if one attends to its constraints.

**F5.9. Brian Upton, *The Aesthetic of Play* (MIT Press, 2015).** Only the publisher's description was read, via [The Video Game Library](https://www.thevideogamelibrary.org/book/the-aesthetic-of-play). The MIT Press page refused access.
THEORY. The book examines "how playful activities unfold from moment to moment and how the rules we adopt constrain that unfolding." It extends a play-based stance to "books, of music, of theater, of art."
Bears on H1, H5. **Neutral (too little read).** The description suggests a moment-to-moment, constraint-based account that could bear on H5's chain. A full reading is an open thread.

**F5.10. Sid Meier, "Interesting Decisions," GDC 2012 session.** [GDC Vault session page](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015756/Interesting), abstract read. Report: Leigh Alexander, "GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions," *Game Developer*, March 7, 2012 ([link](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)), read in full.
PRACTITIONER. Meier's own abstract says that "Long, long ago at a GDC far, far away" he "introduced the idea that a useful way of looking at gameplay is as 'a series of interesting decisions.'" He calls interesting decisions situational, with tradeoffs, risk against reward, and room for personal style. He grants that for "rhythm games or puzzle games," the idea "isn't the best way to look at things."
Bears on H1, H5. **Confirms H5's chain shape; refines H1.** The attribution checks out as Meier's own idea. The year (often given as 1989) is **unverified**: his abstract gives no year, and only search summaries name 1989. Meier frames it as "a useful way of looking at gameplay," not a definition of fun. His unit is a *decision* (a mental challenge with a tradeoff) rather than an effortful obstacle. By his own account, it does not cover rhythm and input-skill games well.

### Topic 6. The smallest unit, as designers define it

**F6.1. Daniel Cook, "The Chemistry of Game Design," *Gamasutra*, July 19, 2007.** [Game Developer (full 2007 text)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-chemistry-of-game-design), read in full. A revised repost, [Lost Garden, March 13, 2021](https://lostgarden.com/2021/03/13/the-chemistry-of-game-design-2/), was also read; it adds a fifth step, "Decision," before Action.
PRACTITIONER / THEORY. The skill atom is a loop of four steps: action (the player acts), simulation (the game's ongoing simulation updates), feedback (the game shows the result), and modeling (the player takes in the feedback and updates their mental models). "The sensation that gamers term 'fun' is derived from the act of mastering knowledge, skills and tools." Skill chains are directed graphs in which "the skill from one atom feeds into the actions of another atom further down the chain." Burnout comes when players "master a new skill, they play with it but fail to find any interesting use for it." Cook grants that games also hold "story, setting, and imagery intended to evoke a particular mood," which his model does not cover.
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Confirms H5 strongly; refines H1 and H3.** Cook's skill chain is the closest designer's version of H5: units linked so that each one's mastery feeds the next. His burnout is H5's failure mode: a mastered unit with nowhere to go. But his atom differs from H1 in two ways. (1) The fun is in the *Modeling* step, the update of the player's understanding, not in a reward handed out after the obstacle. Feedback is information, not prize. (2) Cook's atom needs no explicit "challenge." It needs only something not yet understood.

**F6.2. Daniel Cook, "Loops and Arcs," *Lost Garden*, April 30, 2012.** [Lost Garden](https://lostgarden.com/2012/04/30/loops-and-arcs). Read in full.
PRACTITIONER. A loop: "The player starts with a mental model that prompts them to apply an action to the game system and in return receives feedback that updates their mental model." Loops "are fractal and occur at multiple levels and frequencies throughout a game." They "deliver value through the act of being exercised." An arc "is a broken loop you exit immediately," "not built for repeated usage." In an arc "the feedback is 99% of the payload," and "people burn out on them rapidly."
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Refines.** Cook names two kinds of unit. Loops fit H1 and H5: repeated, effortful, building skill. Arcs (a story beat, a reveal, a cutscene) are a payoff with almost no challenge, and players still value them. On H3, loops give their value *while* being exercised, not only at the end. That is the second source in this file, after Suits (F4.1), to say the payoff runs during the effort.

**F6.3. Raph Koster, "A Grammar of Gameplay: Game Atoms: Can Games Be Diagrammed?", Game Developers Conference, 2005.** [Slides, raphkoster.com (PDF)](https://www.raphkoster.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/A-Grammar-of-Gameplay.pdf). Full slide text read.
PRACTITIONER / THEORY. "All games are sequences of challenges." Games can be seen as chained (sequenced), recursive ("A given challenge is part of a meta-challenge, or includes challenges within itself") and parallel ("multiple challenges undertaken at once"). Atoms "must always have a failure state link, even if said failure is only an opportunity cost." "Nesting ends when there is no skill involved in executing the verb." For the heart of a game, "Crawford says 'verbs.' Meier says 'choices.' Others say 'conflict.'" Koster sides with verbs. He also calls for variable feedback: "more than one possible outcome to the successful completion of the challenge in an atom."
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Confirms H1 and H5; refines H3.** Koster's atom *is* a challenge with a chance of failure, and the smallest atom is the last one that still takes skill. That matches H1's "indivisible" claim closely. Chaining, nesting and parallel atoms give H5 a richer shape than a simple series. On H3, the variable-feedback rule says the payoff should *not* be fixed: the result of a won challenge should vary. Note that Koster's slides also say "Some challenges are risk-free as atoms," which sits awkwardly beside his failure-state rule.

**F6.4. Jaime Griesemer on Halo's "30 seconds of fun," interviewed by Ludwig Kietzmann, "Half-Minute Halo: An Interview with Jaime Griesemer," *Joystiq/Engadget*, July 14, 2011.** [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2011-07-14-half-minute-halo-an-interview-with-jaime-griesemer.html). Read in full.
PRACTITIONER. Per the interview, the phrase came from the 2002 GDC talk "The Illusion of Intelligence," by Griesemer and Chris Butcher. The loop: "Encountering a bunch of guys, melee attacking one of them before they were aware, throwing a grenade into a group of other guys, and then cleaning up the stragglers." The often-omitted second half, cut from a studio video per a search summary, is to play that loop "in different environments, with different weapons, different vehicles, against different enemies." He describes "a 3-second loop inside of a 30-second loop inside of a 3-minute loop that is always different."
Bears on H1, H5. **Confirms H5; refines it.** This is a widely cited core loop, and it is a chain of short challenges, as H5 says. Griesemer's own point is that repetition alone is not enough: the same unit must come back in *changing contexts*, nested at several time scales. The often-quoted half ("30 seconds of fun... over and over") leaves out the variation that, by his account, makes it work.

### Topic 7. Formal and computational theories

**F7.1. Jürgen Schmidhuber, "Formal Theory of Creativity, Fun, and Intrinsic Motivation (1990-2010)," *IEEE Transactions on Autonomous Mental Development* 2, no. 3 (September 2010): 230-247.** [Author's preprint, IDSIA (PDF)](https://people.idsia.ch/~juergen/ieeecreative.pdf). Full text read. Journal details from the [IEEE Xplore record](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5508364/references) as shown in search results.
THEORY (formal model; the agents built on it are machine-learning demonstrations, not studies of human fun). Fun is "intrinsic reward for the active creation or discovery of novel, surprising patterns allowing for improved prediction or data compression." The reward is the "first derivative of learning progress," a measure of "subjective surprise or fun." Both extremes are boring. A dark room is fully predictable; white noise is "fundamentally incompressible." "Neither the arbitrary nor the fully predictable is truly novel or surprising." There are "at least two ways of having fun": find a better compression of data one already has, or act to get new data and then learn to compress it.
Bears on H1, H3, H5. **Refines all three.** In this model, the unit of fun is a *gain in understanding*, not the overcoming of an obstacle. Effort enters only as the work of learning. The payoff is the *rate* of improvement, so it is bigger when the gain is bigger, which loosely echoes H4's proportion. But it is a different measure from the author's: progress, not the size of the challenge. The first way of having fun ("improves the compression of the already known data") needs no new challenge at all, only insight. On H5, the theory predicts that a pattern stops being fun once it is learned, so fun needs a supply of new, learnable patterns: a chain, but a chain of *learnable novelty*. H2 is untouched; the theory says nothing about safety.

**F7.2. Pierre-Yves Oudeyer and colleagues, "Intelligent Adaptive Curiosity," as quoted in Schmidhuber (2010), section IV.** Same source and reading; the Oudeyer paper itself was not opened (Job 2 covers Oudeyer and Gottlieb).
THEORY. Oudeyer's method moves a learning robot toward places with the fastest expected learning progress. Schmidhuber quotes it as keeping the robot "away both from situations which are too predictable and from situations which are too unpredictable."
Bears on H1, H5. **Refines.** This is the computational form of a "Goldilocks" zone. The selection rule is *learning progress*, not challenge size or reward size.

**F7.3. Adrien F. Baranes, Pierre-Yves Oudeyer and Jacqueline Gottlieb, "The effects of task difficulty, novelty and the size of the search space on intrinsically motivated exploration," *Frontiers in Neuroscience* 8 (2014): 317.** [PMC4196545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4196545) and [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2014.00317/full). Methods, results and discussion read through the fetch tool's extracts, not line by line.
MEASURED. 52 adults freely chose among short computer games (a dot-interception task) of varying difficulty, with no money or other external reward. They "started with easier games and progressed to more difficult games." They "sampled the entire choice set including extremely difficult games that could not be learnt." They "repeated moderately and high difficulty games much more frequently than was predicted by chance." But they "had very poor subjective estimates of their rates of progress." Instead they reported heuristics based on their success rates. The authors caution that the learning here was "implicit and slow" and may not carry over to fast, explicit learning.
Bears on H1, H5. **Confirms H1 and H5 for free choice; refines the learning-progress account.** Left alone, with no prize, people sought out harder tasks and climbed in difficulty over time, which is what H1 and H5 predict. But people did not track their own learning progress well, so a conscious sense of "I'm improving" may not be the reward. A sense of success rate was what they reported using. Two limits: one lab task, and enjoyment was not measured, only choice.

### Topic 8. Humor as a micro-unit test

The question: does a joke fit the pattern of challenge, effort, resolution and reward? A joke is a useful test because it is very short and very clearly "fun."

**F8.1. J. M. Suls, "A two-stage model for the appreciation of jokes and cartoons," in *The Psychology of Humor*, ed. Goldstein and McGhee (1972).** Suls's chapter was not opened. His model is read as summarized in Andrea C. Samson, Stefan Zysset and Oswald Huber, "Cognitive humor processing: Different logical mechanisms in nonverbal cartoons, an fMRI study," *Social Neuroscience* 3, no. 2 (2008): 125-140 ([UNIGE open archive, PDF](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/ff40ac68-b013-4d91-8b0a-feef063f0965/download)), introduction read in full.
THEORY. Samson and colleagues liken incongruity resolution to problem solving, citing Suls (1972): "A cognitive rule has to be found to bring two incongruous scripts together." In stage one, the listener meets an incongruity with what was expected. In stage two, the listener finds the rule that makes it fit.
Bears on H1, H3. **Confirms (for this kind of humor).** Incongruity-resolution humor maps well onto the author's unit: a small puzzle (the incongruity), a small effort (finding the rule), and a payoff (mirth). The whole unit runs in a second or two, which shows how small the "indivisible unit" can be. The "effort" here is fast and mostly involuntary, though.

**F8.2. E. Zigler, J. Levine and L. Gould, "Cognitive challenge as a factor in children's humor appreciation," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 6, no. 3 (1967): 332-336.** [PubMed record 6075215](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6075215) opened (it has no abstract). Samson, Zysset and Huber (2008, above) summarize: Zigler and colleagues "stated that the humor response depends on the demand that the stimulus makes on cognitive capacities." The detailed result, that cartoons at the upper limit of a child's understanding drew the most laughter, was seen only in a search summary and is **unverified**.
MEASURED (study not opened; result known only at second hand). Children were shown a set of cartoons. (Two search summaries disagree on the children's grades, which is one more reason to treat the details as unverified.) Per the summaries, mirth rose with comprehension, and the cartoons at the edge of each child's ability were funniest. This later became the "cognitive congruency principle."
Bears on H1, H4. **Confirms, if the detail holds.** A joke too easy to get, or too hard to get, is less funny than one that stretches the listener just enough. That is a challenge-fit effect inside a single joke, close to H1 and to the "proportion" idea. It needs the full paper to confirm.

**F8.3. Andrea C. Samson, Christian F. Hempelmann, Oswald Huber and Stefan Zysset, "Neural substrates of incongruity-resolution and nonsense humor," *Neuropsychologia* 47, no. 4 (2009): 1023-1033.** [UNIGE open archive, PDF](https://access.archive-ouverte.unige.ch/access/metadata/09f06b2e-6e63-40b8-9466-748bbacb29fe/download). Abstract and introduction read in full; methods skimmed.
MEASURED (fMRI with 17 participants; it also reviews Willibald Ruch's factor-analytic humor studies). Ruch's work splits jokes and cartoons by structure. In incongruity-resolution humor the incongruity "is (almost) completely resolvable." In nonsense humor the punch line "may provide no resolution at all, provide a very partial resolution..., or actually create new absurdities." In the reviewed studies, resolvability "explains more variance of the funniness ratings than the content of a joke." Experience seekers prefer nonsense humor, and low experience seekers show the reverse. The authors propose that with nonsense, "people laugh about the absurdity of the two (almost) incompatible scripts rather than about the result of a playful and successful incongruity-resolution process." The paper's introduction also notes the view that laughter began with "non-serious incongruity," a sign of "social play and safety."
Bears on H1, H2, H3, H6. **Refutes H1 as universal for humor; supports H6; links to H2.** Nonsense humor is fun *without* a resolution. The incongruity is the pleasure, and the problem is never solved. So the "overcoming" step of H1/H3 is not needed for all humor. Which kind a person enjoys tracks a temperament trait (experience seeking), which supports H6 directly. The "safety" signal behind laughter is a theory cited in the paper, not tested in it.

**F8.4. Matthew M. Hurley, Daniel C. Dennett and Reginald B. Adams Jr., *Inside Jokes: Using Humor to Reverse-Engineer the Mind* (MIT Press, 2011).** The book was not opened. Read via the publisher's description at [Penguin Random House](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/655433/inside-jokes-by-matthew-m-hurley-daniel-c-dennett-and-reginald-b-adams-jr/9780262518697) and Taylor McNeil, "Everyone's a Comedian: A New Theory of How Humor Works," *Tufts Now*, April 10, 2012 ([link](https://now.tufts.edu/2012/04/10/everyones-comedian)). Both read in full.
THEORY. Natural selection cannot simply order the brain to catch and fix its hasty mistakes in reasoning; "She has to bribe the brain with pleasure." In the Tufts interview: "We catch the error—we'd been expecting one thing, and when another pops up instead, it strikes us as funny." And of why it feels good: "It's important to survival, and the only way it gets done is if it's fun." Humorists exploit this, so "we have become addicted to the endogenous mind candy that is humor." (The book's technical phrase about "covertly entered" beliefs was seen only in search results and is not quoted here.)
Bears on H1, H3. **Refines.** In this theory the unit is not "overcome a challenge, get a reward." It is "*catch your own mistake*, get a reward." The reward is real and comes after a small mental act, which fits H3. But the act is error-detection, not effort against an obstacle. The theory also explains why fun can be farmed: comedians (and, by analogy, designers) supply mistakes to catch with no real stakes, the "mind candy" the publisher's text describes.

**F8.5. Cross-references within this file.** Kant (F1.6) defines laughter as "the sudden transformation of a strained expectation into nothing." He insists it is *not* a resolution into "the positive opposite of an expected object." Schmidhuber (F7.1) explains a punch line as a moment of quick compression progress, and says the progress "is zero whenever the unexpected is just random noise, and thus no fun at all."
THEORY. Bears on H1, H3. **Conflicting.** Kant's account (tension released into nothing) has no solved problem and no prize. Schmidhuber's and Suls's accounts do have a solved problem. Nonsense humor (F8.3) sides with Kant's version. The net reading for humor is in Section 5.

## 4. Conflicts between sources

1. **Is effort essential to play?** Groos (F1.8: "Pleasure in overcoming difficulties is an essential feature of all play"), Huizinga's tension (F2.2), Suits (F4.1, F4.2), Koster's grammar (F6.3) and Lazzaro's Hard Fun (F5.2) say yes. Caillois's *alea* ("negates work, patience, experience, and qualifications," F3.2) and *ilinx* (F3.3), Kant's "play of fortune" (F1.6), Juul's happiness at winning by chance (F5.5), Swink's "kinetic joy" (F5.7) and Lazzaro's Easy Fun say no. The split mostly follows *which play* each author studies. Contest and skill theorists find effort; chance, vertigo, sensation and curiosity theorists do not.
2. **Can fun be broken into parts at all?** Huizinga says the fun of play "resists all analysis" (F2.3). Sutton-Smith calls play ambiguous at root (F4.3). MDA says there is "no Grand Unified Theory" (F5.3). Against them, Koster (F5.1), Cook (F6.1) and Schmidhuber (F7.1) each propose one mechanism (learning or compression progress). They get there partly by narrowing the word "fun" (Koster excludes delight and comfort; Cook sets aside story and mood).
3. **Does the payoff come after the effort or during it?** Lazzaro's fiero (F5.2), Juul's "fair chance of redeeming ourselves" (F5.6) and Huizinga's "end a tension" (F2.2) put it after. Suits ("the activity of trying to win" as its own end, F4.1), Nguyen's striving play (F4.5), Cook's loops that "deliver value through the act of being exercised" (F6.2) and Swink (F5.7) put much of it during.
4. **Does humor need a resolution?** Suls (F8.1), Schmidhuber (F7.1) and Hurley, Dennett and Adams (F8.4) say a joke works by resolving or correcting something. Kant (F1.6, F8.5: "a strained expectation into nothing") and nonsense humor (F8.3) say a joke can work with no resolution at all. The measured evidence (Ruch's work, via F8.3) supports *both kinds*, and people differ in which they prefer.
5. **Is learning progress the felt reward?** Schmidhuber (F7.1) and Koster (F5.1) make learning progress the reward. Baranes, Oudeyer and Gottlieb (F7.3) found that people choosing freely did seek harder tasks, but "had very poor subjective estimates of their rates of progress." This does not refute an unconscious progress signal. It does weaken any version of the theory in which players consciously weigh "how much am I improving."
6. **Is play safe?** Bateson (F2.6), Huizinga's dogs (F2.4), Salen and Zimmerman (F2.5) and Schiller (F1.5) tie play to a frame that keeps harm out. Huizinga's tournaments (F2.4), Juul's "negotiable consequences" (F5.5) and Hurka's golfers playing for money (F4.5) show real costs inside play, chosen on purpose.
7. **Koster against himself.** His grammar slides say atoms "must always have a failure state link" and also that "Some challenges are risk-free as atoms" (F6.3). His 2012 talk excludes "delight" from fun (F5.1) while Schell makes surprise the core of fun (F5.4).
8. **Where does play live?** H6 places fit in the person. Bogost, per Ivory's review, places play "not in us, but in the things with which we play" (F5.8). MDA (F5.3) and Ruch's humor data (F8.3) side with H6. Bogost's view is a philosophical stance, not a measured result.

## 5. Verdict per claim

**H1, the unit (an effortful challenge is the smallest unit of fun).**
- Strongest for: Groos, *The Play of Man* (F1.8, THEORY); Huizinga on tension (F2.2, THEORY); Suits (F4.1, F4.2, THEORY); Koster, "A Grammar of Gameplay" (F6.3, PRACTITIONER); Lazzaro's Hard Fun and fiero (F5.2, weak MEASURED); Baranes, Oudeyer and Gottlieb, where people freely chose harder tasks with no prize (F7.3, MEASURED).
- Strongest against: Caillois's *alea* and *ilinx* (F3.2, F3.3, THEORY); Juul on winning by pure chance (F5.5, THEORY); MDA's eight aesthetics, only one of which is Challenge (F5.3, THEORY/PRACTITIONER); Lazzaro's three non-challenge keys (F5.2, weak MEASURED); nonsense humor (F8.3, MEASURED); Swink (F5.7, PRACTITIONER); Aristotle's amusement as rest (F1.1, F1.2, THEORY).
- Net reading: **partly supported.** H1 is a strong account of *one family* of fun: contest, skill and puzzle fun (Caillois's *agon* and *ludus*; Lazzaro's Hard Fun). It is contradicted as the single, indivisible unit of *all* fun. Chance, vertigo, sensation, curiosity, company, rest and nonsense humor are all called play or fun by these sources without an effortful challenge. Even Groos, one of H1's strongest classic backers, puts a second root ("joy in being a cause") beside it.

**H2, safety (usually non-damaging, generally non-lethal).** (Job 3 is the main test.)
- Strongest for: Bateson's playful nip (F2.6, THEORY); Huizinga's "not serious" and the dog rule "not bite hard" (F2.1, F2.4, THEORY); Salen and Zimmerman, the frame "creates a game's feeling of safety" (F2.5, THEORY); Caillois's "separate" and "make-believe" (F3.1, THEORY).
- Strongest against: Huizinga's lethal early tournaments (F2.4, THEORY); Juul's "negotiable consequences" (F5.5, THEORY); Hurka on wanting real stakes for real pressure (F4.5, THEORY).
- Net reading: **partly supported.** The hedged wording ("usually," "generally") fits the sources well. The sources point to a frame condition rather than an outcome condition: the activity is *understood* as separate from real consequence, or its real stakes are chosen and agreed.

**H3, payoff (getting through delivers a positive benefit or reaction).**
- Strongest for: Lazzaro's fiero (F5.2, weak MEASURED); Cook, fun "derived from the act of mastering" (F6.1, PRACTITIONER); Juul's fair chance of redemption (F5.6, THEORY); Hurley, Dennett and Adams, mirth as the brain's reward (F8.4, THEORY); Schmidhuber's intrinsic reward (F7.1, THEORY).
- Strongest against or refining: Suits and Nguyen, where the striving itself is the end and the goal serves it (F4.1, F4.5, THEORY); Cook's loops, valued while exercised (F6.2, PRACTITIONER); Huizinga's "no profit" and Caillois's "unproductive," which rule out material reward (F2.1, F3.1, THEORY); Kant's laughter "into nothing" (F1.6, THEORY).
- Net reading: **supported, with two corrections.** Almost every source names a positive payoff. But (1) much of it arrives *during* the effort, not only after, and (2) it is an *inner* payoff (triumph, mastery, mirth, insight). Material reward is either excluded from play (Huizinga, Caillois) or an optional add-on (Juul).

**H5, chains (consistent fun is an interconnected series of units).**
- Strongest for: Cook's skill chains and burnout (F6.1, PRACTITIONER); Koster's chaining, nesting and parallel atoms (F6.3, PRACTITIONER); Griesemer's nested 3-second, 30-second and 3-minute loops (F6.4, PRACTITIONER); Meier's "series of interesting decisions" (F5.10, PRACTITIONER); Schmidhuber's need for a fresh supply of learnable patterns (F7.1, THEORY); Baranes and colleagues' players climbing in difficulty (F7.3, MEASURED).
- Strongest against: none found that denies chains. The limits are Cook's arcs, one-shot units that are not repeated (F6.2); Aristotle's view of amusement as rest *between* exertions (F1.1); and Meier's own exception for rhythm and input-skill games (F5.10).
- Net reading: **supported, mostly by practitioner and theory sources.** Job 1 found almost no measured test of chaining as such. The sources add three conditions H5 does not state: *variation* (the same unit in changing contexts), *nesting* at several time scales, and a *supply of novelty* to head off burnout.

**Incidental readings (main tests in Jobs 2 and 3):**
- **H4/H4b:** Juul's *Meteos* anecdote (F5.6) is a vivid case of H4b, an unearned win that felt insulting. Zigler and colleagues (F8.2, details unverified) and Nguyen's "appropriate difficulty" (F4.5) point to a fit between challenge and player. Groos (F1.8) ties pleasure to the size of the *effect*, not of the challenge.
- **H6:** MDA's "different games appeal to different players, or to the same players at different times" (F5.3) and Ruch's experience-seeking data on humor (F8.3) support H6. Bogost (F5.8) leans the other way.

## 6. Refinements the evidence suggests

These are options for the author, not decisions. Several can be combined.

1. **Scope the unit to one family of fun.** Keep H1 as the unit of *challenge fun* (Caillois's *agon* and *ludus*, Lazzaro's Hard Fun, Koster's atoms). Then name the other families the sources keep finding, each possibly with its own unit: chance (*alea*), sensation and vertigo (*ilinx*, game feel), curiosity and discovery (Easy Fun), company (People Fun), rest and relief (Aristotle), and delight.
2. **Widen "challenge" to "tension that resolves."** Huizinga's tension and "will it come off?", Caillois's uncertainty, Kant's strained expectation and Sutton-Smith's mastered emotion all fit a unit of *a tension or uncertainty that resolves*. Effort is then one way to resolve it (contest), and chance another (dice). This keeps one unit for more kinds of fun, at the cost of losing "effort" as a defining part.
3. **Or widen it to "a gap in understanding that closes."** Koster, Cook, Schmidhuber and the incongruity-resolution humor theorists would put it this way. It explains puzzles, discovery and many jokes well. It explains chance, vertigo, comfort and nonsense humor poorly.
4. **Let the payoff run during the effort.** Add that the reward can be felt *while* striving (Suits, Nguyen, Cook's loops, Swink), not only on the far side. One option is a two-part payoff: a running reward for engaged effort, and a peak at resolution (fiero, mirth).
5. **Make the payoff inner by definition.** Following Huizinga ("no profit") and Caillois ("unproductive"), the payoff could be stated as triumph, mastery, insight or mirth. Material stakes would then be optional add-ons (Juul's negotiable consequences). Job 2's overjustification evidence bears on what happens when they are large.
6. **Restate H2 as a frame condition.** For example: "the challenge sits inside a frame the player understands as separate from real consequence, or whose real stakes the player chose and agreed to" (Bateson, Huizinga, Salen and Zimmerman, Juul, Hurka). This keeps H2's spirit and covers wagers, sports injuries and haunted houses.
7. **Add voluntariness.** Suits ("voluntary"), Huizinga ("free") and Caillois ("free") all treat free choice as essential. Zhuangzi's cook (F1.7) shows the same challenge-and-triumph unit inside paid work. So the unit alone may not separate fun from work; free choice may be what does (Job 2 tests this).
8. **Add uncertainty of outcome.** Caillois, Huizinga and Koster's "variable feedback" (F6.3) all require that the result not be settled in advance. A challenge whose outcome is certain may not be fun, however much effort it takes.
9. **Add three conditions to H5:** variation (Griesemer), nesting at several time scales (Griesemer, Koster), and a steady supply of new learnable material to prevent burnout (Cook, Koster, Schmidhuber). The chain could also be stated as loops interleaved with arcs (Cook): repeatable challenges mixed with one-time payoffs such as story beats.
