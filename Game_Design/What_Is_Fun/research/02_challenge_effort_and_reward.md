# Job 2: challenge, effort and reward

## 1. Scope
This file tests claims H3 (payoff), H4 (proportion), H4a (too small a reward makes it work), H4b (too large a reward feels cheap) and the line between work and play. It touches H1, H5 and H6 where the same sources bear on them. The nine topics come from the brief of November 1, 2026: flow; challenge and enjoyment experiments; optimal complexity; reward in the brain; rewards that undermine fun; effort that raises value; fairness of reward; video-game difficulty studies; and what makes an activity feel like work.

How sources were read is stated on each finding. "Full text" means the whole paper was opened (usually a PDF converted to text). "Abstract" means the publisher's or an index's abstract (often the OpenAlex record, api.openalex.org). "Search summary only" means only a search engine's summary was seen; such items are marked unverified. Where a paper is known only through another paper that cites it, the finding says so.

A note on the word "reward." The author's theory does not say whether the reward is inside the activity (pride, relief, suspense resolved, a skill learned) or outside it (money, points, loot, praise). The literature splits sharply on this line, so the findings below say which kind each source means.

## 3. Findings

### Topic 1. Flow

**F1.1** Csikszentmihalyi, M., and LeFevre, J. (1989). "Optimal experience in work and leisure." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 56(5): 815-822. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.56.5.815. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record and https://onwork.edu.au/bibitem/1989-Csikszentmihalyi,M-LeFevre,J-Optimal+experience+in+work+and+leisure/).
- Kind: MEASURED (experience sampling, 78 adult workers, one week).
- Says: Flow (high challenge and high skill) shaped the quality of experience more than whether people were at work or at leisure. "The great majority of flow experiences are reported when working, not when in leisure." Yet people were "more motivated in leisure than in work."
- Bears on: H4a, the work/play line, H1.
- Direction: refines. The activity that held the most challenge (work) was the one people were least motivated to be doing. So "work" is not simply challenge with too little reward. Something about the frame (obligation, lack of choice) separates them. Topic 9 returns to this.

**F1.2** Csikszentmihalyi's channel model (*Beyond Boredom and Anxiety*, 1975) and the later quadrant model (Massimini and Carli, 1988), as described in Engeser and Rheinberg (2008, full text, see F1.3). I did not open the 1975 book itself.
- Kind: THEORY.
- Says: Flow occurs when perceived skill meets perceived challenge. Challenge above skill gives anxiety; skill above challenge gives boredom. The quadrant model adds that flow needs both to be high; low challenge with low skill gives apathy.
- Bears on: H1, H4.
- Direction: refines. Flow's balance is between challenge and *skill*, not between challenge and *reward*. In flow theory the reward is the experience itself. This is a different two-sided proportion from H4, but it has the same shape: too little and too much both fail.

**F1.3** Engeser, S., and Rheinberg, F. (2008). "Flow, performance and moderators of challenge-skill balance." *Motivation and Emotion* 32(3): 158-172. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-008-9102-4. Read: full text (author copy, https://www.uni-trier.de/fileadmin/fb1/prof/PSY/PGA/bilder/Engeser___Rheinberg_2008.pdf).
- Kind: MEASURED (three studies: a statistics course, N = 246; Pac-Man at set difficulty levels; a French course).
- Says: The balance hypothesis "could only be partially supported." In the statistics course, "flow was more intense when demand was low or just right," and fell when demand was too high. The neat inverted-U between balance and flow held mainly for activities of low perceived importance (Pac-Man fit the model). For important activities, people reported flow "even if skill exceeds difficulty." The achievement motive also moderated the pattern: the balance effect held for people high in "hope of success." The authors note earlier studies found only small effect sizes, and that skill exceeding challenge often gave positive affect.
- Bears on: H4, H6, the work/play line.
- Direction: refines. A two-sided "sweet spot" shows up most cleanly in low-stakes play. When the outcome matters, people prefer to be over-skilled. Stakes change the shape of the curve.

**F1.4** Fong, C. J., Zaleski, D. J., and Leach, J. K. (2015; online 2014). "The challenge-skill balance and antecedents of flow: A meta-analytic investigation." *The Journal of Positive Psychology* 10(5): 425-446. https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2014.967799. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (meta-analysis of 28 studies).
- Says: The link between challenge-skill balance and flow was "moderate, and smaller with intrinsic motivation." It was weaker in individualistic cultures, in work or education settings, and with experience sampling. Balance was a robust contributor "along with clear goals and sense of control."
- Bears on: H4, H6, H1.
- Direction: partly confirms (matched challenge matters) and refines (it is one condition among several; clear goals and a sense of control matter as much).

**F1.5** Baumann, N., Lürig, C., and Engeser, S. (2016). "Flow and enjoyment beyond skill-demand balance: The role of game pacing curves and personality." *Motivation and Emotion* 40: 507-519. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-016-9549-7. Read: full text (author copy, uni-trier.de).
- Kind: MEASURED (computer-game experiment with three pacing conditions, plus personality measures).
- Says: Constant balance was not best. Flow was highest with "dynamic high" pacing (slight overload broken by short rests). Global enjoyment was highest with "dynamic medium" pacing (fluctuating, no overload). So flow and enjoyment came apart. High sensation seekers enjoyed the harder pacing more; low sensation seekers enjoyed constant balance more. The paper notes that "not every form of enjoyment is flow (e.g., lying on the beach is enjoyable but most likely without the experience of flow)," and that many daily-life studies found no link, or a negative one, between global enjoyment and challenge.
- Second-hand in the same paper (not opened by me): Schneider (1973) found people treat a task as "50-50" when its objective success rate is lower (about 37 percent in tabletop soccer).
- Bears on: H1, H4, H5, H6.
- Direction: refines. Enjoyment is not maximized by the hardest fitting challenge. A rhythm of tension and rest beats a flat line, which supports H5's "series of units" and suggests the units need spacing. Personality shifts the best point (H6).

### Topic 2. Challenge and enjoyment experiments

**F2.1** Abuhamdeh, S., and Csikszentmihalyi, M. (2012). "The importance of challenge for the enjoyment of intrinsically motivated, goal-directed activities." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 38(3): 317-330. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211427147. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record), plus details as reported by Lomas et al. 2013 (full text, see F8.2) and Baumann et al. 2016 (full text, F1.5).
- Kind: MEASURED (Study 1: Internet chess games with post-game ratings; Study 2: experience sampling across daily activities).
- Says: In chess, perceived challenge "strongly predicted enjoyment." Games against superior opponents were more enjoyable than games against weaker ones, and "close games were more enjoyable than blowouts." In daily life, the challenge-enjoyment link depended on whether the activity was intrinsically motivated and goal-directed. Lomas et al. report that, by their reading, peak enjoyment came at about a 20 percent chance of winning (an opponent rated about 262 points higher), and that close games were most enjoyed "regardless of the difference in chess ranking or who won." Baumann et al. report a companion finding: players most enjoyed games against stronger opponents in which they held a slight edge during play.
- Bears on: H1, H3, H4.
- Direction: confirms H1 for goal-directed, chosen activities. Refines H3: enjoyment of a close game did not depend on winning, so the payoff is not only the result at the end. Refines H1's reach: the link weakens for activities that are not goal-directed.

**F2.2** Abuhamdeh, S., Csikszentmihalyi, M., and Jalal, B. (2015). "Enjoying the possibility of defeat: Outcome uncertainty, suspense, and intrinsic motivation." *Motivation and Emotion* 39(1): 1-10. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-014-9425-2. Read: abstract (https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/enjoying-the-possibility-of-defeat-outcome-uncertainty-suspense-a/).
- Kind: MEASURED (two experiments with a competitive video game; Study 2 adds a free choice of which game to replay).
- Says: More outcome uncertainty gave more enjoyment, and suspense carried the effect. Winning "by a wide margin maximized perceived competence," but those games "were less enjoyable than closer games." People chose to replay the suspenseful games over the ones that made them feel most competent.
- Bears on: H3, H4b.
- Direction: confirms H4b in spirit (an easy, lopsided win gave the most "reward" in competence and the least enjoyment). Refines H3: much of the pleasure sits inside the challenge (suspense while the outcome is open), not only after it.

**F2.3** Atkinson, J. W. (1957). "Motivational determinants of risk-taking behavior." *Psychological Review* 64(6): 359-372. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043445. Read: full text (PDF copy, https://static1.squarespace.com/static/57309137ab48de6f423b3eec/t/588a1a9846c3c4746d0816d6/1485445785596/Atkinson1957.pdf).
- Kind: THEORY (formal model, with cited data).
- Says: The incentive value of success is set at Is = 1 - Ps, so the pride of success rises as the chance of success falls. "One cannot anticipate the thrill of a great accomplishment if, as a matter of fact, one faces what seems a very easy task." Motivation peaks at Ps = .50 for people whose motive to achieve outweighs their fear of failure; people high in fear of failure avoid exactly those middle tasks. After success, interest in the same task "should begin to wane," and the person moves up to a harder one.
- Bears on: H4, H4b, H5, H6.
- Direction: confirms H4 for the inner reward (pride). In Atkinson's model, inner reward is tied to difficulty by construction, so an easy task cannot give a large sense of achievement. That is H4b's "cheap and unearned," stated as a law of the inner reward. Also supports H5 (success pushes toward the next, harder unit) and H6 (the fear-of-failure type wants different tasks).

**F2.4** Atkinson, J. W. "Achievement Motivation." *International Encyclopedia of the Social Sciences* (entry reproduced at https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences/applied-and-social-sciences-magazines/achievement-motivation). Read: full entry through a fetch tool's summary (quotes checked as returned).
- Kind: THEORY (summary of the McClelland-Atkinson research program).
- Says: The tendency to achieve "is generally stronger when the motive to achieve (Ms) is strong and when the task is one of intermediate difficulty or risk." The entry treats extrinsic motives such as money and approval as separate sources of motivation that add to or compete with the achievement motive.
- Bears on: H4, H6.
- Direction: confirms the "middle difficulty" idea; refines H6 (the preference depends on a measurable motive).
- McClelland's own books (*The Achievement Motive*, 1953; *The Achieving Society*, 1961) were not opened. A popular summary says high achievers treat money "as a measurement of success, not an end in itself." I saw this only in search summaries, so it is unverified.

### Topic 3. Optimal complexity

**F3.1** Marin, M. M., Lampatz, A., Wandl, M., and Leder, H. (2016). "Berlyne revisited: Evidence for the multifaceted nature of hedonic tone in the appreciation of paintings and music." *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 10: 536. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00536. Read: full text through a fetch tool's summary (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5095118). Berlyne's own *Aesthetics and Psychobiology* (1971) and "Novelty, complexity, and hedonic value" (*Perception & Psychophysics* 8, 1970) could not be opened (publisher login wall).
- Kind: THEORY (Berlyne) and MEASURED (Marin et al.'s experiments).
- Says: Berlyne held that moderate arousal potential (from novelty, complexity, uncertainty, conflict) gives the highest hedonic value, an inverted U. Marin et al. state that "converging evidence for his theory is still missing." In their data, complexity raised arousal reliably, but liking did not follow a clean inverted U; the shape depended on whether one measured beauty, pleasantness or liking.
- Bears on: H4, H1.
- Direction: neutral to refines. An optimal middle for stimulation is an old and plausible idea, but its shape is not settled. Note this is a curve of pleasure against *challenge-like stimulation*, not against reward size.

**F3.2** Chmiel, A., and Schubert, E. (2017). "Back to the inverted-U for music preference: A review of the literature." *Psychology of Music* 45(6): 886-909. https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735617697507. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (review of 57 studies).
- Says: Fifty of 57 studies (87.7 percent) fit an overall, segmented inverted U between familiarity or complexity and preference; two were wholly incompatible.
- Bears on: H4 (by analogy), H1.
- Direction: confirms that pleasure peaks at a middle level of demand, at least for music.

**F3.3** Kidd, C., Piantadosi, S. T., and Aslin, R. N. (2012). "The Goldilocks effect: Human infants allocate attention to visual sequences that are neither too simple nor too complex." *PLoS ONE* 7(5): e36399. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0036399. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (two experiments, 7- and 8-month-olds).
- Says: Infants were most likely to look away from events whose complexity was "either very low or very high." The authors infer that infants "seek to maintain intermediate rates of information absorption."
- Bears on: H1, H4.
- Direction: refines. A two-sided middle shows up before any notion of goals, effort or reward exists. What is being balanced is information against the learner's model.

**F3.4** Gottlieb, J., Oudeyer, P.-Y., Lopes, M., and Baranes, A. (2013). "Information-seeking, curiosity, and attention: Computational and neural mechanisms." *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 17(11): 585-593. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2013.09.001. Read: full text through a fetch tool's summary (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4193662).
- Kind: THEORY (computational models), with a review of neural data.
- Says: Learning-progress models reward activities "in proportion to their ability to produce learning progress." This steers away from activities that are "already predictable" or "too difficult to learn to predict," and moves on when one activity plateaus. The dopamine system responds to anticipated information and to curiosity-inducing tasks.
- Bears on: H4, H5, H1.
- Direction: refines. Here the reward is proportional to *progress*, not to difficulty. Too-easy and too-hard both fail because neither produces progress. Moving on at a plateau matches H5's chain.

**F3.5** Baranes, A., Oudeyer, P.-Y., and Gottlieb, J. (2014). "The effects of task difficulty, novelty and the size of the search space on intrinsically motivated exploration." *Frontiers in Neuroscience* 8: 317. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00317. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (adults choosing freely among short computer games of varied difficulty, with no external reward).
- Says: With no rewards and no instructions, people "started with easier games and progressed to more difficult games." They sampled even games that "could not be learnt," and they repeated moderately and highly difficult games more often than chance. The authors read this as serving two inner goals: knowing the task set and maximizing competence across it.
- Bears on: H1, H5, H3.
- Direction: confirms H1 and H5 under free choice: people seek challenge with no outside payoff, and they climb a ladder of difficulty. Refines H3: the "reward" here is competence and knowledge, not anything handed out.

### Topic 4. Reward in the brain

**F4.1** Schultz, W. (2016). "Dopamine reward prediction error coding." *Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience* 18(1): 23-32. https://doi.org/10.31887/DCNS.2016.18.1/wschultz. Read: abstract (PubMed, PMID 27069377). Also Schultz, W., Dayan, P., and Montague, P. R. (1997). "A neural substrate of prediction and reward." *Science* 275(5306): 1593-1599. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.275.5306.1593. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (review of single-neuron recordings in monkeys, rodents and humans) and THEORY (learning models).
- Says: Most midbrain dopamine neurons signal the gap between received and predicted reward. They fire more for "more reward than predicted," stay at baseline "for fully predicted rewards," and dip for less than predicted. "Drugs of addiction generate, hijack, and amplify the dopamine reward signal."
- Bears on: H4, H4b, H5.
- Direction: refines. The brain's teaching signal scales reward against *expectation*, not against effort. A reward that is fully expected adds no signal, so a fixed payoff loses force with repetition; this fits H5's need for a changing chain. The signal itself carries no penalty for a reward that is bigger than expected, so it does not explain H4b's "cheap" feeling. My own inference, not the sources' claim: a hard task lowers the expectation of success, so success at it gives a bigger positive error. That would be one mechanism for Atkinson's link between difficulty and pride (F2.3).

**F4.2** Fiorillo, C. D., Tobler, P. N., and Schultz, W. (2003). "Discrete coding of reward probability and uncertainty by dopamine neurons." *Science* 299(5614): 1898-1902. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1077349. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (monkey recordings).
- Says: Besides the prediction-error burst, a slower response "covaried with uncertainty," rising until the time a reward might come. Uncertainty is greatest at a 50 percent chance.
- Bears on: H3, H4.
- Direction: refines. There is a neural signal that is largest when the outcome is most in doubt, during the wait, not after it. This lines up with Atkinson's 50 percent point (F2.3) and with suspense driving enjoyment (F2.2). Caution: Berridge (F4.3) ties dopamine to wanting, not to pleasure.

**F4.3** Berridge, K. C., and Robinson, T. E. (2016). "Liking, wanting, and the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction." *American Psychologist* 71(8): 670-679. https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000059. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record). Also Berridge, K. C., Robinson, T. E., and Aldridge, J. W. (2009). "Dissecting components of reward: 'liking', 'wanting', and learning." *Current Opinion in Pharmacology* 9(1): 65-73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.coph.2008.12.014. Read: full text through a fetch tool's summary (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2756052).
- Kind: MEASURED (mostly animal studies) and THEORY.
- Says: "Wanting" (the pull toward a reward) and "liking" (its pleasure) run on separate brain systems. Wanting uses large, robust dopamine systems. Liking uses "smaller and fragile" systems "not dependent on dopamine." In addiction, wanting can grow without liking, so a person can feel "a 'want' for what is not cognitively wanted."
- Bears on: H3, H4, and how fun should be measured.
- Direction: refines. Persistence and pleasure come apart. A reward schedule can keep people coming back (wanting) without being fun (liking). So time played or return visits are weak tests of fun.

**F4.4** Przybylski, A. K., Weinstein, N., Ryan, R. M., and Rigby, C. S. (2009). "Having versus wanting to play: Background and consequences of harmonious versus obsessive engagement in video games." *CyberPsychology & Behavior* 12(5): 485-492. https://doi.org/10.1089/cpb.2009.0083. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (survey of players).
- Says: Low satisfaction of basic needs went with "obsessive passion, higher amounts of play, greater tension following play, and low game enjoyment." High need satisfaction went with harmonious passion, enjoyment and energy after play, and did not predict hours played.
- Bears on: H3, the work/play line.
- Direction: refines. Compulsive play is real and is less fun. It feels like "having to," which links it to the work side of Topic 9.

**F4.5** Zendle, D., and Cairns, P. (2018). "Video game loot boxes are linked to problem gambling: Results of a large-scale survey." *PLoS ONE* 13(11): e0206767. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206767. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (survey, n = 7,422; correlational).
- Says: Spending on loot boxes (random paid rewards) was linked to problem-gambling severity (eta squared = 0.054), more strongly than other in-game spending (0.004). The direction of cause is unknown.
- Bears on: H3, H4.
- Direction: refines. Random reward schedules that are cut loose from any challenge are tied to compulsion, not to the fun the author describes. This is one more reason to keep the payoff linked to the challenge.

**F4.6** Koepp, M. J., Gunn, R. N., Lawrence, A. D., and colleagues (1998). "Evidence for striatal dopamine release during a video game." *Nature* 393(6682): 266-268. https://doi.org/10.1038/30498. Read: abstract (PubMed, PMID 9607763).
- Kind: MEASURED (PET imaging).
- Says: Dopamine was released in the human striatum during "a goal-directed motor task, namely a video game." The size of the release "positively correlated with the performance level."
- Bears on: H3.
- Direction: confirms that a goal-directed game engages the reward system, scaled by how well the player does. (Per F4.3, this shows wanting and learning, not proof of pleasure.)

**F4.7** Biederman, I., and Vessel, E. A. (2006). "Perceptual pleasure and the brain." *American Scientist* 94(3), starting p. 247. https://doi.org/10.1511/2006.3.247. The article page would not load (server error 503). Read: University of Southern California press release, June 21, 2006 (https://today.usc.edu/thirst-for-knowledge-may-be-opium-craving/).
- Kind: THEORY, resting on their imaging and rating studies (reported through the press release only).
- Says: Opioid receptors grow denser along the visual pathway toward areas that interpret what is seen. People prefer scenes that are "richly interpretable but novel." Repeated viewing lowers both pleasure ratings and activity in those areas. Biederman: "While you're trying to understand a difficult theorem, it's not fun." Once you get it, "you just feel fabulous."
- Bears on: H1, H3, H5.
- Direction: confirms H3 (the pleasure comes at the moment of understanding). Refines H1: the effort phase itself may not be enjoyed; the fun can sit in the payoff. Habituation supports H5's need for fresh units.

### Topic 5. Rewards that undermine fun

**F5.1** Deci, E. L. (1971). "Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 18(1): 105-115. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0030644. Read: abstract and opening text (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (two lab experiments and one field experiment).
- Says: "When money was used as an external reward, intrinsic motivation tended to decrease," while "verbal reinforcement and positive feedback" tended to increase it.
- Bears on: H4a, H3, the work/play line.
- Direction: refines. The kind of reward matters more than its size. Feedback helps; money hurts.

**F5.2** Lepper, M. R., Greene, D., and Nisbett, R. E. (1973). "Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the 'overjustification' hypothesis." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 28(1): 129-137. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (field experiment with preschool children).
- Says: Children who drew in order to get an expected award later showed less interest in drawing than children who got the same award by surprise or got none.
- Bears on: H4a, H4b, the work/play line.
- Direction: runs against H4a's mechanism. What lowered interest was *expecting* a reward for the activity. The surprise group got the same reward with no harm. Reward size relative to challenge was not the variable that mattered.

**F5.3** Lepper, M. R., and Greene, D. (1975). "Turning play into work: Effects of adult surveillance and extrinsic rewards on children's intrinsic motivation." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 31(3): 479-486. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076484. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (experiment with preschool children; interest measured two weeks later).
- Says: Expecting a reward lowered later interest, and so did being watched by a camera. The two effects were separate.
- Bears on: the work/play line, H4a.
- Direction: refines. Two things that have nothing to do with reward size, an expected payoff and being monitored, each pushed play toward work.

**F5.4** Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., and Ryan, R. M. (1999). "A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation." *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6): 627-668. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (meta-analysis of 128 studies).
- Says: Rewards given for doing, finishing, or performing well all lowered free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28). Positive feedback raised it (d = 0.33 for free choice; 0.31 for interest). Tangible rewards hurt children more than college students.
- Bears on: H4a, H4b, H3.
- Direction: refines, and partly runs against the author's direction. Rewards tied to performance, the kind closest to "proportional to the challenge," did the least harm but still did harm. Feedback that tells a person how well they did helped.

**F5.5** Cameron, J., and Pierce, W. D. (1994). "Reinforcement, reward, and intrinsic motivation: A meta-analysis." *Review of Educational Research* 64(3): 363-423. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064003363. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record). Also Cameron, J., Banko, K. M., and Pierce, W. D. (2001). "Pervasive negative effects of rewards on intrinsic motivation: The myth continues." *The Behavior Analyst* 24(1): 1-44. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03392017. Read: abstract (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2731358).
- Kind: MEASURED (meta-analyses).
- Says: Overall, "reward does not decrease intrinsic motivation." Rewards for low-interest tasks raise it. "Negative effects are found on high-interest tasks when the rewards are tangible, expected (offered beforehand), and loosely tied to level of performance." "When rewards are linked to level of performance, measures of intrinsic motivation increase or do not differ from a nonrewarded control group."
- Bears on: H4, H4a, H4b.
- Direction: refines in the author's favor. Read this way, the harmful reward is one that is out of step with how well the person did. A reward matched to performance is safe. This is close to H4, with "performance" in place of "challenge."

**F5.6** Eisenberger, R., and Cameron, J. (1996). "Detrimental effects of reward: Reality or myth?" *American Psychologist* 51(11): 1153-1166. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.51.11.1153. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (review).
- Says: Harmful effects of reward "occur under highly restricted, easily avoidable conditions."
- Bears on: H4a, H4b.
- Direction: refines (same side as F5.5).

**F5.7** Murayama, K., Matsumoto, M., Izuma, K., and Matsumoto, K. (2010). "Neural basis of the undermining effect of monetary reward on intrinsic motivation." *PNAS* 107(49): 20911-20916. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1013305107. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (fMRI experiment).
- Says: "Performance-based monetary reward indeed undermines intrinsic motivation," measured as voluntary play once the money stopped. Activity in the anterior striatum and prefrontal areas fell along with it.
- Bears on: H4a, H4b, Topic 4.
- Direction: refines. A performance-tied cash reward still undermined later free play, and the brain's valuation areas tracked the drop. This sits on the Deci side of the debate in F5.4 and F5.5.

**F5.8** Cerasoli, C. P., Nicklin, J. M., and Ford, M. T. (2014). "Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives jointly predict performance: A 40-year meta-analysis." *Psychological Bulletin* 140(4): 980-1008. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035661. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (meta-analysis, k = 183, N = 212,468).
- Says: Intrinsic motivation predicted the *quality* of performance; incentives better predicted *quantity*. Intrinsic motivation mattered less when incentives were "directly tied to performance" (crowding out).
- Bears on: the work/play line, H4.
- Direction: refines. Pay buys quantity; interest drives quality. Tight pay-for-performance crowds out the inner reason.

**F5.9** Gneezy, U., and Rustichini, A. (2000). "Pay enough or don't pay at all." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 115(3): 791-810. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355300554917. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (experiments).
- Says: Among paid groups, more money gave better performance. But "subjects who were offered monetary incentives performed more poorly than those who were offered no compensation" in some treatments; small pay did worse than none.
- Bears on: H4a.
- Direction: partly confirms H4a, with a twist. Once a payment is on the table, too small a payment does feel like a bad deal. But no payment at all did better than a small one. So the fix for "too small" is not only "bigger"; it can also be "none."

### Topic 6. Effort that raises value

**F6.1** Inzlicht, M., Shenhav, A., and Olivola, C. Y. (2018). "The effort paradox: Effort is both costly and valued." *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 22(4): 337-349. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2018.01.007. Read: full text through a fetch tool's summary (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6172040/).
- Kind: THEORY (review of human and animal evidence).
- Says: Effort is a cost; given equal rewards, organisms avoid the harder option. Yet "the same outcomes are more rewarding if we apply more (not less) effort." Evidence includes the IKEA effect, effort justification, and animals working for food they could get free. Limits: effort past a point devalues the goal; "unrewarded effort can be especially disappointing"; and people are "less likely to ascribe greater value to an effortful task if they receive sufficient incentives to complete it."
- Bears on: H4, H4a, H4b, H1.
- Direction: confirms the direction of H4b (effort adds value, so little effort adds little) and the two-sided form of H4 (too much effort turns value down). Refines H4a: a large outside incentive removes effort's power to add value. That is the overjustification pattern again, from a different literature.

**F6.2** Norton, M. I., Mochon, D., and Ariely, D. (2012). "The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love." *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 22(3): 453-460. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (four studies: IKEA boxes, origami, Lego).
- Says: People valued what they built themselves about as highly as experts' work. But "labor leads to love only when labor results in successful completion"; when they built and then destroyed it, or failed to finish, "the IKEA effect dissipated."
- Bears on: H3, H4b.
- Direction: confirms H3 (the payoff of finishing is needed) and the earned side of H4b (effort makes the result worth more, but only if the effort succeeds).

**F6.3** Aronson, E., and Mills, J. (1959). "The effect of severity of initiation on liking for a group." *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 59(2): 177-181. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0047195. Read: opening text as given in the OpenAlex record (results section not seen).
- Kind: MEASURED (lab experiment).
- Says: The paper tests the "common observation" that people "who go through a great deal of trouble or pain to attain something tend to value it more highly." The result, as Kamau's replication (F6.4) and search summaries describe it, is that a severe initiation raised how attractive people found the group. I did not see the results section myself.
- Bears on: H4b.
- Direction: confirms (effort raises value), with the caveat in F6.4.

**F6.4** Kamau, C. (2013; online 2012). "What does being initiated severely into a group do? The role of rewards." *International Journal of Psychology* 48(3): 399-406. https://doi.org/10.1080/00207594.2012.663957. Read: abstract (https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/what-does-being-initiated-severely-into-a-group-do-the-role-of-re/).
- Kind: MEASURED (replication, N = 70).
- Says: "A severe initiation did not lead to more group identity than a mild initiation; therefore, Aronson and Mills' findings were not replicated." A reward raised identity, and "a mild initiation followed by a reward led to more group identity than a severe initiation followed by a reward."
- Bears on: H4b.
- Direction: runs against the strong form of effort justification. One small replication, so it weakens rather than overturns the classic result.

**F6.5** Kool, W., McGuire, J. T., Rosen, Z. B., and Botvinick, M. M. (2010). "Decision making and the avoidance of cognitive demand." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 139(4): 665-682. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020198. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (six experiments).
- Says: Given a free choice, people showed "a bias in favor of the less demanding course of action," even when unaware of the difference. The bias responded to incentives and varied with people's executive-control ability.
- Bears on: H1, H4a, H6.
- Direction: refines. The default is to avoid mental effort. For effort to be chosen, something has to pay for it. That fits H4a's cost side, and the individual differences fit H6.

**F6.6** Kurzban, R., Duckworth, A., Kable, J. W., and Myers, J. (2013). "An opportunity cost model of subjective effort and task performance." *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 36(6): 661-679. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X12003196. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: THEORY.
- Says: The felt sense of effort is "the felt output" of a cost-benefit computation. The cost is the next-best use of the same mental machinery.
- Bears on: H4a, the work/play line.
- Direction: refines H4a. An activity feels like a grind when its value falls below what else the person could be doing. So "too small a reward" is judged against alternatives, not only against the challenge.

**F6.7** Westbrook, A., Kester, D., and Braver, T. S. (2013). "What is the subjective cost of cognitive effort? Load, trait, and aging effects revealed by economic preference." *PLoS ONE* 8(7): e68210. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0068210. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (choice experiment).
- Says: People discount money by the mental effort needed to earn it. The discount depends on task load and reward amount, and differs by age and by trait enjoyment of thinking.
- Bears on: H4a, H6.
- Direction: confirms an effort-for-reward exchange rate, and shows the rate differs from person to person (H6).

### Topic 7. Fairness of reward

**F7.1** Adams's equity theory: Adams, J. S. (1963). "Towards an understanding of inequity." *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 67(5): 422-436. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040968. Read: partial abstract (OpenAlex record). Adams, J. S. (1965). "Inequity in social exchange." *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 2: 267-299. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(08)60108-2. Not opened (no free copy reached). The theory's core is stated in Huseman, R. C., Hatfield, J. D., and Miles, E. W. (1987). "A new perspective on equity theory: The equity sensitivity construct." *Academy of Management Review* 12(2): 222-234. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1987.4307799. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: THEORY.
- Says: Adams framed inequity as "a special case of Festinger's cognitive dissonance." In Huseman et al.'s summary, equity theory holds that people who see themselves "as either underrewarded or overrewarded will experience distress," and act to restore balance. Adams's further claims, that under-reward brings anger and over-reward brings guilt, and that the threshold for noticing over-reward is higher, were seen only in search summaries. They are unverified here.
- Bears on: H4, H4a, H4b.
- Direction: confirms that a two-sided proportion rule has a long pedigree in psychology. Note the difference: equity theory compares my outcome-to-input ratio with *another person's*. H4 compares my reward with *my own* challenge.

**F7.2** Austin, W., and Walster, E. (1974). "Reactions to confirmations and disconfirmations of expectancies of equity and inequity." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 30(2): 208-216. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0036622. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (lab experiment with cognitive, emotional and physiological measures).
- Says: People "were more content (and less distressed) when they were equitably rewarded than when they were either underrewarded or overrewarded." But "overrewarded subjects were more content than were underrewarded subjects." Being told to expect inequity beforehand reduced the distress.
- Bears on: H4, H4a, H4b.
- Direction: confirms H4's two-sided shape, and refines it in two ways. The sides are unequal: too little hurts more than too much. And expectation softens both.

**F7.3** Loewenstein, G. F., Thompson, L., and Bazerman, M. H. (1989). "Social utility and decision making in interpersonal contexts." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 57(3): 426-441. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.57.3.426. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (three studies of judged outcomes in disputes).
- Says: Satisfaction fell steeply with "disadvantageous inequality" and only weakly with "advantageous inequality." The relationship and the type of dispute strongly shaped feelings about getting more than the other side, but not about getting less.
- Bears on: H4a, H4b, H6.
- Direction: confirms the asymmetry in F7.2. Refines H4b: whether getting "too much" feels wrong depends heavily on context.

**F7.4** Huseman, Hatfield and Miles (1987), as in F7.1.
- Kind: THEORY.
- Says: "Reactions to equity/inequity are a function of an individual's preferences for different outcome/input ratios" (the equity-sensitivity construct).
- Bears on: H6, H4.
- Direction: refines. The proportion that feels right differs by person. This joins H4 to H6: each person may carry a different "fair exchange rate" between effort and reward.

### Topic 8. Video-game difficulty studies

**F8.1** Klimmt, C., Blake, C., Hefner, D., Vorderer, P., and Roth, C. (2009). "Player performance, satisfaction, and video game enjoyment." In *Entertainment Computing - ICEC 2009*, LNCS 5709: 1-12. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-04052-8_1. Read: full text (https://dl.ifip.org/db/conf/iwec/icec2009/KlimmtBHVR09.pdf).
- Kind: MEASURED (experiment, N = 74; first-person shooter at easy, moderate or very hard settings).
- Says: Enjoyment was highest in the easy version (means 4.29, 3.84 and 3.53 on a 5-point scale; eta squared = .17). Satisfaction with one's own performance followed the same order. Players rated the easy game as easy, yet "still they have fun with being successful, although they 'objectively' did nothing to be really proud of." The authors propose that this holds for players new to a game and may change with experience.
- Bears on: H4b, H1, H6.
- Direction: contradicts H4b for new players. Easy, unearned success was the most enjoyed condition. The authors' own expertise hypothesis is untested here.

**F8.2** Lomas, D., Patel, K., Forlizzi, J. L., and Koedinger, K. R. (2013). "Optimizing challenge in an educational game using large-scale design experiments." *CHI 2013*: 89-98. https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2470668. Read: full text (PDF at https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/463-663/project-part-one/Optimizing%20Challenge.pdf).
- Kind: MEASURED (two randomized online experiments, about 10,000 and 69,642 play sessions of a math game).
- Says: "The easier the game, the longer people played"; the relation was linear, with no inverted U. The easiest designs gave the slowest learning. The authors grant that they may not have tested easy enough settings, and that "engagement may or may not correspond to enjoyment." They offer hypotheses for the gap with the chess study: random assignment versus choice, no advance notice of difficulty ("feedforward"), no status to gain, no win-or-lose state, and younger, less expert players.
- Bears on: H1, H4, H4b, H6.
- Direction: contradicts the two-sided proportion for engagement in this setting. Refines: the conditions the authors list (choice, notice, stakes, expertise) are candidate moderators.

**F8.3** Lomas, D., Koedinger, K., Patel, N., Shodhan, S., Poonwala, N., and Forlizzi, J. (2017). "Is difficulty overrated? The effects of choice, novelty and suspense on intrinsic motivation in educational games." *CHI 2017*: 1028-1039. https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025638. Read: first part of the abstract (OpenAlex record); the rest of the abstract only as a search summary; and a Carnegie Mellon HCII news item, May 11, 2017 (https://hcii.cmu.edu/news/chi-preview-researchers-hcii-believe-difficulty-can-be-overrated-games).
- Kind: MEASURED (three online experiments, over 20,000 play sessions).
- Says: Per the search summary of the abstract: when difficulty was assigned, "the easiest games were most motivating," but "moderately difficult levels were most motivating when self-selected." The news item says narrow victories motivated players most, and that a moderate rate of novelty beat constant or minimal change. The news item also says choice "did not increase motivation for moderately difficult levels," which seems to clash with the abstract summary. I could not open the paper to settle it.
- Bears on: H4, H6, the work/play line (choice), H5 (novelty).
- Direction: refines. Choice, suspense and novelty may carry what designers credit to difficulty. Partly unverified.

**F8.4** Schmierbach, M., Chung, M. Y., Wu, M., and Kim, K. (2014). "No one likes to lose: The effect of game difficulty on competency, flow, and enjoyment." *Journal of Media Psychology* 26(3): 105-110. https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-1105/a000120. Read: abstract (https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/no-one-likes-to-lose-the-effect-of-game-difficulty-on-competency-).
- Kind: MEASURED (experiment, N = 121, casual tower-defense game).
- Says: "Competency is enhanced when players are assigned an easier game mode," and competence feeds enjoyment by helping players reach a challenge-skill balance.
- Bears on: H4b, H3.
- Direction: contradicts H4b in casual play: easier play raised the felt payoff (competence) and, through it, enjoyment. Confirms H3 in the form of felt competence.

**F8.5** Cutting, J., Deterding, S., Demediuk, S., and Sephton, N. (2023). "Difficulty-skill balance does not affect engagement and enjoyment: A pre-registered study using artificial intelligence-controlled difficulty." *Royal Society Open Science* 10(2): 220274. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.220274. Read: full text through a fetch tool's summary (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9890114/).
- Kind: MEASURED (pre-registered experiment, n = 311; tactical game against an AI opponent).
- Says: The AI produced easy (players won 100 percent), balanced, and hard (players lost 94.1 percent) games "without participants noticing the manipulation." Yet "different ratios had no significant impact on enjoyment or engagement" (enjoyment F(2, 308) = 1.29, p = 0.277), and none on voluntary replay or felt agency.
- Bears on: H1, H3, H4, H4b.
- Direction: contradicts a strong form of H4 and H3 for this game. Neither the challenge level nor winning versus losing moved enjoyment. Refines: players did not notice the difference. That suggests what matters is *perceived* challenge and earnedness, not the objective ratio.

**F8.6** Lemmens, J. S., and von Münchhausen, C. F. (2022). "Let the beat flow: How game difficulty in virtual reality affects flow." *Acta Psychologica* 232: 103812. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103812. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (experiment, n = 201, Beat Saber set too easy, matched, or too hard for each player).
- Says: Matched difficulty gave the most flow. "Too hard" lowered flow through frustration, but "too easy did not lead to the expected decrease in flow due to boredom." Flow went with better performance, higher arousal and more enjoyment.
- Bears on: H4, H4b.
- Direction: partly confirms H4 (match is best; too hard fails). Partly contradicts H4b (too easy did not clearly fail).

**F8.7** Dynamic difficulty adjustment (DDA). Robb, N., and Zhang, B. (2020). "Performance-based dynamic difficulty adjustment and player experience in a 2D video game: A controlled experiment." PsyArXiv preprint. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/x9cwt. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record). Constant, T., and Levieux, G. (2019). "Dynamic difficulty adjustment impact on players' confidence." *CHI 2019*: 1-12. https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300693. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (Robb and Zhang: 221 participants, DDA versus constant versus steadily rising difficulty; Constant and Levieux: three games, DDA versus random difficulty, with an in-game betting measure).
- Says: With DDA, players performed better, but "there were no significant differences in terms of enjoyment or experience of flow" (Robb and Zhang). DDA "can lead players to high level of overconfidence" (Constant and Levieux).
- Bears on: H4, H4b.
- Direction: refines. Automatically matching challenge to skill did not raise enjoyment in the one controlled test seen. Hidden help made players overrate their own skill, so success that was partly handed to them felt earned. H4b's "cheap" feeling seems to need the player to notice. (A study of hidden versus disclosed DDA turned up only as a master's thesis seen in a search summary; see Open threads.)

**F8.8** Ryan, R. M., Rigby, C. S., and Przybylski, A. (2006). "The motivational pull of video games: A self-determination theory approach." *Motivation and Emotion* 30(4): 347-363. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8. Read: full text (https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf).
- Kind: MEASURED (four studies) and THEORY (self-determination theory).
- Says: Felt autonomy, competence and relatedness each predicted enjoyment and future play. Competence comes from "ongoing optimal challenges and opportunities for positive feedback." Autonomy is "a sense of volition or willingness," helped by choice and by rewards "structured so as to provide feedback rather than to control the player's behavior." When people feel controlled, "subsequent motivation wanes."
- Bears on: H3, H4, H6, the work/play line.
- Direction: confirms H3 in the form of competence feedback. Refines H4: a reward works when it informs the player how well they did, and fails when it is used to steer them. Adds autonomy as a separate condition.

**F8.9** Johnson, D., Klarkowski, M., Vella, K., Phillips, C., McEwan, M., and Watling, C. (2018). "Greater rewards in videogames lead to more presence, enjoyment and effort." *Computers in Human Behavior* 87: 66-74. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2018.05.025. Read: abstract (https://eprints.qut.edu.au/118504/); the title states the result; the reward design was not visible.
- Kind: MEASURED (repeated-measures experiment, N = 59, high, medium and low in-game reward).
- Says: Effort, enjoyment and presence varied across reward levels, and the title reports more of each with greater rewards.
- Bears on: H4b.
- Direction: contradicts a simple H4b within the range tested: more in-game reward meant more enjoyment, not a "cheap" feeling. How rewards related to the challenge is not known from the abstract.

**F8.10** Frommel, J., and Mandryk, R. L. (2022). "Daily quests or daily pests? The benefits and pitfalls of engagement rewards in games." *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* 6 (CHI PLAY): 1-23. https://doi.org/10.1145/3549489. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (mixed-methods survey, 178 players).
- Says: Players saw rewards such as daily logins and seasonal items as motivating, as negative ("fear of missing out"), "or even as an obligation or chore."
- Bears on: the work/play line, H4a.
- Direction: refines. Rewards that require regular attendance can turn play into a chore. The work feeling comes from obligation, not from a reward being too small.

**F8.11** Tyni, J., Bednarik, R., Kahila, J., and Tedre, M. (2024). "A bird matching game: Difficulty, rewards and intrinsic motivation." *International Journal of Serious Games* 11: 57-77. https://doi.org/10.17083/ijsg.v11i3.805. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (66 game sessions; small).
- Says: Points and badges made no significant difference to progress or intrinsic motivation. "Voluntarily increasing the difficulty is shown to increase" intrinsic-motivation scores.
- Bears on: H4, the work/play line.
- Direction: refines. Self-chosen extra challenge raised motivation; handed-out points did not. Small study.

**F8.12** Alexander, J. T., Sear, J., and Oikonomou, A. (2013). "An investigation of the effects of game difficulty on player enjoyment." *Entertainment Computing* 4(1): 53-62. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.entcom.2012.09.001. Read: search summary only (publisher page refused access). Unverified.
- Kind: MEASURED (as described).
- Says (per search summary): scaling difficulty seemed to raise enjoyment for experienced players, while casual players favored lower difficulty regardless of performance.
- Bears on: H6, H4b.
- Direction: refines (expertise as a moderator). Unverified; it agrees with Klimmt's hypothesis (F8.1).

### Topic 9. What makes an activity feel like work rather than play

**F9.1** Twain, M. (1876). *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer*, chapter 2. Project Gutenberg eBook #74. https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/74/pg74.txt. Read: full text of the chapter.
- Kind: THEORY (a classic argument, made in fiction; no data).
- Says: Tom has found "a great law of human action": "in order to make a man or a boy covet a thing, it is only necessary to make the thing difficult to attain." Then: "Work consists of whatever a body is _obliged_ to do, and that Play consists of whatever a body is not obliged to do." Rich men drive coaches for the fun of it, but "if they were offered wages for the service, that would turn it into work and then they would resign."
- Bears on: the work/play line, H4a, H4b.
- Direction: runs against H4a's mechanism. In Twain's line, work is obligation, and *adding* pay turns play into work. Agrees with the effort-raises-value findings (Topic 6). The pay line anticipates the overjustification studies by about a century.

**F9.2** Glynn, M. A. (1994). "Effects of work task cues and play task cues on information processing, judgment, and motivation." *Journal of Applied Psychology* 79(1): 34-45. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.79.1.34. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (lab experiment, 82 MBA students).
- Says: The same puzzle was labeled either work or play. Under the work label, people attended to the *quantity* of their output and gave "streamlined, goal-directed responses." Under the play label, they attended to *quality*, gave richer responses, and "were more intrinsically motivated." The author calls this "a means orientation in play and an ends orientation in work."
- Bears on: the work/play line, H4a.
- Direction: contradicts H4a as the full account. With the challenge and the reward held the same, the label alone moved the task toward work or play.

**F9.3** Sansone, C., Weir, C., Harpster, L., and Morgan, C. (1992). "Once a boring task always a boring task? Interest as a self-regulatory mechanism." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 63(3): 379-390. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.3.379. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record).
- Kind: MEASURED (three studies).
- Says: When people had to keep doing a boring task and had a reason to, they used "interest-enhancing strategies" that changed "the activity into something more positive to perform." This went with "a change in activity definition."
- Bears on: the work/play line, H6.
- Direction: refines. People can move a task from work toward play from the inside, by redefining it, without any change in the outside reward.

**F9.4** Ridge, M. (2021). "Games and the good life." *Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy* 19(1): 1-26. https://doi.org/10.26556/jesp.v19i1.1618. Read: abstract (OpenAlex record and the article page through a fetch tool).
- Kind: THEORY (philosophy).
- Says: In Thomas Hurka's view, "the good of achievement is more basic," and playing a game is good because it is an achievement. John Tasioulas argues the reverse: the "framing value" of games "is play itself." Ridge proposes a "variable priority" view: sometimes play grounds the value of achievement, sometimes the reverse.
- Bears on: H1, H3.
- Direction: neutral. Philosophers are split on whether challenge-and-achievement or play itself is the basic good. Hurka's side matches H1; Tasioulas's side questions it. (Suits's definition and lusory attitude are Job 1's ground; I saw them here only in search summaries.)

**Cross-references for Topic 9.** Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre (F1.1): most flow happened at work, yet people wanted to be at leisure. Lepper and Greene (F5.3): expected rewards and being watched each turned play toward work. Deci (F5.1): money lowered intrinsic motivation; feedback raised it. Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (F8.8): feeling controlled drains motivation. Przybylski and colleagues (F4.4): "having to" play went with less enjoyment. Kurzban and colleagues (F6.6): effort feels costly when the task is worth less than the alternatives. Frommel and Mandryk (F8.10): obligatory rewards feel like chores.

**Answer to the brief's question for Topic 9 (is it the size of the reward, or something else?).** Mostly something else. The strongest evidence points to obligation and felt control (Twain; Glynn; Lepper and Greene; Ryan and colleagues; Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre), and to an expected outside reward becoming the reason for the activity (Deci; Lepper). Size matters in narrower ways: a small payment can be worse than none (Gneezy and Rustichini), effort is traded against payoff at a personal rate (Westbrook and colleagues), and being under-rewarded is distressing (Austin and Walster). Those effects describe an activity that is "not worth it," which is not quite the same thing as "work."

## 4. Conflicts between sources

**C1. Hard chess versus easy video games.** Abuhamdeh and Csikszentmihalyi (F2.1) found chess most enjoyed against stronger opponents; Lomas and colleagues estimate the peak at about a 20 percent chance of winning. Klimmt and colleagues (F8.1), Schmierbach and colleagues (F8.4) and Lomas and colleagues (F8.2) found the easiest setting most enjoyed or most played. Cutting and colleagues (F8.5) found no effect at all. The sources themselves offer reasons the results may differ: the chess players chose their opponents and knew their ratings; they were expert; they had a rating to gain; chess has a clear win or loss; and the studies measured different things (enjoyment ratings versus time played). None of these has been tested head to head.

**C2. Two readings of the chess data.** The abstract (F2.1) says games against superior opponents were more enjoyable. Baumann and colleagues (F1.5) report that players most enjoyed games in which they held a slight edge during play. Both can be true together: a stronger opponent, and a close game in which one is slightly ahead. It still matters for H4, because the second reading puts the peak nearer an even contest.

**C3. Do performance-tied rewards undermine fun?** Deci, Koestner and Ryan (F5.4) found that performance-contingent rewards lowered free-choice motivation (d = -0.28). Cameron, Banko and Pierce (F5.5) found that rewards "linked to level of performance" raise intrinsic motivation or leave it unchanged. Murayama and colleagues (F5.7) found undermining with performance-based money. The two meta-analyses coded "performance-contingent" differently, and the dispute has run for decades. Cerasoli and colleagues (F5.8) add that tightly tied incentives crowd out intrinsic motivation's link to performance; that is a different outcome from interest.

**C4. How much does challenge-skill balance matter?** The meta-analysis of Fong and colleagues (F1.4) calls it a moderate and robust contributor. Engeser and Rheinberg (F1.3) found only partial support, shaped by stakes and motive. Baumann and colleagues (F1.5) found constant balance was not best. Lemmens and von Münchhausen (F8.6) found balance best for flow, with "too easy" doing little harm. Cutting and colleagues (F8.5), pre-registered, found no effect on enjoyment.

**C5. Effort justification.** Aronson and Mills (F6.3) is the classic support. Kamau (F6.4) failed to replicate it. Inzlicht and colleagues (F6.1) still treat effort-adds-value as well supported across several literatures.

**C6. Choice in Lomas and colleagues (2017).** The abstract, seen through a search summary, says moderate difficulty was most motivating when self-chosen. The CMU news item says choice did not raise motivation for moderate levels (F8.3). The paper itself was not reached.

**C7. More reward, more fun?** Johnson and colleagues (F8.9) found more in-game reward gave more enjoyment. Abuhamdeh and colleagues (F2.2) found that the most lopsided wins, which gave the most felt competence, were less enjoyed. These may concern different kinds of reward (loot handed out versus margin of victory), so they may not truly clash.

## 5. Verdict per claim

**H3, payoff (getting through delivers a positive benefit or reaction).**
- Strongest for: the IKEA effect needs successful completion (F6.2); the pleasure "click" of understanding comes after the struggle (F4.7); felt competence predicts enjoyment (F8.8, F8.4); striatal dopamine rises with game performance (F4.6); positive feedback raises intrinsic motivation (F5.4).
- Strongest against: close chess games were most enjoyed "regardless of ... who won" (F2.1, as reported in F8.2); suspense beat felt competence in players' choices (F2.2); winning every game and losing nearly every game gave the same enjoyment (F8.5).
- Net reading: **partly supported.** A signal of competence or progress is a reliable ingredient. But the payoff does not have to come at the end, and it does not have to be a win. Much of the pleasure sits inside the challenge, while the outcome is still in doubt.

**H4, proportion (the reward must be proportional to the challenge).**
- Strongest for: in Atkinson's model, the inner reward of success rises with difficulty (F2.3); effort adds value, and too much effort takes it away (F6.1); being fairly rewarded is more content-making than being under- or over-rewarded (F7.2); harmful rewards are the ones "loosely tied to level of performance" (F5.5); pleasure or attention peaks at a middle level for chess, music and infant attention (F2.1, F3.2, F3.3).
- Strongest against: easy settings were the most enjoyed or most played in several game studies (F8.1, F8.4, F8.2); a pre-registered study found no effect of the challenge ratio (F8.5); even performance-tied rewards can undermine later free play (F5.4, F5.7), so a proportional outside reward is not automatically safe.
- Net reading: **partly supported.** Proportion holds well for inner rewards (pride, competence, the value of a thing made), where it is close to built in. For outside rewards it holds as a fairness rule: rewards should track performance and inform rather than control. The two sides are unequal, and expertise and perception shift where the balance lies.

**H4a (too small a reward makes it work).**
- Strongest for: once pay is offered, a small amount did worse than none (F5.9); under-reward is the more distressing side of inequity (F7.2, F7.3); people price effort against payoff and against their other options (F6.5, F6.6, F6.7).
- Strongest against: what turns play into work in the evidence is obligation, being watched, expecting a reward for the activity, and even a label (F9.1, F5.2, F5.3, F9.2, F8.10). The activity with the most challenge, paid work, held the most flow and the least wish to be there (F1.1). Paying people tends to *make* play into work (F5.1, F5.2, F9.1).
- Net reading: **contradicted as a definition of work; partly supported as a cause of "not worth it."** A too-small payoff makes an activity feel like a poor trade. What makes it feel like work is mainly the loss of choice and an outside reason for doing it.

**H4b (too large a reward feels cheap and unearned).**
- Strongest for: lopsided wins gave the most felt competence and the least enjoyment (F2.2); there is no "thrill" from success at an easy task in Atkinson's model (F2.3); effort and completion add value (F6.1, F6.2); rewards loosely tied to performance undermine interest (F5.5).
- Strongest against: new players most enjoyed an easy game while knowing it was easy (F8.1); easier modes raised competence and enjoyment in casual play (F8.4); "too easy" did not cut flow (F8.6); more in-game reward meant more enjoyment (F8.9); hidden difficulty help made players overconfident, so the help felt earned (F8.7); being over-rewarded is less distressing than being under-rewarded (F7.2); a classic effort-justification result failed to replicate (F6.4).
- Net reading: **partly supported.** The "cheap" feeling shows up for invested or expert players, in a clear contest, when they can see that the result came too easily. New and casual players often enjoy easy success. The feeling seems to need the person to notice the mismatch.

**The line between work and play.**
- Net reading for the author's account (work is under-rewarded challenge): **contradicted as the main line.** The best-supported line is voluntariness and felt control, plus whether an outside reward has become the reason for doing it (Topic 9).

**Side readings for claims covered mainly by Jobs 1 and 3.** H1: challenge predicts enjoyment for chosen, goal-directed activities (F2.1, F3.5), but not across daily activities in general (F1.5), and the effort phase itself may not be enjoyed (F4.7). H5: success raises the level a person aims for (F2.3); learning-progress models switch tasks at a plateau (F3.4); rising difficulty with short rests beats a flat level (F1.5). H6: achievement motive, fear of failure, sensation seeking, equity sensitivity, effort cost and expertise all shift the best point (F1.3, F1.5, F2.3, F6.7, F7.4, F8.12).

## 6. Refinements the evidence suggests
These are options for the author, not decisions.

1. **Split the reward into inner and outer.** Inner rewards (pride, competence, suspense resolved, a skill learned, a thing made) grow with the challenge almost by nature (F2.3, F6.1, F6.2). Outer rewards (loot, money, points, praise) need a rule of their own: tie them to performance and use them to inform, not to steer (F5.5, F8.8). The evidence on outer rewards turning play into work is strong (Topic 5).
2. **Replace "proportional" with "felt as earned."** The cheap feeling seems to need the person to notice the mismatch. Hidden help felt earned (F8.7). A hidden difficulty change went unnoticed and had no effect (F8.5). Advance notice of difficulty may change how success is valued (F8.2).
3. **Make the two sides unequal.** Under-reward hurts more than over-reward (F7.2, F7.3). "Too hard" fails more reliably than "too easy" (F8.6, F1.3, F8.1).
4. **Add the person's stage and stakes.** Novices enjoy easy success; experts and people with more at stake want harder challenges or more margin (F8.1, F8.12, F1.3, F8.2). This joins H4 to H6.
5. **Redefine H4a's "work."** Option: work is activity done because one is obliged or because an outside reward is the reason. A too-small payoff gives a separate failure, "not worth it." The two can be listed apart.
6. **Add uncertainty to the unit.** Enjoyment peaks when the outcome is in real doubt (F2.2, F2.1, F2.3, F4.2). The unit might read: a challenge whose outcome is uncertain, resolved in a way the person can credit to themselves.
7. **Add rhythm to the chain (H5).** Rising demand with short rests (F1.5), a step up after each success (F2.3), and moving on at a plateau (F3.4, F3.5).
8. **Add choice as a condition.** Self-chosen difficulty raised motivation (F8.11; F8.3, partly unverified), and felt autonomy predicts enjoyment (F8.8).
9. **Test fun by liking, not by persistence.** Wanting and liking are separate (F4.3); compulsive play is less enjoyed (F4.4); random paid rewards link to problem gambling (F4.5); engagement may not equal enjoyment (F8.2).
