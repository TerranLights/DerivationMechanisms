## 1. Verdict per claim

| Claim | Net reading | Strongest evidence for | Strongest evidence against or limiting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H1.** The smallest unit of fun is a challenge that takes effort. | **Partly supported.** A strong account of one family of fun (contest, skill and puzzle fun). Not a unit of all fun. | Groos 1901: pleasure in overcoming difficulties is "an essential feature of all play" (J1 F1.8). Abuhamdeh and Csikszentmihalyi 2012: in chess, perceived challenge "strongly predicted enjoyment" (J2 F2.1). Baranes, Oudeyer and Gottlieb 2014: with no reward, people moved on to harder games (J1 F7.3, J2 F3.5). | Caillois's games of chance and vertigo (J1 F3.2, F3.3). McManus and Furnham 2010: only 22.6% of people describing a fun situation called it "challenged" (J3 8.1). Baumann and colleagues 2016: many daily-life studies find no link, or a negative one, between enjoyment and challenge (J2 F1.5). Lazzaro 2004: three of four kinds of game fun are not about challenge (J1 F5.2). |
| **H2.** It is usually non-damaging and generally non-lethal. | **Partly supported as worded; strongly supported once restated as *felt* safety.** Most fun is low in real danger. What every source agrees on is a frame: the person feels able to come through, or chose the stakes. | Siviy 2010: cat odor stopped rat play for days (J3 1.2). Rozin and colleagues 2013: people enjoy fear and pain when they know the alarm is false (J3 3.1). Bateson, Huizinga, Salen and Zimmerman: play sits inside a frame that keeps harm out (J1 F2.4-F2.6). | Firth and colleagues 2008: 1.3% of mountaineers above Everest base camp died, 1921-2006 (J3 4.1). Lyng 1990: for edgeworkers, handling real danger is the point (J3 4.2). Huizinga's lethal early tournaments (J1 F2.4). |
| **H3.** Overcoming it delivers a positive benefit or reaction. | **Supported, with two corrections.** The payoff is inner (triumph, mastery, insight, laughter). Much of it arrives during the effort, not only after. | Lazzaro's *fiero*, the triumph over adversity (J1 F5.2). Norton and colleagues 2012: effort raised the value of what people built only when they finished it (J2 F6.2). Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski 2006: felt competence predicted enjoyment (J2 F8.8). | Abuhamdeh and colleagues 2015: close games beat blowouts, and suspense carried the effect (J2 F2.2). Cutting and colleagues 2023, pre-registered: winning every game or losing nearly every game made no difference to enjoyment (J2 F8.5). Suits and Nguyen: the striving itself is the end (J1 F4.1, F4.5). |
| **H4.** The reward must be proportional to the challenge. | **Partly supported.** It holds almost by nature for inner rewards: pride grows with difficulty. For outside rewards it holds as a fairness rule: tie the reward to performance and use it to inform, not to steer. The two sides are unequal. | Atkinson 1957: the pride of success rises as the chance of success falls (J2 F2.3). Austin and Walster 1974: people were most content when fairly rewarded (J2 F7.2). Inzlicht and colleagues 2018: effort adds value, and too much effort takes it away (J2 F6.1). | Klimmt and colleagues 2009: the easiest setting was the most enjoyed (J2 F8.1). Lomas and colleagues 2013: the easier the game, the longer people played (J2 F8.2). Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999: even performance-tied rewards lowered later free-choice motivation (J2 F5.4). |
| **H4a.** Too small a reward makes it work. | **Contradicted as the definition of work; partly supported as the cause of "not worth it."** What turns play into work is mainly lost choice and an outside reason for doing it. | Gneezy and Rustichini 2000: small pay did worse than no pay (J2 F5.9). Austin and Walster 1974: under-reward was the most distressing case (J2 F7.2). Kurzban and colleagues 2013: effort feels costly when a task is worth less than the alternatives (J2 F6.6). | Twain 1876: work is what a body is "obliged" to do, and pay turns play into work (J2 F9.1). Lepper and Greene 1975: an expected reward and being watched each lowered later interest (J2 F5.3). Glynn 1994: the same puzzle, labeled "work," was done differently (J2 F9.2). Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre 1989: most flow happened at work, yet people wanted to be at leisure (J2 F1.1). |
| **H4b.** Too large a reward feels cheap and unearned. | **Partly supported.** The cheap feeling shows up for invested or expert players, in a clear contest, when they can see that the win came too easily. New and casual players often enjoy easy success. | Abuhamdeh and colleagues 2015: lopsided wins gave the most felt competence and the least enjoyment (J2 F2.2). Atkinson 1957: no thrill from success at an easy task (J2 F2.3). Juul 2013: an unearned win that felt insulting (J1 F5.6). | Klimmt and colleagues 2009: new players enjoyed easy success while knowing it was easy (J2 F8.1). Schmierbach and colleagues 2014: easier modes raised competence and enjoyment (J2 F8.4). Constant and Levieux 2019: hidden difficulty help made players overconfident, so the help felt earned (J2 F8.7). |

## 2. Where the three jobs agree
These points came up independently in two or three jobs, so they are the best-supported changes to the theory.

1. **Felt quantities beat objective ones.**
   - Safety that is felt, not actual (J3; J1's frame).
   - A reward that is felt as earned, not one that is objectively proportional (J2: hidden help felt earned; an unnoticed difficulty change had no effect).
   - Challenge as perceived (J2 F8.5).
2. **Uncertainty of outcome is central, and the theory does not name it.**
   - Caillois and Huizinga require an open outcome (J1).
   - Enjoyment peaks when the result is in real doubt (J2 F2.2), and so does motivation (Atkinson's 50 percent point, J2 F2.3).
   - A dopamine signal rises with uncertainty during the wait (J2 F4.2).
3. **Free choice is what separates play from work.**
   - Suits, Huizinga and Caillois each call play voluntary (J1).
   - Obligation, being watched and expected pay push play toward work (J2 Topic 9).
   - Dangerous fun is chosen danger (J3).
4. **The payoff is inner and runs through the effort.**
   - Suits, Nguyen and Cook (J1).
   - Suspense and close games (J2).
   - Material reward is optional at best, and often harmful when it becomes the reason for doing the activity (J2 Topic 5).
5. **Challenge fun is one kind of fun among several.**
   - Chance, vertigo, curiosity, company, rest and humor (J1).
   - Lying on the beach (J2 F1.5).
   - Contentment and sociability fun (J3 8.1).
6. **The best point moves.** Personality, current state, stakes, expertise and age all shift where the right challenge sits (J2, J3).

**The main disagreement left open:**
- Chess players most enjoyed hard opponents and close games (J2 F2.1).
- Several video-game studies found the easiest setting most enjoyed or most played (J2 F8.1, F8.2, F8.4).
- The sources suggest why: choice of opponent, expertise, a rating at stake, a clear win or loss, and knowing the difficulty in advance. None of these has been tested head to head (J2 C1).

## 3. Refinements, as options for the author
None of these is a decision. Several can be combined.

**The unit (H1)**
1. **Scope it:** keep H1 as the unit of *challenge fun*, and name the other families beside it, each perhaps with its own unit:
   - chance;
   - sensation and vertigo;
   - curiosity and discovery;
   - company;
   - rest;
   - humor and delight.
   (J1 refinement 1; J3 refinement 9.)
2. **Widen "challenge" to "a tension or uncertainty that resolves."** Effort becomes one way to resolve it, and chance another (J1 refinement 2; J2 refinement 6).
3. **Widen it instead to "a gap in understanding that closes."** This fits puzzles, discovery and many jokes, but chance, vertigo and comfort poorly (J1 refinement 3).
4. **Add uncertainty of outcome as a condition** (J1 refinement 8; J2 refinement 6).
5. **Add free choice as a condition** (J1 refinement 7; J2 refinement 8).

**Safety (H2)**

6. **Replace "non-damaging" with felt safety.** The person feels able to come through, through skill, preparation, gear, trust in a partner, or knowing it is fiction (J3 option A; J1 refinement 6).
7. **Treat threat as an ingredient with a personal sweet spot,** higher for sensation seekers (J3 option B).
8. **Let real stakes raise the bar:** the more real the consequences, the more a person's skill must exceed the challenge to stay enjoyable (J3 option C).
9. **Keep "usually non-damaging" as the design default,** with edgework (handling real danger as the point) as a named exception (J3 option D).
10. **In shared play, the frame is kept by signals and fairness:** play bows, self-handicapping, taking turns (J3 option E).

**Payoff and proportion (H3, H4)**

11. **The payoff runs during the effort,** with a peak at the resolution (J1 refinement 4).
12. **Split the reward into inner and outer.** Inner rewards grow with challenge by nature. Outer rewards (loot, points, money, praise) follow their own rule: tie them to performance and use them to inform (J1 refinement 5; J2 refinement 1).
13. **Replace "proportional" with "felt as earned"** (J2 refinement 2).
14. **Make the sides unequal:** too little hurts more than too much, and too hard fails more reliably than too easy (J2 refinement 3).
15. **Add the person's stage and stakes:** novices enjoy easy success; experts and people with more at stake want more (J2 refinement 4).
16. **Redefine "work"** as activity done from obligation or for an outside reward. Keep "not worth it" as a separate failure: a payoff too small for the effort (J2 refinement 5).

**Chains (H5)**

17. **Add variation, nesting at several time scales, and a steady supply of new material** (J1 refinement 9).
18. **Add rhythm:** rising demand with short rests, a step up after each success, moving on when progress stalls (J2 refinement 7).
19. **Mix repeatable loops with one-time arcs,** such as story beats (J1 refinement 9).

**Fit (H6)**

20. **Add the person's current state** (stress, fatigue, stakes, mood) to stable personality (J3 H6 option A).
21. **Think in dimensions people mix, not types** (J3 H6 option B).
22. **Add what the person wants out of it:** thrill, mastery, calm, growth or company (J3 H6 option C).
23. **Each person may carry their own "fair exchange rate"** between effort and reward (J2 F6.7, F7.4).

**Testing fun**

24. **Judge fun by liking, not by persistence.** Wanting and liking run on separate systems. Compulsive play is less enjoyed, and random paid rewards are linked to problem gambling. Time played is a weak test of fun (J2 refinement 9).

## 4. Questions only the author can settle
1. **What is the unit for?** Is it meant as a definition of what fun *is*, or as a design tool that reliably produces one kind of fun? The evidence supports the second far better than the first. Huizinga and Sutton-Smith hold that fun resists breaking into parts at all (J1 Conflict 2, Open threads).
2. **Which word?** One design-research tradition separates "fun" (distraction) from "pleasure" (absorption, progression, commitment). The author's theory reads closer to the second sense. This comes second-hand: Blythe and Hassenzahl 2004, read through Kono and Araake 2022 (J3 8.2).
3. **The rule on South Asian sources, by author name:** see `held_for_review_ten_et_al_2021.md`. Job 2 also set aside Ariely and colleagues 2009, mainly as off topic and partly because some of its experiments were run in India (J2 Rejected sources).

## 6. A note on the standing no-level-scaling law
These are observations, not proposals.
- **Automatic help did nothing for enjoyment in the tests found.** Difficulty that adjusted itself to the player did not raise enjoyment (Robb and Zhang 2020). Hidden help inflated players' confidence (Constant and Levieux 2019) (J2 F8.7).
- **People given free choice moved up to harder tasks** (Atkinson 1957; Baranes and colleagues 2014).
- **Choosing a harder setting oneself raised motivation** (Tyni and colleagues 2024, a small study).
- **Experienced players notice and dislike unearned wins** (J2 F2.2, F8.12).

None of this conflicts with a world of fixed difficulty that the player grows into and chooses where to go.
